Online fitness coaching for people in Connecticut

Connecticut is a state that commutes toward two cities and still lifts more than most of the states on these pages, with one of the higher household broadband shares and a median age a little above the middle. Coaching for Connecticut is about progression for people who already train, fitted around the train and the parkway. Diogo is based in South Florida and coaches online. How Connecticut trains, per the CDC's state survey

In 2023, 43.2% of adults in Connecticut reported muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week (BRFSS, 95% CI 41.6 to 44.8). In 2025, 19.5% reported no leisure-time physical activity at all (BRFSS, 95% CI 18.3 to 20.7).

Daily life in Connecticut, by the numbers

From the Census Bureau's American Community Survey, published value and margin of error together: about 16.0% of workers who live here work from home (90% margin of error plus or minus 0.2); commuters average 26.5 minutes to work (90% margin of error plus or minus 0.1); the median age is 41.1 years (90% margin of error plus or minus 0.2); 92.2% of households have a broadband subscription (90% margin of error plus or minus 0.2). State figures: your own city may differ.

How coaching works from a distance

Diogo is based in South Florida and coaches online. Your workouts live in the Trainerize app, you weigh in twice a week, you send progress photos every two weeks, and we talk on a weekly call scheduled around your time zone. Between calls, you can message me with questions.

My day is the train to the city and back. Where does training fit?

On the ends of it, and on the days you do not ride. Connecticut’s mean commute sits in the middle of these pages, and about a sixth of workers here work from home, so most weeks have two shapes: a train day that starts early and ends late, and a home day with a window in it. The plan writes two or three sessions into whichever windows the week actually has: a gym near the station before the ride home, the home day’s midday hour, the weekend. Each session is forty minutes around the big movements, so none of them needs a free evening. Coaching for a busy week is the shape of it.

A lot of people here already lift. What does a coach add?

A direction and a logbook that changes. The state survey puts Connecticut among the higher shares of adults lifting two or more days a week on these pages, and among the lower shares reporting no activity at all, which is a state of people who show up. The common problem after showing up is a program that stays the same for a year: same lifts, same weights, same session. Coaching adds enough weekly work for each muscle, a progression you can see from one week to the next, and someone who reads the log every week and changes what needs changing. Coaching to build muscle is the program behind that.

I am past forty and never trained properly. Is it too late to start?

No, and in Connecticut you are in good company: the median age here is a little above the middle of these pages, and plenty of people start strength work later than they were told they could. The plan for a later start progresses by adding a rep or a set before it adds weight, keeps most sets well inside what you can do, and leaves the recovery days where they belong. The weekly call runs on the connection most Connecticut households already have, so nothing about the setup is the hard part. If something hurts or a doctor is managing a condition, that comes first. How to start lifting weights is the first month on your own.
