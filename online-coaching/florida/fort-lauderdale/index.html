Online fitness coaching for people in Fort Lauderdale

Fort Lauderdale is the county seat, the city of canals and the beach road, and, by the numbers, a city where a lot of people work from home. That shapes the plan more than the weather does: when a fifth of workers are home, the training window is not only six in the morning or eight at night. Diogo is based in South Florida and coaches online.

Activity and sleep in Fort Lauderdale

Age-adjusted, an estimated 23.8% of adults in Fort Lauderdale report no leisure-time physical activity (a modeled estimate, 95% CI 20.6 to 27.0). An estimated 39.4% usually sleep less than 7 hours in a 24-hour period (same kind of estimate, 95% CI 34.8 to 44.3).

Compared with the cities around it, Fort Lauderdale is already moving: about one adult in four reports no activity in their free time, so most people here do something. So the coaching problem here is less often starting and more often keeping going, which is a structure problem, not a willpower one.

Daily life in Fort Lauderdale, by the numbers

From the Census Bureau's American Community Survey, published value and margin of error together: about 19.0% of workers who live here work from home (90% margin of error plus or minus 1.2); commuters average 26.1 minutes to work (90% margin of error plus or minus 0.6); the median age is 42.8 years (90% margin of error plus or minus 0.9); 91.8% of households have a broadband subscription (90% margin of error plus or minus 1.0).

Training through the Fort Lauderdale heat

At the FT LAUDERDALE station (USC00083163, 7.0 km from the Census internal point), the 1991–2020 normal July high is 90.2 F, with 74.8 days a year at or above 90 F on average, an average January low of 57.7 F, and 68.77 inches of rain a year.

The station is the same one Hollywood uses, and the weather is the same: a long hot stretch and a wet season. Hard sessions go early, late or indoors from June to September, walks move to the water’s edge in the evening, and every outdoor piece has an indoor version written next to it for the days it pours.

How coaching works from a distance

Diogo is based in South Florida and coaches online. Your workouts live in the Trainerize app, you weigh in twice a week, you send progress photos every two weeks, and we talk on a weekly call — Fort Lauderdale is Eastern time, so scheduling the call is the easy part. Between calls, you can message me with questions.

Half my team works from home. Does coaching fit that?

It fits it better than a gym appointment does. With about one worker in five at home, a lot of Fort Lauderdale weeks have a midday hour nobody is watching, and a forty-minute session at noon beats a skipped one at seven. The plan is written in sessions, not in time slots, so you drop them where your calendar has room. The weekly call needs nothing more than the connection you already work on.

Where do people actually run here without cooking?

Early, and near the water. With more than seventy days a year at or above ninety degrees, the summer answer is a start time, not a tougher attitude: the beach path before the sun is up, the bridges when you want a hill, the shade of the park loops after it drops. The rainfall figure above is why every running or walking day in the plan carries an indoor swap; if the forecast is bad when we talk on the weekly call, the swap is made before the day is lost, not after.

I train, but I keep drifting off after a few weeks. What changes with a coach?

Two things. Someone reads your numbers every week, which is the part an app cannot do, and the plan has a floor built in for the weeks that go wrong, which is the part a poster schedule never has. The short-sleep estimate above is worth a look too: in an active city, the stall is often recovery rather than effort, and the weekly call asks about sleep before it asks about sets. Why you keep starting over is the longer version.
