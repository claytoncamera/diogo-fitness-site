Running pace calculator: pace, time, distance and a race-time estimate

Enter two of pace, time and distance and get the third, with even splits for every kilometre or mile. Then see what your time over one distance suggests for another, and why that is an estimate.

Short answer: Pace is time divided by distance: a 25-minute 5K is 5:00 per kilometre, which is 8:03 per mile.1 To estimate a time over another distance, Riegel’s formula multiplies your time by the distance ratio raised to the power 1.06,4,5 so a 25:00 5K suggests about 52:07 for 10K. It’s an estimate, not a forecast.

How pace, time and distance fit together

Pace = time ÷ distance. A 25:00 5K is 1,500 seconds over 5 kilometres, which is 300 seconds a kilometre, or 5:00 per km. Rearranged, time = pace × distance, and distance = time ÷ pace. That’s all the calculator does for the top half of the page.

Kilometres and miles differ by a fixed factor. A mile is exactly 1.609344 km,1 so a pace per kilometre becomes a pace per mile when you multiply it by 1.609344. The marathon is 42.195 km,2 which makes the half marathon half of that, 21.0975 km.

What the split table is

The split table shows even splits: the same time for every kilometre or mile, with a shorter last row if the distance doesn’t divide evenly. It’s a practical way to check your watch against a target at each marker, not a prescription for how to pace a race. Plenty of runners pace by effort, and hills, wind and crowds change the split you can hold.

How the race-time estimate works

The estimate uses Riegel’s formula: T2 = T1 × (D2 ÷ D1)1.06, where T1 is a time you ran over distance D1 and T2 is the estimated time over D2. Riegel described a power law linking record times and distance in a 1981 paper on athletic records,3 and 1.06 is the exponent a later paper fixes for it, describing the value as derived from world-record performances.4 The exponent is slightly above 1 because runners slow as the distance grows, which means the estimate for twice the distance is a little more than twice the time.5

A worked example: a 25:00 5K is 1,500 seconds. The distance ratio to 10K is 2, and 2 to the power 1.06 is about 2.085, so the estimate is about 3,127 seconds, or 52:07.

What the estimate can’t tell you

It is an estimate from two numbers. Here’s where it stops being reliable, from the studies that have looked at it:

  • It's meant for endurance-length efforts. A 2021 review, citing Riegel's 1981 paper, puts endurance competitions at about 3 to 230 minutes.6 The calculator marks any time outside that range with †.
  • The exponent isn’t one fixed number. One study reports that Riegel cited 1.08 for elite runners and 1.05 to 1.06 for male recreational runners aged 40 to 70, and chose 1.07 as an average for its own work.5 A 2024 paper on world running records describes the speed-and-distance power law from Riegel’s paper as having an exponent that varies between 0.054 and 0.083, depending on age and gender.7 That exponent is written for speed; in the time form used here it corresponds to roughly 1.054 to 1.083 (arithmetic: 1 plus the speed exponent). The calculator uses 1.06 for everyone.
  • The marathon estimate was the roughest in one study. In a survey of 2,303 recreational runners, Riegel's formula was well-calibrated up to a half marathon. Its marathon predictions, using an exponent of 1.07, were 10 minutes or more too fast for about half of the runners in a 156-runner validation sample.5 Treat the marathon row as the roughest one.
  • It knows two numbers and nothing else. Your training for the longer distance, the course, the weather and how you pace the day aren’t inputs.

So use it to see where a result sits against another distance, not to set a goal you’ll be held to. Racing hard is a big effort for a body that hasn’t been running. I’m a fitness coach, not a doctor, so if you have a medical question, or you haven’t been active in a while, check with your doctor before you train for a race.

Common questions

How do I convert min/km to min/mile?

Multiply the pace per kilometre by 1.609344.1 Pick “kilometres”, enter a distance and a time, and the line under the result shows both. Going the other way, divide a pace per mile by 1.609344.

How accurate is the race predictor?

It’s an estimate. In the one survey of recreational runners cited here it was well-calibrated up to the half marathon; for the marathon, with an exponent of 1.07, it was 10 minutes or more too fast for about half of a 156-runner validation sample.5

Does it work for a walk?

The pace, time and distance part is plain arithmetic, so yes. The studies cited here looked at runners, so leave the race-time estimate for runs. For walking calories, there’s the walking calorie calculator.

Is anything saved or sent?

No. The arithmetic runs in your browser, and the page keeps nothing once you close it.
