Fit

Who this is for. And who it isn’t.

This is for you if…

  • You train, but you’re making it up as you go. Whatever the gym is offering that day. No plan, no order, no way to tell if last month was better than this one.
  • You used to train and stopped. Work, a move, a new job. You want back in with a plan that fits the life you have now. (Getting back into it.)
  • Your week isn’t predictable. Long days, travel, a schedule that moves. You need a plan that bends without falling apart.
  • You want to be stronger and build muscle. Both, without picking a lane or reading six programs. (Strength and muscle each have their own page.)

Not a fit if…

  • You want someone standing next to you every set. Then a few sessions with a trainer at your gym are the better start, and online coaching can take it from there. (The honest comparison.)
  • Something hurts, or a doctor is managing a condition. Doctor or physical therapist first, then either of us, with their OK.
  • You want a plan that is hard for its own sake. The plan is as much as you can recover from and repeat, not as much as one good day can handle.

What coaching fixes

You probably don’t need a harder workout. You need a plan.

If you already lift, you know the exercises. What usually goes missing is everything around them: the order, the progression, the honest read on effort, and a week that survives work.

01

Structure instead of improvising.

The 2026 position stand of the American College of Sports Medicine concludes that healthy adults should do progressive resistance training, which asks a little more of you over time.1 That is what a plan is. Random sessions feel like work but give you nothing to build on, and nothing to look back at when you wonder if it’s working.

02

Showing up twice a week is already a real program.

The World Health Organization’s guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week for adults.2 The ACSM stand finds strength is enhanced with two or more sessions a week.1 So a man with a packed schedule isn’t choosing between a perfect plan and nothing. Two good sessions count, and a third is a bonus. (How often to train.)

03

An honest read on effort.

Training hard and training smart get confused. A review of the research on predicting how many reps are left in a set found people were imperfect at it, regardless of their training background.3 Which means “I had nothing left” or “that was easy” is an estimate, not a measurement. So we track reps in reserve and the numbers in the logbook, and let the trend tell us whether to go heavier. (How hard each set should be.)

04

Strength and size, from the same plan.

A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies found that gains in muscle size and strength both increase as weekly volume increases, with diminishing returns.4 In plain terms, more sets help, up to a point, and then they help less. Most of the people in that analysis were men, 79.1 percent, with an average age of about 25,4 so its findings come largely from younger men. Where a goal needs more, the build-muscle and strength pages go into it.

How it runs

A plan that works around your work week.

An outline of the mechanics. Your real plan is built from your goal, your history, your schedule and your equipment.

  1. Your equipment, your days

    I write the plan around whatever equipment you have: a full gym, a garage rack, a pair of dumbbells. It lives in the Trainerize app, with the lifts, the sets and the weight from last time. With three days a week, I usually start people on full body.

  2. Numbers that don’t lie

    You weigh in twice a week and send progress photos every two weeks. The logbook tells the rest: which lifts moved, which stalled, which sessions got skipped and why. (Consistency, not motivation.)

  3. The weekly call

    We go through the week: what the logbook shows, what work and life allowed, and the one thing to change next. Between calls you can message me.

  4. The bad week

    On a busy week I cut exercises before I cut effort. You still do the main lifts and still push them; you keep the workout right for your level. A short session you did beats a full one you skipped. (Coaching for a busy week.)

Questions

Questions men ask.

Short answers. The rest is on the main questions page.

How many sets a week should I do?

There isn’t one number I can give a stranger. A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies found that more weekly sets kept paying off for muscle size and strength, but each extra set paid less than the one before.4 I start from your schedule and your history, and the logbook shows how it’s going.

I work long hours. Does online coaching fit?

It’s built around your work week. Tell me how many days you can really train and what equipment you have, and the plan is written around both and lives in the Trainerize app. The World Health Organization’s adult guideline asks for muscle-strengthening on at least two days a week,2 so a two-session week is a legitimate one. When work takes over, the plan gets shorter, not softer. (Coaching for a busy week.)

I used to lift and stopped. Can I come back with coaching?

Yes. Tell me what you were doing last, when you stopped, how many days you can train and what you have to train with. The plan starts from there, and the weekly call is where we change it. (Getting back into it.)

How will I know it’s working?

You’ll have numbers to look at: the lifts you log in Trainerize, two weigh-ins a week and progress photos every two weeks. The reps-left number in the log is an estimate, and the research review I cite found people were imperfect at predicting it whatever their training background.3 So we read the trend across weeks, not one hard set.

Should I just hire a trainer at my gym?

If you want a person beside you for every set, a few sessions with a trainer at your gym are the better start, because online I can’t stand next to you. Online coaching suits you if you can get yourself there and want the plan, the order and the weekly adjustments. (The honest comparison.)
