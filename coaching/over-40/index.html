What the research says

Lifting still works as the years pass.

Much of the research on lifting later in life studies people 50 or 65 and up, so I’ll say which group each finding covers. The direction is the same throughout.

01

Muscle responds to use.

The National Strength and Conditioning Association’s position statement on older adults says aging is linked to losses in muscle mass, strength and function, and that resistance training is a powerful way to counter that loss.1 Lifting is how you push back.

02

Starting later still works.

A meta-analysis of 47 studies and 1,079 adults aged 50 and over found resistance exercise improved each of the four strength tests analysed, with higher-intensity training linked to bigger gains.2 If a doctor is managing a condition for you, check with them first.

03

The bar is lower than you think.

For adults 18 to 64, the World Health Organization recommends muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or greater intensity, involving all the major muscle groups, on two or more days a week.4 Two days is a real program. (Why two or three days works.)

From 65, the same guidelines ask for more variety: varied activity that emphasises functional balance and strength training, at moderate or greater intensity, on three or more days a week.4 That is years away for many readers, and it is a reason to build the habit now.

What changes

The training is the same. The setup is more careful.

An outline, not a prescription. Your real plan is built around your goal, your history and your week.

  1. A proper warm-up.

    More of the session goes to getting ready: a few minutes of easy movement, then lighter warm-up sets of your first lift before the weight you came to lift. (How to warm up before lifting.)

  2. Weights you can control.

    The American College of Sports Medicine’s review found muscle growth did not differ between lighter and heavier loads, while strength gains were bigger with heavier ones.3 So you don’t have to live at the heavy end to make progress, and you build up to heavier work rather than starting there.

  3. Swaps instead of skipping.

    The same review found equipment type and exercise complexity did not consistently change training outcomes.3 When a movement doesn’t sit right, there is usually another way to train the same muscles, and we swap it. If it hurts, that is a question for a doctor or physical therapist first.

  4. Recovery you can plan.

    With three days a week I usually start people on full body. In one trial of healthy adults aged 65 to 79, lifting once, twice or three times a week gave similar strength gains,5 so more days is not automatically better. The right number is the one you can recover from and keep doing. (Rest days, explained.) And on a busy week I cut exercises before I cut effort.

In your 50s and beyond

In your 50s, 60s and 70s, strength still rose.

The meta-analysis above included adults aged 50 and over, and resistance exercise improved their strength.2 The lifting-frequency trial used healthy adults aged 65 to 79, and strength rose in all the lifting groups.5

The coaching works the same way at 55 as at 35: workouts in the app, weigh-ins twice a week, progress photos every two weeks, and a weekly call. The call is where we adjust the plan if a week went badly or an exercise didn’t sit right.

Questions

Questions people over 40 ask.

Short answers. The rest is on the main questions page.

Is it too late to start lifting at 40 or 50?

The research I’d point to pooled 47 studies of 1,079 adults aged 50 and over, and every one of the four strength tests it analysed improved with resistance exercise.2 That covers people 50 and up and is not a promise about you. There’s no age cutoff for applying, and your plan starts from your history and equipment. If a doctor is managing a condition for you, check with them first.

How many days a week should I lift after 40?

For adults 18 to 64, the World Health Organization asks for muscle-strengthening on two or more days a week.4 Given three free days, full-body sessions are my usual starting point; given two, we start with two. The right number is the one you can keep. (How often to train.)

Can I lift just once or twice a week?

Yes. In the one trial I cite, healthy adults aged 65 to 79 who lifted once, twice or three times a week gained similar strength, so extra sessions didn’t buy extra strength there.5 We set the number of days from your schedule and how the last week went. (Rest days, explained.)

Do I have to lift heavy?

Not every set. The American College of Sports Medicine’s 2026 review of healthy adults found lighter and heavier loads built similar amounts of muscle, with the bigger strength gains on the heavy side.3 That review wasn’t an over-40 analysis. You build up to heavier weights over the first weeks, and the warm-up comes first: a few minutes of easy movement, then lighter sets of your first lift.

I’m a woman over 40. Is this the right page, or the women’s page?

Both apply, and the plan is one plan. This page covers what changes after 40: the warm-up, the exercise swaps and the recovery built into the week. Coaching for women covers getting stronger when you have done mostly cardio or classes, or followed generic programs and dropped them. Read whichever matches your question; the application is the same.

I haven’t exercised in years. How do I get in shape after 40?

Start smaller than you think: walk most days and lift twice a week at two sets with reps to spare for the first two weeks, then add the third day and the full sets over the next month. The step-by-step version is on getting in shape after 40. With coaching, that ramp is written around your equipment and your week, and we adjust it on the weekly call.

What if an exercise doesn’t feel right?

Tell me, on the weekly call or in a message, and we swap it for one that trains the same muscles. If something hurts rather than just feels hard, check with your doctor before you keep going.
