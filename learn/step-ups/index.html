Step-ups: box height, the push and where they fit

One foot on a box, and you stand up on it. Step-ups train one leg at a time with nothing more than a sturdy box and a pair of dumbbells, and they score near the top of the glute-activity studies. Here's the box height, the rep, the versions, what the research says they train, and where they go in a week.

Short answer: Put one whole foot on a box about knee height, lean forward slightly, and stand up by pushing through that front heel, letting the back foot just follow. Step down under control and repeat. Holding dumbbells at your sides, do two or three sets of 8 to 12 a leg, after your squat or hinge.

A step-up is a single-leg squat that starts at the bottom: one foot on a box, and you stand up on that leg until you're on top of the box. It needs a box or a sturdy step and nothing else, it's easy to learn, and it loads one leg at a time, which is how light dumbbells keep up with your legs.

This page covers the box height, the rep, the dumbbell, knee-drive and other versions, what step-ups train, and where they fit a leg day. The same one-leg job with your back foot on a bench is the Bulgarian split squat.

How high should the box be?

About knee height for most people: high enough that your front thigh is close to level with the floor when your foot is on the box, low enough that you can stand up without bouncing off the back foot. One study of loaded step-ups used an 18-inch (46-centimeter) box.1

  • Starting out: a low step or the bottom stair, with no weight, until the rep feels steady.
  • Most sets: about knee height, a weight bench or a sturdy box.
  • Higher: only if you can still stand up without pushing off the back foot. A taller box that you jump off isn't a harder step-up; it's a different exercise.

Whatever you stand on has to stay put. Test a chair or a step at home before you trust it with your weight, and put it against a wall if it slides.

The rep: push through the front foot

  1. Whole foot on the box. Heel included, not just the toes hanging on the edge.
  2. Lean forward a little, chest over the front knee.
  3. Stand up through the front heel. The back foot leaves the floor and just follows; it shouldn't push you up. If it does, lower the box or the weight.
  4. Stand tall at the top, hips straight, then bring the back foot up beside the front one or keep it hovering.
  5. Step down under control with the back foot, the front foot staying on the box. Finish all the reps on one leg, then switch.

The back-foot push is the fault that matters. It turns the step-up into a hop and moves the work to the leg you're not training. A slow count on the way down, about two seconds, makes it hard to cheat.

Step-ups: muscles worked

The glutes and the front of the thigh of the leg on the box, with the hamstrings helping. The evidence:

  • Near the top for glute activity. A 2020 review of 16 studies of glute activity rated step-ups and their variations highest, followed by loaded exercises such as deadlifts, hip thrusts, lunges and squats.2
  • The forward step-up for the glutes and hamstrings. In 15 women who regularly trained their lower body, lifting a six-rep max on an 18-inch box, the forward step-up produced the most glute and hamstring activity of four versions; the crossover step-up worked the side glute (gluteus medius) most on the way up.1
  • The inner thigh muscle, sideways. In 30 healthy adults (19 men and 11 women), the lateral step-up was one of only two exercises in its study above 45% of a maximal contraction in the vastus medialis, the inner muscle of the front thigh.3
  • The load scales the work. In nine healthy college-aged men lifting up to 75% of their body mass, the estimated forces in the glutes and the front of the thigh rose with the load, often more steeply in the step-up and the split squat than in the squat.4

Activity is measured during a set; it isn't growth over weeks. In a nine-week trial that measured both, higher glute activity in the first session didn't consistently predict glute growth.5

Step-ups for glutes

A training trial has tested step-ups as part of a program. In a six-week trial, 19 resistance-trained adults followed either a program of vertically loaded exercises or one of horizontally loaded ones. Glute thickness rose similarly in both groups, and the vertical group gained more in the vastus medialis, the inner muscle of the front thigh.6 A 2025 review of 12 studies of glute growth lists that vertical program as back squats, barbell step-ups and box jumps, and the horizontal one as hip thrusts, reverse hyperextensions and long jumps.7 The step-ups were one part of that program, so the trial can't say what they did alone. The review lists step-ups with squats and leg presses as exercises that grow the front of the thigh along with the glutes.7

For more glute work in the rep: the forward version, a box near knee height, a slight forward lean, and no push from the back foot. That's the version the leg day swaps in for the lunge when glutes are the goal, and the one the glute workout uses.

Dumbbell, knee-drive and other versions

VersionHowWhen to use it
Bodyweight step-upHands free or one hand on a wallFor beginners: learning the rep, the first weeks at home
Dumbbell step-upA dumbbell in each hand, arms straight at your sidesThe standard version; the dumbbell plan opens Day C with it
Goblet step-upOne dumbbell or kettlebell at the chestOnly one weight, or a grip that tires first
Step-up with knee driveAt the top, drive the back knee up to hip height before stepping downA balance challenge at a lighter weight
Lateral step-upStand beside the box and step up sidewaysA sideways version; it scored high for the inner front thigh in one study
Barbell step-upA bar on the upper backHeavy sets, once balance is solid
Cable step-upA low cable handle held at your sideA gym with a cable station and no heavy dumbbells

The knee drive adds balance work, not leg work: the knee rises after the front leg has already done its job. Keep the weight lighter so the front leg still does the standing.

How heavy can a step-up go? In well-trained rugby players lifting 70% to 90% of their max, the step-up produced more force through each leg than the squat, despite the lighter bar. The authors suggest that's enough overload for strength, and call for training studies to test it.8

Sets, reps and where step-ups fit a leg day

  • Two or three sets of 8 to 12 a leg, stopping with a rep or two in reserve. (How to judge reps left.) The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand advises at least two sets per exercise.9
  • After the squat or the hinge, in the single-leg slot: in place of the lunge or the split squat, not on top of both.
  • First at home, as the dumbbell plan's Day C does, when dumbbells are the heaviest thing you have.
  • Count it for the glutes and the thighs in your weekly total. The same stand found muscle growth was enhanced by ten or more sets per muscle group a week.9

Alternatives

  • No box: a reverse lunge, or a split squat with the back foot on the floor.
  • A bench: the Bulgarian split squat, the same one-leg job without stepping up.
  • No box, and good balance: the pistol squat, a full squat on one leg.
  • Two legs: the goblet squat, or the leg press on a machine.

Common mistakes

  • Pushing off the back foot. Lower the box or the weight until the front leg does the standing.
  • Half the foot on the box. Put the whole foot up, heel included.
  • Dropping down. Step down slowly; the lowering is half the rep.
  • Front knee caving in. Keep it travelling over your toes.
  • A box that moves. Use something that stays put, or brace it against a wall.
  • Stepping through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
