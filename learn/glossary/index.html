Training terms, in plain words

Reps in reserve, RPE, volume, a deload, a hinge: training has its own vocabulary, and most of it is simpler than it sounds. Here are 98 terms, A to Z, each defined in a sentence or two and linked to the page that covers it in full.

Short answer: Three terms do most of the work in a training plan: reps in reserve (how many more good reps you could have done before failure), volume (the hard sets a muscle gets each week) and progressive overload (doing a little more than last time, on purpose). The other 95 are below, A to Z, each with a link.

This glossary defines the training words used across this site. Each entry gives the definition first, then a sentence on how the site uses the term, then a link to the article that covers it in full. Every term has its own address, so you can link straight to one: /learn/glossary/#rir goes to reps in reserve.

Looking for a plan instead? The free workout plans are sorted by days and equipment.

A

Active recovery

Light movement on a day off, done on purpose: a walk, an easy bike ride or swim, some mobility work. The World Health Organization describes light-intensity activity as about a 2 to 4 on a 0 to 10 effort scale.1 On this site it's one of the two kinds of rest day; if you're breathing hard, it isn't recovery anymore. What a rest day looks like.

Ankle mobility

How far your knee can travel forward over your foot with the heel down: the ankle range a deep squat uses. The site's check is the knee-to-wall test, scored as the farthest spot from the wall where your knee still touches it with the heel down; raised heels are the quick workaround while you work on it. Ankle mobility for squats.

B

Barbell row

A horizontal pull done hinged forward, torso between about 45 degrees and parallel to the floor, pulling the bar in a straight line to your lower ribs or upper stomach with your torso still. On this site it's a main pull for the back day; the Pendlay version starts every rep from a dead stop on the floor. Barbell row form.

Bench press

The horizontal push: lying on a bench with your feet flat and your shoulder blades pulled back, lowering the bar under control to your chest and pressing it back up. The site's form page covers the setup, the grip width and the rep, and adds weight in small steps; the version with the bench raised has its own entry. Bench press form.

Body recomposition

Building muscle while losing body fat over the same stretch of time, so the scale may barely move while your lifts go up. The site tracks it with strength, a waist measurement, photos and a weekly weight average rather than a single weigh-in. Can you do both at once?

Bodyweight squat

A squat with no weight: feet about shoulder-width apart, toes turned out a little, sitting down between your heels as deep as your heels stay on the floor, then standing. The site starts it to a bench and, once 20 reps are easy, slows the lowering, adds a pause at the bottom, or adds a weight. The bodyweight squat.

Bodyweight training

Training with your own body as the resistance: squats, glute bridges, push-ups, doorframe rows and planks. On this site it's the no-equipment option, progressed by harder versions instead of more weight. A bodyweight workout.

Bracing

Taking a big breath and tightening your midsection before a rep, as if about to be poked in the stomach, so your trunk stays stiff under the load. The site's squat, deadlift and goblet squat pages all build it into the setup. The squat setup, step by step.

Bulgarian split squat

A one-leg squat with the top of your back foot resting on a knee-high bench a long step behind you, lowering until the back knee nearly touches the floor, then driving up through the front foot. On this site it's a leg-day exercise, two or three sets of 8 to 12 a leg, with the step-up as its sibling. The Bulgarian split squat.

C

Cable machine

A machine that moves a weight stack through a cable and a pulley, so you can pull or press from almost any angle: lat pulldowns, cable rows, cable crunches. The site uses them as gym options on the back, shoulder and core pages. A back workout with machines and cables.

Calf raise

Rising onto the balls of your feet against a load, then lowering all the way into a stretch, pausing, and rising as high as you can. Standing, with straight knees, trains both calf muscles; seated, with bent knees, adds work for the deeper soleus. The site makes the standing raise the main calf exercise. The calf workout.

Calories

The energy in food and drink. This glossary is about training, so calories get a pointer, not numbers: calories and protein has the site's general guidance on both.

Carry

Walking with a weight: a farmer's carry has one in each hand, a suitcase carry one in one hand. On this site it's bracing on the move, the "stop the lean" job in the core block, ended when your position slips rather than when you can't go on. Carries.

Chest fly

Arms out wide with a slight, fixed bend at the elbow, brought together in a wide arc in front of your chest, then opened until you feel a stretch and no deeper. With dumbbells, cables or a machine, the site keeps it after the presses, an extra rather than the base of a chest day. The chest fly.

Compound exercise

An exercise that moves more than one joint and works several muscles at once, like a squat, a deadlift, a bench press, a row or a pulldown. Every plan on this site is built on them, with isolation exercises added after. Compound exercises, explained.

Consistency

Training on your planned days week after week, whether or not you feel like it. The site treats it as a system problem, not a willpower one: set days and times, a smaller workout for bad days, and never missing twice in a row. Consistency vs motivation.

Cool-down

Easy, low- to moderate-intensity exercise done within about an hour after training. A 2018 review found an active cool-down largely ineffective for performance later the same day and the next day.2 On this site it's optional: keep it short and easy if you like one. Do you need a cool-down?

D

Deadlift

Lifting a weight from the floor to standing by hinging at the hips with a straight back, then driving the hips forward. The conventional deadlift pulls a bar from the floor; the sumo version widens the stance with the hands inside the knees; the trap bar puts you inside a frame with handles at your sides. The site teaches the hinge first, then picks a version. Deadlift form.

Deload

A planned lighter week: the same days and exercises, with fewer sets, lighter weights or sets stopped further from failure. Coaches interviewed for a 2022 study typically programmed one every four to six weeks,3 but that's practice, not proof: coaches in a 2023 panel agreed that scientific studies on deloading are limited.4 How to deload.

DOMS (delayed-onset muscle soreness)

The dull ache in a muscle that shows up after exercise it isn't used to. It typically appears 12 to 24 hours after, peaks between one and three days, and is gone within five to seven.5 On this site, soreness is treated as a poor scorecard for a workout, and sharp pain as something else entirely. Muscle soreness, explained.

Double progression

Adding reps inside a rep range first, then weight: start at the bottom of a range like 8 to 12, try to add a rep or two each week, and when every set reaches 12, add weight and drop back to 8. It's the simplest progression system on this site. How fast to add weight.

Drop set

A set taken to failure, then continued straight away with a lighter weight, usually for one to three drops. A 2023 meta-analysis found drop sets and regular sets built similar muscle, with some drop-set versions taking a half to a third of the time.6 The site's advice: use them on single-joint exercises, not on heavy free-weight lifts such as the squat. Drop sets.

Dumbbell row

A horizontal pull with one arm: one hand and one knee braced on a bench, the dumbbell hanging under your shoulder, pulled back past your ribs with the elbow close to your side, then lowered to a straight arm. On this site it's the pull a single dumbbell can cover, two or three hard sets of 8 to 12 per arm. The dumbbell row.

E

Eccentric and concentric

The two halves of a rep. The concentric part is the lifting, when the muscle shortens; the eccentric part is the lowering, when it lengthens under the load. The site uses slow eccentrics, or negatives, as one way into a first pull-up. Negatives and the pull-up.

F

Face pulls

A cable exercise: with the rope at about face height and your arms straight, you pull it toward your forehead, elbows high, until your hands are level with your ears. The site uses it for the rear of the shoulder and the muscles that turn the arm out, and gives band and dumbbell versions for when there's no cable. Face pulls, step by step.

Flexibility

How far the muscles, tendons and other soft tissues around a joint let it move: one trainable part of its range of motion.7 On this site, flexibility is kept separate from mobility, which adds the control to use that range in a lift. Mobility vs flexibility.

Form

The way a rep is done: your setup, the path the weight takes, the depth and the control. On this site, good form means each lift's checkpoints, repeated every rep, not one perfect position for everyone. Squat form, deadlift form and bench press form.

Free weights and machines

Free weights are barbells, dumbbells and kettlebells you move and balance yourself; machines guide the weight along a fixed path. A 2023 meta-analysis of 13 studies found no difference between the two on strength or muscle size when compared directly, though strength gains were specific to the equipment people trained on.8 The site's rule: use whichever you have and will keep doing. Machines vs free weights.

Frequency

How often you train: sessions a week, or how many times a week each muscle is worked. On this site, the days you can repeat come first, and the sets in them matter more than the layout. How often to train.

Front squat

A squat with the bar across the front of your shoulders, held with a clean grip, crossed arms or straps, elbows high, sitting straight down between your heels with the torso upright. The site's page covers the three ways to hold the bar and how it compares with the back squat; whatever the hold, if the elbows drop, the bar rolls forward. The front squat.

Full body

A split where every session trains the whole body, usually with a squat, a hinge, a push and a pull. It's the site's default for two or three days a week, and most of its plans use it. Full body and the other splits.

G

Glute bridge

Lying on your back, knees bent and feet flat, you drive through your heels to lift your hips until shoulders, hips and knees line up, then lower them. It's the floor version of the hip thrust, and the site's choice when there's no bench or while you're learning the hip drive; two or three hard sets of 8 to 20. The glute bridge.

Goblet squat

A squat holding one dumbbell or kettlebell against your chest. The site treats it as the beginner's squat: no rack, no bar on your back, and a weight in front to sit down against. The goblet squat.

Good mornings

A hinge with the bar across your upper back: hips pushed back, torso bowing forward until it nears parallel or the backs of your thighs are fully stretched, then driving the hips forward to stand. The site uses it as a second hinge, not the first, started light at two or three sets of 8 to 12. Good mornings.

H

Hack squat

A squat on a machine: back flat on the pad, shoulders under the pads, feet on the platform, lowering until your thighs are at least parallel and pressing up through your whole foot. The pad holds your torso, so the site treats it as heavy leg work standing, with no balance to manage, next to the leg press and the barbell squat. The hack squat.

Hammer curls

A dumbbell curl with the palms facing your thighs and the thumbs up, elbows still at your sides. On this site it's the second curl of an arm day, two or three hard sets of 8 to 15, and the page says the grip changes less than its reputation. Hammer curls.

Hamstrings

The muscles on the back of the thigh, with two jobs: extending the hip and bending the knee. So the site trains them two ways, a hinge like the Romanian deadlift and a curl, each reaching parts the other doesn't; three sets of each, once or twice a week. The hamstring workout.

Heart-rate reserve

Your max heart rate minus your resting heart rate. A zone given as a percentage of reserve takes that share of the gap and adds it back on top of your resting rate, which the site's calculator does by default, with a resting rate of 60 filled in, unless you clear the box. The heart rate zone calculator.

Heart-rate zones

Bands of heart rate, each a range of percentages of your max, used to set how hard cardio is. The site's calculator splits an estimated or measured max into five zones, with zone 2 at 72 to 82% of max on the scale it uses, and lets the talk test have the last word. Find your heart rate zones.

Hinge

A movement that bends mainly at the hips, with the knees only slightly bent and the back straight: the pattern behind deadlifts, Romanian deadlifts, hip thrusts and back extensions. Every full-body plan on this site has one, alongside a squat, a push and a pull. Learn the hinge.

Hip mobility

How far your hips can move where your lifts need it, which the site tests in two places: the bottom of a bodyweight squat and a hinge with a flat back. If one falls short it adds a few drills after lifting, and it counts lifting through a full range as range work. Hip mobility for lifters.

Hip thrust

A lift done with your upper back on a bench and the weight across your hips: raise the hips until the thighs are level, then lower under control. The site puts it after the squat or hinge, two or three hard sets of 8 to 12; the glute bridge is its floor version. The hip thrust.

Hypertrophy

Muscle growth: an increase in the size of a muscle. The site's reading of the research is that there's no single hypertrophy rep range; a wide range of reps works when the sets are hard. How many reps to build muscle.

I

Incline bench press

A bench press with the bench raised, set on this site to about 30 degrees: shoulder blades back and down, feet flat, the bar or dumbbells lowered to your upper chest just below the collarbones and pressed back up over your shoulders. It's the second press on the site's chest day, after the flat one. The incline bench press.

Interference effect

The idea that cardio done alongside lifting blunts what the lifting builds. The site's reading of the research is that for most people the cost to muscle size and maximal strength is small or nothing, and it grows when you are well trained, when the cardio comes right before or after lifting, and when the goal is explosive power; its rule is to lift first when strength is the priority. The interference effect.

Isolation exercise

An exercise that moves one joint and targets one muscle or group, like a biceps curl, a lateral raise or a leg extension. The site adds them after the compound lifts, for muscles you want more work for. Compound vs isolation.

K

Kettlebell swing

A hinge, not a squat: the bell is hiked back between your legs, then driven forward by snapping the hips through, the arms only guiding it as it floats up to about chest height. The site teaches it from the same hinge as its deadlift page, as one of the five movements in its one-bell plan. The swing is a hinge.

L

Lat pulldown

A vertical pull on a cable machine: thighs locked under the pad, the bar pulled from overhead to the top of your chest with the elbows driving down, then let back up until your arms are straight. On this site it's the pull-up's motion with a weight you choose, and one path to a first pull-up. Lat pulldown form.

Lateral raises

Standing tall with light dumbbells at your sides, raising your arms out to the sides with the elbows nearly straight until your hands reach shoulder height, then lowering slowly. The site gives the side of the shoulder its own exercise because presses mostly cover the front; a cable or a machine does the same job. Lateral raises.

Leg curl

A machine curl for the hamstrings: knees lined up with the pivot, the pad just above the heels, curling your heels down and back as far as they go, then returning slowly to nearly straight. The site picks the seated version when there's a choice and pairs the curl with a hinge; at home, slider or towel curls stand in. The leg curl.

Leg extension

A machine for the front of the thigh: knees lined up with the pivot, the pad on your lower shins, legs straightened until nearly locked, then lowered slowly to a deep knee bend. On this site it fills the gap squats and leg presses leave in the thigh, and the page says not to skip the bottom half of the range. The leg extension.

Leg press

A machine version of the squat pattern: seated with your back and hips flat on the pad, you push a weighted platform away and lower until your knees bend to about a right angle or deeper. With no bar to balance, the site's leg day lets you pick it or the back squat as the first lift. The leg press.

M

Maintenance

In training, doing enough to keep the strength and muscle you have, through a busy stretch or a trip. One review found that strength and muscle size could be kept with as little as one strength session a week and one set per exercise, at least in younger people, as long as the load stayed heavy.9 Here the word is used in its training sense only. Keeping a travel week on track.

Max heart rate

The highest heart rate you can reach, the number the zones are percentages of. Without a measured value, the site's calculator estimates it as 208 minus 0.7 times your age and shows 220 minus age beside it; its page explains why age formulas miss; a measured max overrides the estimate. Why age formulas miss.

Mobility

As lifters use the word, the range of motion you can reach and control in a lift, not just reach. On this site, lifting through a full range already counts, and a ten-minute routine is the add-on for more range. A mobility routine for lifters.

Muscle memory

The idea that muscle built before comes back faster after a break. The site's page on getting back into training is more cautious. It says the movement comes back faster than the muscle does, that after a layoff you start out of practice, not from zero, and that the restart is about half your old volume with weights that feel easy. Getting back after a break.

O

One-rep max (1RM)

The most weight you can lift once, with good form, on a given exercise. Studies use it to set loads as a percentage, but the site's method doesn't need one: choose the reps first, then a weight that leaves a few in reserve. How much weight to lift. To estimate yours from one hard set, the one-rep max calculator.

Overhead press

A press that takes a weight from the shoulders to straight arms overhead, done standing or seated with a barbell, dumbbells or a machine. The site calls it the vertical push, the one a week of bench pressing leaves out, and builds its shoulder workout on it. The dumbbell version that turns the palms is the Arnold press. Overhead press form.

P

Pallof press

Standing side-on to a cable or band anchored at chest height, holding the handle at your chest with both hands and pressing it straight out without letting it turn you. On this site it's the "stop the twist" job in the core block, made harder by moving from kneeling to standing with the feet together, then heel to toe. The core block.

Partial reps

Reps through only part of the range of motion, often the stretched bottom half. The site puts full reps first and allows a few partials at the end of a set once full reps run out; it does not treat them as better than full reps. Partial reps and the lengthened position.

Plank

A hold on your forearms and toes with your body in one straight line, your midsection stopping your lower back from sagging. The site holds it only as long as the line holds, and when a good hold gets easy, moves to a harder version rather than a longer time. How to do a plank.

Plate math

Working out which plates go on each side of a barbell: subtract the bar from the target weight, split the rest in two, and load each side starting with the heaviest plate. The site's calculator does it in pounds or kilograms, and the smallest change you can make is a pair of its lightest plates. The plate calculator.

Plateau

A stretch where a lift stops going up. The site treats a stall as information, not failure, and checks sleep and food, effort, volume and the rep range before changing exercises. When you stall.

Progressive overload

Gradually asking your muscles to do a little more over time, with more weight, more reps or more sets. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 position stand recommends adding 2 to 10% to the weight once you can do one or two reps more than your target.10 Every plan on this site moves forward this way, one logged session at a time. Progressive overload, explained.

Protein

One of the parts of food your body uses to build and repair muscle. Like calories, it gets a pointer here rather than numbers: calories and protein covers it.

Pull-up

Hanging from a bar with straight arms and pulling until your chin clears it, palms facing away from you; with the palms facing you it's a chin-up. If you can't do one yet, the site trains the parts: slow negatives, assisted pull-ups and lat pulldowns, taking a little less help as each gets easier. How to do a pull-up.

Push/pull/legs

A split with three kinds of session: push (chest, shoulders and triceps), pull (back and biceps) and legs. The site suggests it for five or six days a week; on three days, each muscle is trained only once a week. Which split for your days.

Push-up

Hands a little wider than your shoulders, body in one straight line from head to heels, lowering your chest close to the floor and pressing back up. Too hard from the floor? The site starts you with your hands on a counter or a bench, or on your knees, and works lower over time before raising your feet. How to do push-ups.

R

Range of motion

How far a joint moves during a rep, from the bottom position to the top. A 2021 meta-analysis of 16 studies found that a full range of motion built more strength and more lower-body muscle than partial reps.11 So the site's squat pages give a depth target rather than a partial rep. How deep to squat.

Rep

Short for repetition: one complete lift, down and up. A rep range, like 8 to 12, tells you how many to aim for in a set, and on this site the range only means something when the set is hard. How many reps.

Reps in reserve (RIR)

How many more good reps you could have done before failure. For most working sets, the site aims for about 1 to 3 reps in reserve.12,13 New lifters start further from failure, with three or four left. Reps in reserve, explained.

Resistance band

Elastic resistance: the more you stretch the band, the harder it pulls back. On this site a set of bands and a door anchor covers every movement a full-body plan needs; you make it harder with more reps, a step farther from the anchor, or a thicker band. A resistance band workout.

Rest between sets

The time you rest after one set before the next. A 2024 meta-analysis found a small benefit for muscle growth from resting more than 60 seconds and no clear gain past 90.14 The site gives heavy compound lifts two to five minutes and smaller exercises one to two. How long to rest.

Rest day

A day with no training planned, either fully off or with light active recovery. On this site, a day between hard sessions for the same muscles matters more than the total number of rest days. How many rest days you need.

Romanian deadlift (RDL)

A deadlift that starts standing and lowers the bar down your thighs with the knees slightly bent, only as far as your hamstrings allow, before you stand back up. It's the hamstring version of the deadlift, and the site uses it as the hinge in its leg and back days. The Romanian deadlift, step by step.

RPE (rating of perceived exertion)

A 1 to 10 rating of how hard a set was. For lifting, it's built on reps in reserve: RPE 10 means nothing left, RPE 9 one rep left, RPE 8 two. So RPE is 10 minus your reps in reserve, and the site's 1 to 3 RIR target is RPE 7 to 9. RIR and RPE. For a first-guess load at a target RPE, the calculator's RPE table.

S

Seated cable row

A horizontal pull on a cable: feet on the platform, knees slightly bent, sitting tall, the handle pulled to your stomach with the elbows close, then let out until your arms are long without rounding forward. The site keeps the torso nearly still, leaning back a little and no more. The seated cable row.

Set

A group of reps done in a row before a rest. On this site, the sets that count toward your week are the hard ones, ending with only a few good reps left; warm-up sets and easy sets don't. What counts as a set.

Skull crusher

The lying triceps extension: with a bar or two dumbbells over your shoulders on a bench, you lower the weight toward your forehead, bending only at the elbows, then straighten your arms. The site's arm page runs it or the overhead extension, one of the two at a time. Skull crushers.

Sled push

Driving a loaded sled across the floor with your arms long and your body in a straight line from head to back foot, in short, quick steps for 10 to 20 meters. The site loads it so it never stops moving and runs it four to six times after lifting or on another day, mostly as leg work. The sled push.

Smith machine

A barbell that runs on a fixed track, so the bar's path is set for you. The site's bench press, barbell row and incline pages each list the Smith version as another way to load the same lift. Dumbbells, the Smith machine and benching alone.

Specificity

You get strongest at the version you train: the tool, the lift, one limb or two. On this site that's the rule for choosing between dumbbells and a barbell: train the lift whose number matters to you, use the other for the second exercise, and keep one version for a block of weeks so the log compares like with like. You get strongest at the version you train.

Split

The plan for which muscles or movements each session trains across your week: full body, upper/lower or push/pull/legs. A 2024 meta-analysis of 14 studies found split and full-body routines built similar strength and muscle when weekly volume was matched.15 The site's rule: count your days first, then pick the split that fits them. Which workout split.

Squat pattern

A movement that bends the hips, knees and ankles together: back squats, box squats, goblet squats, leg presses and split squats. It's one of the four patterns every full-body plan on this site starts with, alongside the hinge, the push and the pull. Squat form.

Step-ups

One whole foot on a box about knee height, a slight lean forward, standing up by pushing through that front heel with the back foot just following, then stepping down under control. The site puts them after the squat or hinge, holding dumbbells at your sides, two or three sets of 8 to 12 a leg. Step-ups.

Strength

How much force you can produce, usually measured by how much you can lift for a set number of reps. On this site, heavier sets of about 1 to 6 reps are the strength end of the range, though moderate reps get most people most of the way. How many reps for strength.

Superset

Two exercises done back to back, then a rest. A 2025 meta-analysis found supersets kept about the same reps and total load as regular sets in sessions about 37% shorter, though they felt harder.16 The site pairs different muscles and keeps heavy free-weight lifts out of them. Supersets, explained.

T

Talk test

A check on cardio effort by speaking: if you can say a sentence or two aloud comfortably, you're below your first threshold; if you doubt you could keep talking, you're at it, so ease off a little. On this site it has the last word over any heart-rate formula. How to find your zone 2 without a lab.

Tempo

How fast you move through each part of a rep, especially the lowering. The site's default is a controlled lowering of about two or three seconds with no bounce, a short pause only if it keeps you in position, and an intentional lift. A default tempo to use.

Time under tension

How long a set keeps a muscle working. Tempo, the speed of each rep, is often written as four numbers in seconds, in order: lowering, bottom pause, lift, top pause. The site judges a set by the reps you had left, not by seconds, and keeps its default tempo under Tempo. Time under tension, explained.

Training to failure

Ending a set when you can't complete another rep with decent form. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found that taking sets to failure didn't add to gains in strength, muscle size or power.12 So the site's sets stop a few reps short. Should you train to failure?

U

Upper/lower

A split that alternates upper-body sessions (chest, back, shoulders, arms) with lower-body sessions (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves). The site suggests it for four days a week, two of each, so every muscle is trained twice a week. Which split for your days.

V

Volume

How much training a muscle gets, counted on this site as hard sets per muscle per week. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found that 10 or more sets per muscle group a week enhanced muscle growth.12 New lifters start lower and add a set or two when progress stalls. How many sets per week.

W

Warm-up

What you do before your working sets: a few minutes of easy movement, then warm-up sets of your first lift, climbing from about half your working weight to about 80% of it. Later exercises for the same muscles need one light set or none. How to warm up before lifting.

Warm-up sets

The lighter sets of your first lift before the working sets, climbing from about half the working weight. On this site the count depends on the working set: more before a heavy set of five or fewer reps, fewer before higher reps, and none of them count toward your weekly sets. The calculator builds the ramp. The warm-up set calculator.

Working set

A set at the weight and effort the plan calls for, after the warm-ups. These are the sets you log and count toward your weekly volume, and they should end with a few good reps left. How to log them.

Workout log

A running record of your sessions with five columns: exercise, weight, reps per set, reps left in reserve and a brief note. The site uses it for progression: look at last week's line before you train and aim to beat it by a rep or a small jump in weight. How to keep a workout log.

Z

Zone 2 cardio

On this site, zone 2 means steady cardio at an effort just under your first threshold, where your breathing begins to pick up. You can still talk in full sentences, but it isn't effortless. It's the cardio done beside lifting, found with the talk test and the heart rate zone calculator rather than a lab. Zone 2 cardio, explained.
