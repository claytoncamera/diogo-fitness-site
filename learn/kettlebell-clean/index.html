Kettlebell clean: one bell from a swing to the rack at your chest, without the bang on the wrist

The kettlebell clean takes one bell from between your legs to the rack position at your chest, driven by the hips like a swing. It's the move that gets a bell into place for a press, a squat or a carry. Here's how to do it, how to stop the bell banging your forearm, what the studies measured, the versions, and the sets.

Short answer: Hike one kettlebell back between your legs, drive your hips forward, and guide it up close to your body so it rolls around your hand into the rack at your chest, elbow tucked. A kettlebell clean takes one bell from the floor, or from between your legs, to the rack position: resting against your chest and forearm, the handle at about collarbone height, the elbow tucked in. The power comes from the hips, as in the kettlebell swing; the difference is that the bell travels up close to your body and finishes at your chest instead of floating out in front.

It's the move that gets a bell into place. Most of what you do with a kettlebell at your shoulder, a press, a front squat, a carry, starts with a clean. The barbell lift that shares the name is the power clean; the kettlebell version is lighter, one-armed, and easier to learn on your own.

How to do a kettlebell clean

  1. Set the bell a little in front of you, between your feet, feet about shoulder width. Hinge at the hips with a flat back and grab the handle with one hand.
  2. Hike it back between your legs, high toward your hips, like the start of a swing. Your forearm touches your inner thigh.
  3. Drive your hips forward and stand up hard. The bell moves because your hips snapped, not because your arm lifted it.
  4. Keep it close. Let your elbow stay near your side so the bell rises along your body instead of swinging out. Think of zipping up a jacket.
  5. Open your hand around it. As the bell reaches your chest, loosen your grip and slide your hand through the handle so the bell rolls around it and settles against your forearm, rather than flipping over and landing hard.
  6. Rack it. Wrist straight, elbow tucked in against your ribs, the bell between your forearm and your chest. Stand tall, then let the bell drop back between your legs for the next rep, or press it.

These steps are practical coaching cues, not study findings. The hinge is the same one taught on the swing page, and the squat you can do from the rack is the goblet squat's cousin, with the bell held on one side.

What the research is

A PubMed search for this page found one study that recorded muscle activity in the one-arm kettlebell clean, and one that measured a kettlebell sport routine built on the clean. That's thin, and this page says so.

  • Clean, swing and snatch compared. Fourteen resistance-trained people did five reps each of a one-arm swing, a one-arm snatch and a one-arm clean with a weight they could lift 8 to 10 times. The clean and the snatch drew more activity than the swing from the obliques on the side opposite the bell. The swing drew more from the outer thigh muscle than the clean, and more from the muscles along the spine than the snatch. The authors conclude the three aren't redundant exercises.1
  • The clean as a sport. In kettlebell sport's "long cycle", a clean and a jerk overhead are combined into one repeated cycle, with a bell in each hand: the bells drop from the rack into a swing, then are cleaned back to the rack.2 Thirteen competitive kettlebell athletes, men aged 26 to 53, did as many cycles as they could in 10 minutes: 81 on average, from 60 to 125. Peak heart rate reached 185 beats a minute, and the effort was rated about 17 out of 20. Their maximal squat strength didn't change afterwards.2

Neither study trained people on cleans for weeks, so this page can't say what the clean builds over time. The sport routine was done by competitive athletes with about four and a half years of kettlebell training on average; it shows how hard continuous cleans and jerks can be, not a starting point.

Versions: from the floor, double, clean and press

  • Dead clean: each rep starts from the bell resting on the floor, with no swing back between reps. A practical way to learn the path before you add the hike.
  • Swing clean: the bell drops from the rack back between your legs and comes straight up again, rep after rep. This is the version in the sport routine above.2
  • Double kettlebell clean: a bell in each hand, the same path. Your feet go a little wider so both bells fit between your legs.
  • Clean and press: clean the bell, pause in the rack, press it overhead, lower it to the rack, then drop it into the next clean. The press half is covered on the kettlebell workout.
  • Clean and jerk: clean the bell, then dip and drive it overhead with your legs, as in the barbell clean and jerk.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • The bell banging your forearm. It's flipping over your hand at the top. Keep it closer on the way up and open your hand to slide through the handle earlier.
  • Swinging it out in front. That's a swing that you then curl in. Keep your elbow near your side so the bell travels up your body.
  • Curling it with the arm. If your biceps are tired after a set, the hips aren't doing the work. Snap the hips and let the arm guide.
  • A bent wrist in the rack. The bell pulls your hand back. Keep the wrist straight and the handle deep in your palm.
  • Squatting it instead of hinging it. Hips back, shins near vertical, as in the swing; the swing page covers the difference.
  • Lifting through something that hurts. If you have pain, an injury or a condition, ask a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where they go

These are practical starting points, not findings. The one muscle study used a weight each person could lift 8 to 10 times for five reps,1 and the sport routine ran for 10 minutes in experienced athletes.2

  • To learn it: three sets of five dead cleans on each side with a bell you can swing easily, resting about a minute.
  • In a workout: clean the bell into place before each set of presses or squats on that side, or add clean and press in sets of five on each side.
  • Once or twice a week, in the same session as your swings. They share the same hinge.

Common questions

Kettlebell clean or swing?

Both are driven by the hips. The swing sends the bell out in front to about chest height; the clean brings it in close and stops it at your chest. In the one study comparing them, the clean drew more activity from the obliques and less from the outer thigh.1

What muscles does the kettlebell clean work?

The study above recorded eight: the biceps, the front and back of the shoulder, the muscles along the spine, the outer thigh, one of the hamstrings, the obliques on the opposite side, and the glutes.1 It compared the clean with the swing and the snatch, not with other exercises.

Why does the bell hit my wrist?

Because it's flipping over your hand instead of rolling around it. Keep it close and slide your hand through the handle at the top. Lighter is fine while you learn.

What if something hurts?

If you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, a doctor or a physical therapist should set the limits before you train hard.
