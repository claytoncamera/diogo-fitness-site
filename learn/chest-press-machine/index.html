Chest press machine: the seat, the handles, and how it compares with the bench press

A chest press machine is a bench press done sitting up: you sit with your back against a pad, take two handles at chest height, and push them forward. The machine's arms hold the path, so there's no bar to balance, no spotter needed, and a pin to change the weight. It works the same muscles as the bench press, the chest, the front of the shoulder and the triceps, in somewhat different shares, as the studies below show.1

This page covers the setup, the press, how the machine compares with free weights, when a beginner starts here, the faults, and the sets. The barbell version is on bench press form, and how presses fit a week of chest work is on the chest workout.

For a complete schedule around this exercise, the machine-only full-body plan puts the chest press alongside leg work, rows, pulldowns and core work.

How to set up the chest press machine

  1. Seat height first. Set it so the handles line up with the middle of your chest when you sit. Handles at your collarbones or down at your belly are the two common misses.
  2. Back and head against the pad, feet flat on the floor or the footrest, shoulder blades drawn slightly back and down.
  3. Set the starting position, if the machine has a lever or a handle adjustment: the handles should start level with your chest, far enough back for a stretch across it, but not so far that your shoulders roll forward off the pad to reach them.
  4. Choose the grip. Horizontal handles put your palms down, as on a bench press; vertical handles turn your palms to face each other. Either way, your wrists stay straight over your forearms.

The press

  1. Press the handles forward until your arms are straight, without slamming your elbows into a hard lockout or letting your shoulders leave the pad.
  2. Bring them back under control, taking about two seconds, until your hands are about level with your chest.
  3. Start the next rep just before the weight stack touches down, so the load stays on your muscles between reps.

Your elbows travel a little toward your sides rather than straight out at shoulder height, the same path as on the bench press.

Chest press machine vs bench press

Muscle activity. In 10 competitive bodybuilders lifting 80% of their max, the middle and lower chest worked about as hard on a chest press machine as on a flat bench press. The machine produced less upper-chest activity than any of the free-weight presses, less triceps activity than the flat and decline bench presses, and less side-shoulder activity than all three, which the authors put down to the free weights being less stable.1 In 12 trained men who tested a barbell, a Smith machine and dumbbells, chest activity didn't differ between the three; the loads did.2

Muscle growth. A 2023 meta-analysis of 13 studies with 1,016 participants found no significant difference in muscle growth between free-weight and machine training in the five studies that measured it.3 In one trial, 38 resistance-trained men trained for eight weeks with either barbells or machines, the machine bench press among them, and made similar strength gains and similar growth in the chest and the other muscles measured.4 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found strength gains didn't differ between free weights and machines, and too little data to say whether muscle growth did.5

Strength follows the tool. In the same meta-analysis, free-weight training raised free-weight test scores more, and machine training tended to raise machine scores more.3 In 46 young adults, 26 of them women, who trained with free weights or machines for eight weeks, the machine group gained more on the machine bench press, 13.9% against 8.6%, while the free-weight bench press rose in both groups with no difference between them.6

So for building the chest, the studies that measured growth found no clear gap, though ACSM calls that evidence thin. For getting better at the bench press itself, bench. Many weeks use both: the bench press first, then the machine for more chest work without another heavy bar.

When a beginner starts here

The chest press machine is one of the three pushes in this site's beginner plan, with push-ups and a dumbbell press (how to start lifting weights). It suits a beginner for three plain reasons: the path is fixed, so the only job is pushing; you can train alone, with no bar to get stuck under; and the pin moves the weight in small, exact steps.

Beginners get stronger on it. In 20 healthy women who hadn't been exercising, eight weeks on a chest press machine raised their one-rep max whether the machine's arms moved on a fixed path or more freely; the freer machine raised it more.7 The authors' explanation was coordination: after training on the freer machine, the muscles that steady the shoulder had adapted too, which they hadn't on the fixed one.

When to add a bench press: when you want to get better at the bench press itself, or when the machine's stack runs out. The machine stays useful after that, as a second press.

Machine, cable, dumbbells or push-ups

  • A cable chest press, one arm or two. In 15 adults, nine men and six women, a cable chest press drew more activity from the biceps, the front of the shoulder and the obliques than a standard plate-stack machine press.8 The one-arm version is in the cable workout.
  • Dumbbells on a flat bench, each arm on its own. In the 12-man study, the dumbbell max was 17% lighter than the barbell and 14% lighter than the Smith machine.2
  • Push-ups at home, with your hands on a bench if the floor is too hard: how to do push-ups.
  • An incline press for the upper chest, which a flat chest press machine reaches least: the incline bench press.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • The seat at the wrong height. Handles at your neck or your belly. Reset the seat until they line up with the middle of your chest.
  • Shoulders rolling forward at the end of the press. Keep your shoulder blades on the pad, and stop when your arms are straight rather than reaching further.
  • Lifting your hips or arching hard to push. Your hips stay on the seat; if they won't, the weight is too heavy.
  • Letting the stack crash between reps. Lower in about two seconds and start the next rep just before the plates touch.
  • Half reps. Bring the handles all the way back to chest level, where the stretch is.
  • Pressing through something that hurts. Sore the next day is normal; a sharp or lasting hurt in the shoulder or chest isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • As the main press, three sets of 8 to 12; as a second press after the bench press, two. The chest workout uses it both ways.
  • Close to failure is fine. Machines are a good place to stop with zero or one rep left, since there's no bar to get stuck under. (How to judge reps left.) The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, which covers healthy adults, advises at least two sets per exercise.5
  • Pair it with a pull. One set of the chest press, then one set of the rear delt fly or the seated cable row, then rest. A tricep pushdown can finish the session.
  • Add a rep, then a pin. When every set reaches the top of the range, move the pin down one plate. (Progressive overload covers how.)
