Dumbbell vs barbell: what each does better, lift by lift

A barbell usually lets you lift more. Dumbbells let each arm or leg work on its own. Past that, the answer changes from lift to lift. Here's what the studies compared for each lift people ask about, what they measured, and which lifts have no head-to-head study at all.

Short answer: A barbell usually lets you lift more weight; dumbbells let each side work on its own and make one-arm and one-leg versions easy. A 2025 meta-analysis found one-limb and two-limb training built similar muscle, while strength rose most in the version trained.10 So choose lift by lift, by what you most want to get strong at.

"Dumbbell or barbell?" has a different answer for each lift, which is why it gets asked one lift at a time. Two things hold across all of them: a barbell usually lets you lift more weight, and dumbbells let each arm or leg work on its own. The rest depends on the lift and on what you want to get strong at.

This page goes lift by lift: what the studies compared, what they measured, and which lifts have no head-to-head study. Whether dumbbells alone are enough to train with is a separate question, answered in the three-day dumbbell plan. The bench press's loads, and how to bench without a spotter, are on bench press form.

Muscle activity, measured with electrodes on the skin, shows which muscles are working during a set. It doesn't show how much they'll grow over months, so read most of this table as tendencies.

Lift by lift

Bench press

Bench press form has the loads and the basic activity comparison. Three more studies add to it:

  • Over several sets. 19 trained men did four sets at a ten-rep max with each tool. They got more total reps with dumbbells than with the bar, about 31 against 28. The dumbbell press produced more chest and biceps activity, the barbell more triceps activity.1
  • Recovery. In 27 trained men, eight sets at a ten-rep max with a barbell, dumbbells or a Smith machine left strength and muscle thickness recovering on the same timeline over four days. Only the barbell and Smith machine groups reported sore triceps.2
  • Ten weeks of training. 36 trained men were split into three groups that pressed twice a week for ten weeks, with dumbbells, a Smith machine or on an exercise ball, and were tested on all three and on the barbell bench. The dumbbell group improved more at the dumbbell press than at the presses it didn't train, a pattern that showed up early and lasted.3

Incline bench press

One study tested both tools at more than one bench angle: 20 lifters with at least a year of training, 10 men and 10 women, pressed with dumbbells and a barbell on an incline, a flat and a decline bench, at 70% of their flat-bench max. Every difference it reported came with a change of angle: the front of the shoulder, for one, worked harder on the incline barbell press than on either decline press.4 So on an incline, the angle is the bigger decision; the chest workout covers it. Dumbbells or a bar is your call.

Shoulder press and overhead press

  • In 15 healthy men tested at 80% of their max, seated and standing, dumbbells produced more front-deltoid activity than the bar in both positions. The barbell drew more on the triceps, about 39% more standing, and on the biceps, about 33% more seated. Standing, the dumbbell max was about 7% below the barbell max.5
  • In 30 resistance-trained people pressing seated, the load they could lift fell as the setup got less stable, from a barbell on a bench to dumbbells on an exercise ball, and triceps activity was highest with the barbell on a bench.6

So the bar moves more weight and brings in more triceps, while dumbbells get more front-shoulder work out of a lighter load. Which press to build a shoulder day on is on the shoulder workout.

Row

There's no head-to-head study of a dumbbell row and a barbell row for the back muscles they're done for. What separates them in practice is how you're held in place. A one-arm dumbbell row braces one hand and one knee on a bench; a barbell row means you hold yourself bent over from the first rep to the last. That's why the back workout calls the one-arm row the home row and says to learn the hinge before the barbell row.

Romanian deadlift

No head-to-head study here either. It's the same hinge with either tool, and deadlift form covers it. A barbell is easier to load heavy, since it doesn't depend on how heavy a pair of dumbbells goes. Dumbbells make the single-leg version simple, and one-leg training builds one-leg strength most (the specificity section below).

Curl

In 12 trained men with at least three years of lifting, each doing one set of ten at 65% of their max on each curl, an EZ bar (the bent bar) produced more biceps and brachioradialis activity than dumbbells, and a straight bar beat dumbbells only in parts of the rep. The authors called the choice between the straight bar and the EZ bar a matter of comfort.7 That's one session of activity, not growth. For which curl to train and how many sets, see the arm workout.

Shrug

No head-to-head study. Dumbbells hang at your sides and a barbell sits in front of your thighs, but the movement is the same: shoulders straight up toward your ears, a short pause, then down under control. Use whichever your grip can hold for clean sets.

Lunge

In 19 recreationally active men, thigh and lower-leg muscle activity in the lunge didn't differ between a barbell on the back, dumbbells in the hands and a kettlebell. The same study found the front and back of the thigh more active in the lunge than in the squat.8 Carry the load whichever way you can balance; the dumbbell version of the leg day uses a Bulgarian split squat.

Across the lifts

  • The bar is heavier. Both shoulder-press studies found the barbell version moved more, and bench press form shows the same for the bench. A bar also goes up by the smallest plates in the gym, while dumbbells jump by a fixed step per hand, which matters on the upper-body lifts where progressive overload asks for the smaller jumps.
  • Dumbbells work each side alone. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 position stand includes both two-limb and one-limb exercises in its recommendations for strength programs.9 Dumbbells make the one-limb versions easy: a one-arm press, a single-leg Romanian deadlift, a split squat.

You get strongest at the version you train

A 2025 meta-analysis of nine studies compared training one limb at a time with training both together. Muscle growth didn't differ. Two-limb training raised two-limb strength more, and one-limb training raised one-limb strength more; the authors' reading is that strength gains follow the principle of specificity.10 The ten-week bench study above found the same with tools: the dumbbell group improved most at the dumbbell press.3 So:

  • If a barbell number matters to you, train that lift with the barbell, and use dumbbells for the second exercise of the day.
  • If you train at home with dumbbells, your dumbbell lifts are the ones that will move most, and those are the numbers to track.
  • Pick one version for each slot and keep it for a block of weeks, so the logbook compares like with like.

What these studies didn't test

Most of the comparisons on this page are single sessions of muscle activity in small groups, mostly trained men. None of them ran a dumbbell and a barbell version of the same lift for months and measured muscle size; the closest is the meta-analysis of one-limb against two-limb training, which found no difference in growth. For the row, the Romanian deadlift and the shrug, there's no head-to-head study of the main muscles to report. So use the table as a starting point, and let the version you can keep adding weight or reps to decide.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing the numbers directly. Two dumbbells usually add up to less than the bar on the same lift. Log each version on its own.
  • Switching tools every week. Strength follows the version you train, so constant switching hides your progress.
  • Dumbbells too heavy to get into position. If getting them onto your chest or up to your shoulders costs you the first rep, go lighter or use the bar.
  • Lifting through pain. Stop if a lift brings sharp pain, not just soreness, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
