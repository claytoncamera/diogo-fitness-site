Periodization: what it means in training, the main models, and what the research found

Periodization is planning how your training changes across a program instead of repeating the same week. Here's what the words mean, the three main models, what the research found about them, where linear progression and cluster sets fit, and how much of it you actually need.

Short answer: Periodization is planning changes to your training, such as load, volume and frequency, across a program divided into blocks called mesocycles. Periodization means planning how your training changes over time, instead of doing the same sets, reps and weights every week. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand describes it as intentionally changing training variables, such as load, volume and frequency, throughout a program.1 One 2010 review calls it systematic progression: training that follows predictable patterns of change.2

This page is about strength training. (Periodization in history, splitting the past into named eras, is the same word for a different idea.) It covers the words, the three main models, what the research found, where linear progression and cluster sets fit, and how much of it you need. The week-to-week mechanics of adding weight or reps are on progressive overload.

Macrocycle, mesocycle, microcycle

Periodized plans are described in three nested sizes. In one 2010 review, a 12-month plan was the macrocycle, its subdivisions of 3 to 4 months the mesocycles, and units of 1 to 4 weeks the microcycles.2 Other writers use shorter ones: in the 10-week trial below, each mesocycle lasted weeks, not months.3 The 2026 ACSM stand notes that definitions of periodization vary, and that periodization and programming are often conflated.1 So read the words as sizes, big, middle and small, rather than fixed numbers of weeks.

The mesocycle: the block you actually train in

For planning, the mesocycle is the useful unit: a block of weeks with one main focus, ending in a change. One trial shows what that can look like. In 22 resistance-trained men training for 10 weeks, a block group spent each mesocycle on one quality, power, maximal strength or muscle growth, while a mixed group trained all three in every session. With volume equal, the mixed group gained more muscle thickness and more bench press strength, 8.6% against 2%, and the block group more vertical jump, 7.2% against 1.2%.3

A practical shape on this site is a few weeks of building, then a lighter week before the next block. That lighter week is a deload week, and its page covers what the evidence does and doesn't say about it.

The three main models: linear, undulating and block

ModelWhat changesHow often
Linear (also called classic)Volume and load, from more volume and lighter loads toward less volume and heavier loadsAcross several mesocycles
Undulating (also called nonlinear)Volume and load, up and downDaily, weekly or every two weeks
BlockThe focus: one quality per block, such as muscle growth, maximal strength or powerEach mesocycle

The 2010 review describes the classic, or linear, model as changing volume and load across several predictable mesocycles, and the nonlinear model as changing them more often, daily, weekly or every two weeks; its reverse linear model runs the other way, raising volume and lowering load.2 The block model is the one in the 10-week trial above.3

What the research found

  • Periodized or not, with volume matched. A 2022 meta-analysis of 35 studies found a small edge in one-rep-max strength for periodized programs and no difference in muscle growth. Undulating plans built more strength than linear ones in trained participants, but not in untrained ones.4
  • Linear or undulating, for strength. A 2015 meta-analysis of 17 studies and 510 participants found no difference between the two for upper- or lower-body strength. The authors noted that the studies were short.5
  • Linear or undulating, for growth. A 2017 meta-analysis of 13 studies found the two likely to build similar muscle.6
  • The 2026 position stand. The ACSM couldn't determine periodization's effect on strength, noting that one review slightly favoured it, and found muscle growth wasn't affected. Its conclusion for healthy adults: with appropriate progressive overload, periodization is not significantly better than a non-periodized program, and less important than once thought.1

Read together: steady progressive overload does most of the work. Planned variation may add a little strength, more likely in trained lifters than in beginners, and in these studies it didn't change muscle growth. Which model you pick matters less than whether you keep progressing.

Linear progression

Linear progression is a simpler idea that shares a word: add a small amount of weight every session or every week, as long as you keep making your reps. It's a way to progress, not a periodization model. It runs until the jumps stop coming; then you switch to a slower method, such as the double progression on progressive overload, or to planned blocks.

Don't confuse it with linear periodization, the model in the table above, which shifts volume and load across blocks of weeks.

Cluster sets

A cluster set breaks a set into smaller pieces with short planned rests inside it, between single reps or small groups of reps. The research splits into one session and many weeks:

  • In one session, faster reps. A 2019 meta-analysis of 25 studies found that cluster sets held bar speed, power and peak force up better than traditional sets during a session.7
  • Over weeks, no difference. A 2021 meta-analysis of 29 studies found no difference between cluster and traditional sets in gains in strength, power, speed, muscle growth or endurance.8 The 2026 ACSM stand likewise found strength wasn't affected by cluster set structures.1

So cluster sets are a tool for keeping heavy or fast reps sharp within a session, not a shortcut to more strength or muscle.

How much periodization do you need?

  • Newer to lifting: steady progressive overload on a simple plan. In the 2022 meta-analysis, undulating plans were no better than linear ones for untrained participants.4 A plan from the workout plans is enough structure.
  • Once you're trained: planned blocks or undulating weeks may add a little strength, and they give you a reason to change exercises and rep ranges on a schedule rather than on a whim.
  • Either way, think in blocks. A block of a few weeks is a natural unit to review: what went up, what stalled, what to change next, and when to take a lighter week.

If you'd rather not plan it alone

Periodization is easy to describe and hard to apply to a week with work, sleep and missed sessions in it.
