Time under tension: what tempo does, and what it doesn't

Slow reps feel harder, so it's easy to believe they build more. The trials say otherwise: across a wide range of speeds, hard sets build similar muscle. Here's what tempo means, what the evidence shows and doesn't, how to use negatives, and when a slower rep is worth it.

Short answer: Time under tension is how long a set keeps a muscle working; tempo is the speed of each rep. In the trials, reps lasting from half a second to eight seconds built similar muscle when sets were taken to failure, and very slow reps looked worse. Lower under control, lift with intent, and judge sets by reps left.

Time under tension (TUT) is how long a muscle works during a set: the number of reps times how long each one takes. Tempo is how you split each rep, how fast you lower, pause and lift. Both get sold as a growth lever: slow it down, keep the muscle working longer, grow more.

This page covers what tempo notation means, what the research shows for muscle growth and strength, eccentrics and negatives as a tool, and the cases where a slower rep earns its time. How hard a set should be is on reps in reserve.

What the tempo numbers mean

Programs often write tempo as four numbers, in seconds: the lowering, the pause at the bottom, the lift, and the pause at the top. A squat at 3-1-1-0 is three seconds down, one second still at the bottom, one second up, and straight into the next rep. Ten reps at that tempo is a 50-second set; ten reps at 1-0-1-0 is about 20 seconds. The lowering is the eccentric part of the rep, the lifting the concentric part.

What the evidence shows for muscle growth

  • A wide range works. A 2015 meta-analysis of eight studies in which every set went to failure found similar muscle growth with reps lasting anywhere from half a second to eight seconds. Very slow reps, over ten seconds each, looked worse, though few controlled studies tested them.1
  • Slower wasn't better. A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 studies in healthy adults compared slower reps (about 3.5 seconds on average) with faster ones (about one second), with everything else in the training matched. Growth was similar; the estimates trivially favored the faster reps. Its conclusion: tempo appears to have minimal overall effect on muscle growth.2
  • Same time, same result. In 38 untrained men, ten weeks of bench pressing with either 12 reps of three seconds or six reps of six seconds, both 36 seconds a set at the same load, built similar chest and triceps muscle and similar strength.3
  • The 2026 overview agrees. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand lists time under tension among the variables that didn't consistently change training outcomes, muscle growth included.4

What it shows for strength

Less is settled here. A 2025 meta-analysis of nine studies with 166 participants, about half of them trained, compared longer and shorter lowering phases. For strength and for muscle growth the estimates were uncertain. In trained participants, and in trials that matched the total work, the longer lowering phase gave similar or larger strength gains; the shorter one improved jump height.5 So a slower lowering is a reasonable choice for strength work, not a requirement.

What the evidence doesn't show

  • No best set length. Once sets are hard, the trials above found no rep speed or set duration that builds more muscle, so a target such as "40 seconds a set" isn't something they support.
  • Slow isn't a substitute for load or effort. A slow set with a weight you could lift twenty times, stopped at ten, is still an easy set. What makes a set work is how close it ends to failure.
  • The research is thin. The reviews above pooled eight, nine and fourteen studies.

Eccentrics and negatives

The eccentric part of a rep, the lowering, is where a muscle can handle the most load; a 2009 meta-analysis of eccentric training ties its advantages to the higher loads developed while lowering.6 That's the case for negatives, reps where you only do the lowering.

  • Lowering-only against lifting-only training. A 2017 meta-analysis of 15 studies found eccentric training's average muscle growth higher than concentric training's, 10.0% against 6.8%, but the difference wasn't statistically significant, and the authors concluded both are effective.7 A larger 2025 meta-analysis of 26 studies with 682 people found no difference, with very low certainty in the evidence.8
  • Heavier lowering. That 2009 meta-analysis of 20 trials found that eccentric training at higher intensities raised strength more than concentric training, but the gains were more specific to the kind of movement trained.6 The ACSM's 2026 stand found muscle growth was enhanced by eccentric overload, lowering more than you lift.4

In practice, normal reps already include the lowering, so negatives are a tool for two cases. One: a movement you can't do yet, such as a pull-up or a full push-up, where a slow lowering from the top builds toward the full rep. (How to get your first pull-up.) Two: an extra hard set at the end of a lift, with a partner or a machine to help lift the weight you then lower alone. Expect more soreness the first few times; muscle soreness explains why it fades.

When a slower tempo helps

  • Learning a lift. A two- or three-second lowering gives you time to feel where your knees, hips and back are.
  • When the weights are light. At home, with dumbbells or bands, slowing the lowering makes a set hard in fewer reps; it doesn't add growth, it saves reps. (When the dumbbells run out.)
  • Where momentum takes over. Curls, raises and pushdowns turn into swings when they're rushed.
  • Staying in a range that feels good. A controlled rep lets you stop short of a position that feels wrong rather than bouncing into it.

A default to use

Lower in about two or three seconds without bouncing, pause briefly if it helps you stay in position, and lift with intent: as fast as you can while the rep stays clean, even if the weight moves slowly. Count reps, not seconds, and judge each set by how many reps you had left. Progress the usual way, with reps and then weight; progressive overload covers how.

Common mistakes

  • Slowing down instead of adding weight once a weight is easy. Add the load.
  • Very slow reps on every set. Over ten seconds a rep, the evidence looks worse, and the sessions get long.
  • Dropping the weight on the way down. Half the rep is the lowering; control it.
  • Heavy negatives alone. Lowering more than you can lift needs a partner or a machine.
  • Pushing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Common questions

Is time under tension a myth?

As a target, the evidence doesn't support it: in the trials on this page, once sets were hard, no rep speed or set duration built more muscle, and the ACSM's 2026 stand lists time under tension among the variables that didn't consistently change outcomes.4 For strength, a slower lowering is a reasonable choice, not a requirement.
