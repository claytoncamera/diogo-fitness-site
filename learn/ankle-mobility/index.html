Ankle mobility for squats: the knee-to-wall test and what to do about it

In a squat, your knees travel forward over your feet, and how far they can go with your heels down is ankle range. Here's why it matters for squat depth, how to test it against a wall, the drills and stretches with some evidence behind them, raised heels as a workaround, and what the research does and doesn't show.

Short answer: Test it first: foot a few inches from a wall, push your knee toward the wall with your heel down, and measure the farthest spot that still works. In 20 healthy adults, that range was the only measure linked with how deep they squatted.1 Calf stretching adds a few degrees; raised heels are the quick workaround.

Ankle mobility, for a lifter, mostly means one movement: dorsiflexion, the shin tilting forward over the foot with the heel on the floor. Every squat needs some. As you sit down, your knees travel forward past your toes; if your ankles run out of range first, something else gives: the usual signs are heels lifting, feet turning out or the chest dropping to keep you balanced. Strengthening the muscle that lifts the foot is a different job: tibialis raises.

This page covers why ankle range matters for squat depth, the knee-to-wall test, the drills and stretches, raised heels as a workaround, and what the evidence does and doesn't show. It's about training range in a healthy ankle. The full ten-minute routine for ankles, hips, upper back and shoulders is the mobility routine, and how deep to squat is on squat form.

Why ankle range matters for the squat

In 20 healthy adults, researchers measured back squat depth alongside ankle range in two tests, the ankles' resistance to stretch and Achilles tendon stiffness. Only one measure was linked with how deep people squatted: ankle range in the standing lunge test, with the knee bent.1 Those with low and high squat depth differed on that measure and no other.1 It's one small study and an association, not proof that more ankle range makes a deeper squat, but it points at the right test.

Not every limit is the ankle, or even flexibility. A 2026 paper on range of motion notes that the shape of the bones at a joint can set the limit, and gives a lifter's example: a deep squat stops when the thigh meets the lower leg, even if the person has the flexibility to go deeper.2 The test below tells you whether the ankle is part of your answer. The other usual limit is the hip: hip mobility has its checks.

The knee-to-wall test

  1. Barefoot, face a wall with the foot you're testing in front, big toe a few inches from the wall, the other foot behind you for balance.
  2. Keep the heel down and push your knee forward toward the wall, in line with your middle toes.
  3. If the knee touches with the heel still down, move the foot back a little and try again. If it doesn't, move it closer.
  4. Your score is the farthest distance from big toe to wall at which the knee still touches without the heel lifting. Measure it with a tape, and test both sides.

It's a good test. In 13 healthy people, both the toe-to-wall distance and the angle of the shin gave excellent reliability, between raters and when two of them repeated it a week later.3 A 2015 review of 12 reliability studies agreed, and found that a change of about 1.6 to 1.9 centimeters is needed to be outside the test's own error.4 So retest every few weeks, not every day, and don't read much into a few millimeters.

What the test doesn't give you is a pass mark for lifting. Use it two ways: compare your sides, and compare yourself with yourself a month from now.

Drills and stretches

  • Knee-to-wall rocks. The test position, done as a drill: knee toward the wall and back, slowly, ten times a side.
  • Banded ankle rocks. A band looped low around the front of your ankle, anchored behind you, while you rock the knee forward.
  • Straight-knee calf stretch. Hands on a wall, back leg straight, heel down, until you feel the calf.
  • Bent-knee calf stretch. The same, with the back knee bent. It shifts the stretch lower in the calf and matches the bent-knee test position.
  • Foam rolling the calves. A minute or so a side, before the rocks. The foam rolling page covers the rest of the body.

What the evidence says about these:

  • Calf stretching helps, a little. A 2006 review of five trials found static calf stretching added about 2 to 3 degrees of dorsiflexion, whether the total stretching time was under 15 minutes or over 30. The authors called the increase small, and said it's unclear whether it matters.5
  • Several methods work. A 2026 network meta-analysis of 41 trials in 1,670 physically active, healthy adults compared static, dynamic and ballistic stretching, PNF stretching (a stretch combined with a muscle contraction) and foam rolling. For passive ankle range, PNF and foam rolling ranked highest, and both beat no intervention over programs longer than a month. For active range, foam rolling gave the biggest short-term gain and static stretching held up best over longer programs.6
  • The banded drill works, briefly. In 26 young adults with limited dorsiflexion, one round of banded self-mobilization, learned from a video, added 6.5 degrees on the lunge test. The gain faded within about 25 minutes, and it didn't change how far the ankle bent during a step-down.7 So if you use it, use it right before you squat.
  • Squats count too. A 2023 meta-analysis of 55 studies in healthy people found lifting increased range of motion, and gained about as much as stretching in the studies that compared the two.8 A goblet squat held at the bottom for a few breaths is ankle work with a weight in your hands.

Raised heels: the quick workaround

Put small plates or a wedge under your heels, or wear shoes with a raised heel, and the squat asks less of your ankles. A 2026 meta-analysis of 14 studies, 177 healthy adults in all, found raising the heels increased range of motion at the ankle by about 4 degrees and at the knee by about 5 during the squat, with no change at the hip or trunk. The ankle gain showed up only with higher lifts, above about 2.5 centimeters.9 In 14 recreational lifters, seven men and seven women, squatting at 80% of their max to at least parallel, raised heels didn't change trunk angles or muscle activity.10

So raised heels let your knees travel farther without changing much else. It's a legitimate setup, not a cheat, and you can keep using it while you work on the drills, or for good.

What the evidence does and doesn't show

  • It shows that the knee-to-wall test is reliable, that ankle range on it tracked squat depth in one small study, that stretching, foam rolling and lifting all add range, and that raised heels open up the knee and ankle in the squat.
  • It doesn't show that gaining range on the test makes your squat deeper: none of the studies above tested that in a squat. In the banded-drill study, extra range on the test didn't carry over to a step-down,7 and most of the samples were small.
  • It doesn't set a target. No study above gives a knee-to-wall distance a lifter needs.

And this is training advice for a healthy ankle. If an ankle hurts, or you're coming back from an ankle problem, a doctor or a physical therapist is the right first stop.

A ten-minute ankle routine, and where it goes

DrillDoseWhen
Foam roll the calvesAbout a minute a sideBefore squats, or any time
Knee-to-wall rocks2 × 10 slow reps a sideBefore squats
Banded ankle rocks1 × 10 a sideBefore squats
Goblet squat hold2 holds of 30 secondsBefore squats, as a warm-up set
Straight-knee calf stretch2 holds of 30 seconds a sideAfter lifting, or on a rest day
Bent-knee calf stretch2 holds of 30 seconds a sideAfter lifting, or on a rest day

The rocks move the joint and the squat hold is short, so both fit into the warm-up. The long holds go after the session or on another day, for the reasons in the warm-up guide's stretching section. On a leg day, that's rocks before the squats, calf stretches at the end, and the hamstring workout's hinge and curl in between. Retest the knee-to-wall distance every four weeks.
