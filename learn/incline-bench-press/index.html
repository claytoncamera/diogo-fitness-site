Incline bench press: the angle, the setup and how it differs from flat

Raise the back of the bench and the press moves up your chest, until the front of the shoulder starts taking over. Here's what the studies say about the angle, the setup for a barbell and for dumbbells, the faults, how it compares with the flat bench, and the machine versions.

Short answer: Set the bench to about 30 degrees, the angle where the upper chest was most active in a study of 30 trained adults.1 Shoulder blades back and down, feet flat, grip a little wider than your shoulders. Lower the bar or dumbbells to your upper chest, just below the collarbones, and press back up over your shoulders.

The incline bench press is a bench press with the back of the bench raised, so you press up and slightly back rather than straight up from your chest. The change in angle moves the work toward the upper chest, the clavicular head of the pectoralis major, and toward the front of the shoulder. How far it moves depends on the angle, which is what most of the research is about.

This page covers the angle, the setup and the rep with a barbell and with dumbbells, the faults, how the incline compares with the flat bench, and the machine versions. The flat bench and its full setup are on bench press form; where the incline sits in a week of chest work is on the chest workout.

What angle? The evidence

Five studies measured muscle activity across bench angles. They agree on the direction, less on the exact number:

  • 30 degrees came out on top. In 30 trained adults, each with at least a year of lifting three or more times a week, pressing at 0, 15, 30, 45 and 60 degrees at 60% of their max for each angle, the upper chest was most active at 30 degrees. The middle and lower chest were most active flat, and the front of the shoulder most active at 60 degrees. Above 30 degrees, the front of the shoulder did more and all three parts of the chest did less.1
  • Steeper was higher in one study. In 15 healthy men at 70% of their max, upper-chest activity was greater at 44 degrees than flat and than at 28 degrees, while the lower chest was most active flat. The front of the shoulder worked harder at every incline than flat. The authors' conclusion: both the flat and the incline press are needed to cover the chest.2
  • The upper chest's share moved up at 45 degrees. In eight volunteers measured with a grid of sensors across the chest, the peak of activity shifted toward the upper chest when the bench went from flat to 45 degrees.3 In 10 competitive bodybuilders lifting 80% of their max, the upper chest was more active on the way up in a 45-degree incline press than flat, and the middle and lower chest more active flat.4
  • A smaller difference in trained men. In 14 resistance-trained men at 65% of their max, upper-chest activity over the whole press didn't differ between flat, 30 and 45 degrees. Both inclines were higher only in one part of the press, and the lower chest was least active at 45.5

Read together: raising the bench moves the press up the chest, the studies disagree on whether 30 or 45 degrees does it best, and they agree that the front of the shoulder works harder as the bench gets steeper. Start at 30 degrees, the setting the chest workout uses. If the press starts to feel like your shoulders rather than your chest, the bench is too steep. All of these measured activity in a single session, not growth.

Does it build the upper chest?

One training trial has tested it. 47 untrained young men trained once a week for eight weeks on the flat bench, the incline bench or both, with the same number of sets in each group. Strength rose by about the same in all three. Muscle thickness changed differently at only one of three sites, high on the chest, where the incline group gained the most.6 That's one small trial in beginners, so it points the same way as the activity studies without settling it.

How to set up: barbell

  1. Set the angle to about 30 degrees, and the seat so your hips stay on it as you press.
  2. Eyes under the bar. Sit back so the bar in the rack is roughly over your eyes or forehead.
  3. Feet flat and wide enough to keep you from sliding down the bench.
  4. Shoulder blades back and down, set before you unrack, as on the flat bench.
  5. Grip a little wider than your shoulders, thumbs around the bar, wrists stacked over your elbows.
  6. Unrack with straight arms and bring the bar over your shoulders before the first rep.

The rep: lower the bar under control to your upper chest, just below your collarbones, elbows travelling a little toward your sides rather than straight out. Touch lightly, then press up and slightly back so the bar finishes over your shoulders. Upper back, hips and head stay on the bench.

Incline dumbbell press

Sit on the inclined bench with a dumbbell standing on each thigh. Lie back and use your knees to help bring the weights to your shoulders, then press them up over your upper chest, palms facing forward or angled slightly in. Lower until the dumbbells are beside your upper chest, then press back up.

In 12 trained and 12 novice women, the incline dumbbell press worked the front of the shoulder more than the flat dumbbell press and less than an overhead press. The upper chest was more active in the incline's lowering phase than in either of the others, though the flat press led on the way up.7 On the flat bench, dumbbells were about 17% lighter than a barbell in 12 trained men, with the same chest activity,8 so expect the incline dumbbells to be lighter too, and choose them when you want each arm to work on its own or have no rack.

Incline vs flat bench press

  • The incline is lighter. In 12 national and international bench press athletes, the six-rep max was 18.5% to 21.5% lower on the incline than flat or declined. Chest activity didn't differ significantly between the three; the incline used less triceps and more biceps.9
  • The flat bench covers the middle and lower chest, which were most active flat in the angle studies above, and it moves the most weight.
  • Strength carried over either way in the beginners' trial: the flat, incline and combined groups gained about the same on both lifts.6

So it's not one or the other. A flat press as the main chest lift and an incline as the second is the pattern on the chest workout, which puts the incline on its second chest day. Setup for the flat version is on bench press form.

The machine and Smith versions

An incline chest-press machine or a Smith machine, with the bar on fixed rails, runs the same pattern with less balancing, so the same angle and the same bar path apply. One caution from the data: in the bodybuilders' study, a chest press machine produced less upper-chest activity than any of the free-weight presses,4 so a standard chest press machine isn't a substitute for the incline. On the flat bench, a Smith machine moved about 3% less than a barbell with the same chest activity.8 Use the version your gym has, set near 30 degrees.

Common mistakes

  • A bench set too steep. The steeper it gets, the more the front of the shoulder takes over. Keep it near 30.
  • Hips sliding or lifting. Set the seat and plant your feet so you stay put.
  • Lowering to the middle of the chest. On an incline, the bar touches higher, just below the collarbones.
  • Elbows flared straight out. Let them travel a little toward your sides.
  • Chasing the flat-bench weight. Expect the incline to be lighter, and progress it on its own numbers.
  • Pressing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

Three sets of 6 to 12, stopping with two or three reps in reserve, as the second press in a chest day or the first on a second chest day. (How to judge reps left.) The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found muscle growth was enhanced by ten or more sets per muscle group a week,10 so count incline sets toward the chest total; the chest workout's week of ten includes three of them.
