HIIT vs steady-state cardio: what the trials found for fat loss, fitness and sticking with it

Intervals are short and hard; steady state is longer and easier. The argument about which one is better has more research behind it than most gym arguments, and the answer is calmer than either camp. Here's what each one is, what the trials found for body fat and for fitness, which one people actually keep doing, and how to run a week with both.

Short answer: Both work. In 31 studies, interval training and steady cardio reduced body fat by similar, modest amounts.7 In 28 studies of healthy adults, both raised aerobic fitness, intervals by a possibly small margin more.3 Intervals save time and feel harder; steady cardio is easier to keep up. Pick the one you'll do.

HIIT is high-intensity interval training: short efforts hard enough that you couldn't keep them going, with easier recovery between them. Steady-state cardio is one continuous effort at a pace you can hold: a run, a ride, a row, a brisk walk. Both get called "cardio," and both get sold as the one true way to do it.

This page covers what each one is, what the trials found for body fat, what they found for aerobic fitness and endurance, which one people keep doing, and how to run a week with both beside lifting. It's about what the research measured, not a promise about the scale. The steady end of the scale, zone 2 and the talk test, is on zone 2 cardio; where any cardio goes next to lifting is on cardio and lifting.

What each one is

The research splits interval work in two. A 2017 review describes high-intensity interval training as repeated near-maximal efforts, and sprint interval training as supramaximal ones, all-out bursts harder than the pace you could hold at your maximal oxygen uptake; both are repeated bouts of relatively intense exercise with short recovery periods between them.1 Moderate-intensity continuous training is the research name for steady-state cardio, and a 2021 paper on what "moderate" means puts its upper limit at the first threshold, the intensity where your breathing starts to climb faster than your oxygen use.2

For aerobic fitness and endurance

This is where intervals have their clearest case, and it's still a narrow one.

  • Both improve fitness a lot; intervals a little more. A 2015 meta-analysis pooled 28 controlled trials of 723 healthy adults, average age 25, lasting two weeks or longer. Against doing nothing, steady endurance training raised maximal oxygen uptake by about 4.9 mL/kg/min and interval training by about 5.5. Compared head to head, intervals came out ahead by about 1.2 mL/kg/min, which the authors rated a possibly small benefit.3
  • Sprints match steady training on less time. A 2014 meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials, 318 people with an average age of about 23, found all-out sprint intervals raised aerobic capacity by about 3.6 mL/kg/min, around 8%, and the effect was not different from the endurance-training groups it was compared with, on a reduced volume of exercise.4
  • The interval length matters. A 2019 meta-analysis of 53 randomized trials in adults found that short intervals of 30 seconds or less, low-volume sessions of 5 minutes or less and programs of 4 weeks or less all improved maximal oxygen uptake compared with doing nothing, but only longer intervals of 2 minutes or more, sessions of 15 minutes or more and programs of 4 weeks or more beat steady training.5
  • Low-volume intervals still improve fitness. In a 2019 meta-analysis of 47 studies of adults, interval programs with a small weekly dose improved cardiorespiratory fitness more than both doing nothing and steady training.6

Read together: if your goal is fitness and you have 15 minutes, intervals buy more per minute. If you have 45, a steady session gets most of the way there, and the gap between the two is small.

For fat loss and weight loss

Here the two come out close to even, and neither is a big lever on its own.

  • Similar, modest changes in body fat. A 2017 meta-analysis of 31 studies, each at least four weeks long, found body fat percentage fell by about 1.3 points with intervals and 1.5 with steady training, and fat mass by about 1.4 and 0.9 kg, with no significant difference between the two for any body-fat measure. Comparisons where the interval program took less time, or used less energy, tended to favour steady training. The authors' summary was that neither produced clinically meaningful reductions in body fat over the short term.7
  • A bigger pooled analysis leans to intervals, a little. A 2024 umbrella review pooled 16 systematic reviews covering 79 randomized trials and 2,474 apparently healthy adults. Interval training reduced body fat percentage by about 0.8 points more than steady training, with larger differences in people with overweight or obesity, in programs of 12 weeks or more, on bikes, and with low-volume intervals. Most of the reviews it pooled scored low or critically low on quality.8
  • Short interval sessions didn't move fat. The 2019 meta-analysis of 47 studies found no difference between low-volume intervals and either doing nothing or steady training for fat mass or body fat percentage, even though fitness improved.6

So the honest version: both kinds of cardio were linked with small drops in body fat in these trials, the difference between them is under a percentage point either way, and a short interval session is a fitness tool more than a fat-loss one. Cardio of either kind sits beside what you eat, not in place of it; walking and steps and why you can't lose fat cover that side, and fat loss coaching is the version where someone builds the training and the daily movement into your week.

Which one people keep doing

The best cardio is the kind you do next month, so this question matters more than the decimals above.

  • Intervals feel worse during, and get rated more enjoyable after. A 2021 meta-analysis of 33 studies found people felt less positive during high-intensity intervals than during steady moderate exercise, at the lowest point and after the session as well, though not at the end of the work, and yet rated the interval session as more enjoyable afterwards, though the authors note the findings are clouded by methodological issues.9
  • Attendance is about the same. A 2023 meta-analysis of 188 studies and 8,928 adults who were insufficiently active or had a medical condition found attendance at supervised interval sessions averaged 89% and at steady sessions 93%, with no difference between the two when compared directly. Unsupervised adherence averaged 63% for intervals and 68% for steady training.10

In practice: if the thought of the hard efforts keeps you off the bike, do steady. If 45 minutes at one pace is what you can't face, do intervals. Many people settle on mostly steady sessions with one harder one a week, which is also what the guideline below allows for.

A week with both

The World Health Organization's guideline for adults is 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent mix, plus muscle-strengthening work on two or more days.11 Steady sessions count as moderate minutes; interval sessions count as vigorous, and the guideline's two ranges make a vigorous minute worth two moderate ones. Both columns land a little under the WHO floor once the interval minutes are counted as vigorous; a brisk walk or two closes the gap. Keep the hard session away from a heavy leg day; cardio and lifting covers how far apart, and why explosive strength is the quality most affected when the two share a session.

A first session of each

  • Steady: 30 minutes on a bike, rower, treadmill or outdoors at a pace where you could still say a sentence or two out loud. The talk test and heart-rate ranges set the pace.
  • Intervals: 5 minutes easy, then 4 rounds of 3 minutes hard and 3 minutes easy, then 5 minutes easy, on a bike, a rower or a stair climber. "Hard" means you could manage a few words, not a sentence. Add a round every week or two until you're at 6, then shorten the rests.
  • Sprints, once the above is routine: 5 minutes easy, then 4 rounds of 20 seconds all-out and 2 minutes easy, then 5 minutes easy. On a bike or rower first; all-out running sprints are a skill of their own.
  • New to cardio altogether? Start with the steady session, twice a week, and add intervals after a month. The WHO's guidelines say some physical activity is better than none.11

Common questions

Is HIIT or steady-state cardio better for fat loss?

Neither by much. In 31 studies, body fat percentage fell by about 1.3 points with intervals and 1.5 with steady training, with no significant difference between them;7 a 2024 pooled analysis of 79 trials put intervals ahead by about 0.8 of a point.8 Pick the one you'll keep doing.

Is HIIT or steady-state cardio better for endurance?

Intervals improved maximal oxygen uptake by about 1.2 mL/kg/min more than steady training in 28 trials of healthy adults, a possibly small difference,3 and only longer intervals in longer programs beat steady training in a 2019 analysis of 53 trials.5 For a long event, you still need long steady sessions to practise the distance.

Can you do HIIT and steady-state cardio in the same week?

Yes, and the WHO guideline is written for a mix: moderate and vigorous minutes combine, with a vigorous minute counting for two.11 One or two interval sessions and one or two steady ones is a common split.
