Sled push: the setup, what it trains, and where it fits beside lifting

Lean into the handles and drive the sled across the floor. A sled push is hard leg work with no bar on your back and no lowering half to the rep. Here's the setup, what the studies say it trains, pushes against pulls, where it goes in a lifting week, and what to do without a sled.

Short answer: Load the sled so you can keep it moving without stopping. Hold the handles with your arms long, lean in until you're in a straight line from head to back foot, and drive with short, quick steps for 10 to 20 meters. Rest until you can push hard again, four to six times, after lifting or on another day.

A sled push is what it sounds like: a weighted sled on a strip of turf or floor, two upright handles, and you drive it forward with your legs. There's no bar on your back and no lowering half to the rep, so it's leg work you can push hard without much technique to learn.

This page covers the setup, what the sled push trains, pushes and pulls, how to fit it next to your lifting, and what to do when there's no sled. How cardio and lifting share a week in general is on cardio and lifting; carries, the other loaded walk, are on the core workout.

How to set up and push

  1. The load. Start with the empty sled, or a plate or two. You want a weight you can keep moving at a steady drive; if the sled stalls halfway, it's too heavy for that distance.
  2. The handle height. The high grip, hands at about chest height, lets you stand more upright. The low grip, hands near hip height, puts you in a longer, lower lean. Start high.
  3. Arms long. Hands on the handles, elbows nearly straight, so your legs move the sled instead of your arms.
  4. The lean. Lean in until your body makes a straight line from your head to your back foot, back flat, eyes on the floor a few feet ahead.
  5. The stride. Drive through the balls of your feet with short, quick steps, each knee pushing forward. Keep driving until you pass the line.

End each push before your position goes. When your hips sag, your back rounds or your steps turn into stumbles, that push is over.

What the sled push trains

Mostly the legs, and their ability to push forward hard. Most of the research is in athletes training to sprint faster, and it reads like this:

  • Thigh activity like a squat. In 10 healthy resistance-trained men, a 20-step maximal push on a weighted sled and a 10-rep-max back squat produced similar activity in the front and back of the thigh and in the deep abdominal muscles. The sled worked the calves about 61% harder, and the squat worked the back muscles along the spine about 75% harder.1
  • Faster first steps. In 50 male high school athletes, eight weeks of sled-push sprints with light, moderate or heavy loads improved split times between 5 and 20 meters, while the same sprints without a sled didn't. The heaviest load brought the biggest gains.2
  • The review. A 2026 meta-analysis of 12 studies, 324 people in all, found that sled training cut sprint times more than sprinting alone, most clearly over the first 10 meters.3
  • Not every time. In 28 physically active women who sprinted once a week for eight weeks, with a sled at 40% of their body mass or without one, the two groups' sprint and jump results didn't differ significantly.4

So the sled trains leg drive and acceleration, and for a lifter it's a hard leg effort with no bar on your back. None of these studies measured muscle growth, and the training trials were in young athletes and active adults, so what a sled adds to a lifting week beyond conditioning is less tested than what a squat adds.

Sled push vs sled pull

Most sleds also take a harness or a strap, so you can pull instead of push:

VersionHowWhat changes
Push, high gripHands at chest height, leaning inThe standard version; easiest to learn
Push, low gripHands near hip height, a longer leanA lower, longer drive
Forward pullA harness or waist strap, running or walking away from the sledUpright, closer to a sprint; your hands are free
Backward dragFacing the sled, straps in your hands, walking backwardShort backward steps
Rope pullStanding still, pulling the sled to you hand over handGrip and arms more than legs

The research on pulls reads like the research on pushes. In 53 male high school athletes, eight weeks of forward sled pulls with loads of about 44%, 89% and 133% of body mass all improved sprint times over 5, 10 and 20 meters, and the heaviest load brought the biggest gains over the first 10 meters.5 Push or pull, then: heavier sleds and short distances. The rope pull holds the rope with the palms facing each other, the grip of a hammer curl, so it trains the arms and grip as much as anything.

How to fit it beside lifting

A hard sled push is conditioning with some strength in it, so the rules in cardio and lifting apply: if strength is your priority, lift first and push after, or push on another day.

  • As a finisher: after your lifting, four to six pushes of 10 to 20 meters, resting until you can push hard again, often a minute or two.
  • As a session of its own: on a day you don't lift legs, six to ten pushes over the same distances.
  • Not before your squats or deadlifts. A hard push leaves your legs tired for the bar.
  • It doesn't linger long. In 27 team-sport athletes, a heavy sled session raised heart rate, blood lactate and effort ratings more than a light one, but sprint and jump performance were back to where they started within 24 hours either way.6
  • With a carry: on a lower-body day, a few pushes and a few farmer's carries make a short finisher, the sled for your legs and the carry for your grip and trunk.

How heavy: heavier sleds made bigger gains over short sprints in the athlete studies, but you aren't training for a 10-meter race. Pick a load you can push the whole distance without stalling, and add a plate when every push feels the same. If you're training for Hyrox or another race with a sled push, the race's own rules set the sled weight for your division, so check them and practice at that weight.

No sled? The alternatives

  • A weight plate on turf, or a towel on a smooth floor with your hands on it, pushed in the same lean. Check the floor and the space first.
  • Short uphill sprints on a hill or a flight of stairs: the same forward drive, without the load.
  • Walking lunges or step-ups with dumbbells: loaded leg drive, one leg at a time.
  • A heavy farmer's carry: the other loaded walk, harder on your grip than on your legs.

None of these is a sled. They cover the same job, hard leg drive in short efforts, with what you have.

Common mistakes

  • Pushing with your arms. Bent elbows turn it into a press. Keep your arms long and let your legs drive.
  • Hips too high. You end up bent over the handles. Drop into the lean so your body is one straight line.
  • Long, slow strides. Short, quick steps keep the sled moving.
  • Too heavy to keep moving. A sled that stops and starts is a different, slower exercise. Take a plate off.
  • No rest between pushes. Count each push as a set and rest until you can push hard again.
  • Pushing through something that hurts. Tired legs are the point; a sharp or lasting hurt isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
