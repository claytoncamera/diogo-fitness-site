Compound exercises: build the week on a few big lifts

A compound exercise moves more than one joint and trains several muscles at once: squats, presses, rows. Here's why they're the base of a plan, where isolation exercises still earn a place, and whether machines count.

Short answer: Compound exercises move more than one joint and work several muscles at once, like squats, deadlifts, presses, rows and pulldowns. Build each session on at least a lower-body exercise, an upper-body push and an upper-body pull,1 do them early, and add isolation exercises for muscles you want more work for.

A squat, a hinge, a press, a row: these are compound exercises. The American College of Sports Medicine's guidelines include both compound and isolation exercises but recommend emphasizing the compound ones, as a 2021 review of time-efficient training notes.1 This page covers what makes an exercise compound, the main movement patterns, how many to include, what the research says about compound versus isolation exercises, which to do first, and whether machines count.

New to lifting? The first four weeks already builds its sessions on a squat, a hinge, a push and a pull.

Compound vs isolation: the difference

The same review puts the difference simply. Single-joint, or isolation, exercises target specific muscles: a biceps curl, a lateral raise, a leg extension. Multi-joint, or compound, exercises work several muscle groups at the same time and let you lift heavier weights: a squat, a bench press, a barbell row.1

A squat bends the hips, knees and ankles and trains the quads, glutes and more in one movement. A leg extension bends only the knee and trains mainly the quads. Both build muscle. The squat trains more of it per set.

The main compound movement patterns

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand didn't compare body regions directly, but its authors' view is that training the upper and lower body with both pushing and pulling exercises, four regions in all, is enough to reach the major muscle groups. For the upper body they add horizontal and vertical directions, which makes six.2 In practice that looks like this:

Lunges and step-ups are compound too: single-leg versions of the squat pattern. Every lift in that table that has its own guide here, and the isolation exercises, are listed by body area and by equipment in the exercise library.

How many compound exercises per workout

Fewer than you might think. The 2021 review says all the major muscle groups can be trained with as few as three: a leg-pressing exercise, an upper-body push and an upper-body pull, preferably two-sided, multi-joint movements.1 That's the minimum for a session that covers the whole body.

A practical full-body session uses three to five compound exercises: a squat or a hinge (or both), a push and a pull, and sometimes a second push or pull in the other direction. Add zero to three isolation exercises after them for the muscles you want more work for. How many sets that adds up to for each muscle is how many sets per week, and how many reps to use on the big lifts versus the small ones is how many reps.

Compound vs isolation: what the research says

The studies are small, mostly in young men, mostly about the arms, and they don't all agree.

  • For strength, compound exercises did more. In an eight-week trial with the total work matched, 36 physically active men who were new to lifting trained with only compound or only isolation exercises. The compound group improved its one-rep max more on the squat, the bench press and even the leg extension.3
  • Adding isolation work to compound lifts added little. In a ten-week trial, 29 untrained young men did either the lat pulldown and bench press alone or the same lifts plus curls and triceps extensions; the muscles at the front of the upper arm grew and got stronger about the same in both groups.4 An eight-week trial in 20 men with at least two years of lifting found the same, and its authors called compound-only training a time-efficient approach.5
  • But isolation exercises can still build a specific muscle. In one ten-week trial in 29 untrained men, lat pulldowns alone and curls alone grew the front of the upper arm by about the same amount, with similar strength gains.6 In another, ten untrained men trained one arm with dumbbell rows and the other with curls for eight weeks; the curling arm's elbow flexors grew about twice as much, and each arm gained more strength on the exercise it trained.7
  • The big reviews call it open. The ACSM's 2026 overview found too little data to say whether single-joint or multi-joint training builds more muscle.2 The 2021 review concludes that isolation exercises are unlikely to add much for most people over compound-only training, especially early on, while noting that their role remains equivocal.1

Put together: compound exercises cover a lot of muscle in little time, which is why reviews of time-efficient training favor them, and in one trial they did more for strength when the work was matched, so they're the base. Isolation exercises earn a place when a muscle needs more work than the compound lifts give it, or when you simply want to train it. Neither replaces the other.

Which to do first

Compound first, as a rule. The ACSM's 2009 position stand recommends that strength programs put large-muscle exercises before small ones and multiple-joint exercises before single-joint ones.8 The 2026 stand found strength was enhanced for exercises done at the start of a session rather than the end, but that exercise order didn't change muscle growth.2 So if you care about getting stronger on a lift, put it first. If it's a muscle you're after, order matters less, and doing the heaviest, most technical lift while you're fresh is still the sensible default.

Machines vs free weights

A compound exercise doesn't need a barbell. A leg press, a chest-press machine and a cable row are all compound.

  • For strength and muscle, it's close to a tie. A 2023 meta-analysis of 13 studies with 1,016 participants found no difference between free-weight and machine training when compared directly, on strength or on muscle size. Strength gains were specific to the equipment: people got stronger on whatever they trained on.9 The ACSM's 2026 overview likewise found strength wasn't affected by free weights versus machines.2
  • Machines suit beginners. The 2021 review notes that machines let people train hard, even to failure, without a spotter, which may especially help inexperienced lifters.1
  • Free weights suit home and variety. The same review points out that free weights allow many compound exercises with one set of equipment, and are preferable for a home gym.1 (A three-day dumbbell plan is the home version.)

Use whichever you have, can learn safely and will keep doing. Mixing them is normal.

Common mistakes

  • A week of only isolation exercises. Curls, raises and extensions on their own cover less of the body than a few compound lifts do.
  • Pushing without pulling. Every week needs rows and pulldowns as well as presses, and hinges as well as squats.
  • Too many heavy compound lifts in one session. Three heavy squat and deadlift variations in one day can be more fatigue than the session needs. Spread them across the week.
  • Treating "compound" as "barbell only". A leg press, a goblet squat or a push-up counts.
  • Supersetting the heaviest lifts. Pairs work well for machines and dumbbells, not for heavy squats and bench presses. (When supersets help and when they don't.)
  • Lifting through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.
