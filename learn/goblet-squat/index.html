Goblet squat: the beginner's squat, and when to move on

One dumbbell held at your chest, and you squat between your elbows. It's the squat you can learn in one session and load from the first day. Here's the setup, how deep to go, a version to a bench, the faults to watch for, and when the barbell takes over.

Short answer: Hold one dumbbell or kettlebell against your chest, feet a little wider than your shoulders, toes turned out slightly. Sit down between your heels, elbows inside your knees, as deep as you can keep your heels down and your chest up, then stand. A goblet squat is a squat with one dumbbell or kettlebell held against your chest, the way you'd hold a goblet. That's the whole difference from other squats, and it's why the goblet squat is a beginner's squat: no rack, no bar on your back, a load you pick up off the floor, and a weight in front that gives you something to sit down against.

This page covers the setup, how deep to go, a version to a bench or a box, what it trains, the faults, and when to move to a barbell. The barbell squat and its checkpoints are on squat form; this page is the step before it.

How to do a goblet squat

  1. Pick up the weight. Hold a dumbbell vertically by one end, both hands cupped under the top plate, or a kettlebell by the sides of its handle. Keep it against your chest, under your chin.
  2. Feet a little wider than your shoulders, toes turned out slightly. Adjust until the bottom position feels balanced. (Much wider is the sumo squat.)
  3. Breathe in and brace your midsection before each rep.
  4. Sit down between your heels. Hips and knees bend together, knees travel the way your toes point, and your elbows end up just inside your knees.
  5. Stand up by pushing the floor away, chest and hips rising together. Breathe at the top. That's one rep.

The weight stays on your chest the whole time. If it drifts forward, away from you, the squat tips forward with it.

What the goblet squat trains

Mostly the front of the thighs, with the glutes and inner thighs working as you go deeper. Two studies looked at where the load sits:

  • Goblet vs landmine squat. In 16 men and 16 women, each squatting with a load of 30% of their body mass, the goblet squat produced more front-of-thigh activity and more downward force than a landmine squat.1
  • Dumbbell, kettlebell or barbell. In 19 recreationally active men, every loaded squat produced more muscle activity than an unloaded one. With a kettlebell, one hamstring muscle was more active and a shin muscle less active than with a barbell, dumbbells or a weighted vest.2

So a dumbbell and a kettlebell both work; use whichever you have. Muscle activity during a set isn't growth over weeks, and neither study trained anyone.

How deep to go

As deep as you can hold the position: heels down, chest up, the weight against you. Depth has some of the better evidence in squatting:

  • A 2021 meta-analysis of 16 studies found that training through a full range of motion built more strength and more lower-body muscle than partial reps.3
  • In a 10-week trial, 17 men trained twice a week with full or half barbell squats. The front of the thigh grew about the same in both groups, while the glutes and inner thighs grew more with full squats.4
  • In a 12-week trial of 17 male students, the deep-squat group got stronger at both depths, while the shallow-squat group got much stronger at its own depth and less at the deep one.5

Those trials used a barbell, and they're small and in men, but the direction is the same: train the depth you want to be strong at. A practical target is thighs at least level with the floor. If you can't get there with your heels down yet, the box version below gives you a marker.

Goblet squat to a bench or a box

Put a bench, a box or a sturdy chair behind you and squat down until you touch it, then stand straight back up. It's useful in two ways:

  • A depth marker. Every rep reaches the same place, so a set doesn't get shallower as it gets hard. Start on a seat a little below knee height, and lower it as your depth improves.
  • A first squat. If a free squat feels unsteady, the box gives you a target. Brand-new lifters can start here with no weight at all.

Touch, don't sit. Resting your full weight on the box and rocking back changes the lift. A study of 12 male powerlifters found that the powerlifting box squat, done with a wide stance, kept the shins more vertical than a traditional squat and changed the turning forces at the spine, hips and ankles.6 That's a different exercise with its own uses; the goblet version to a bench is a normal squat with a marker at the bottom.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Heels lifting. Your weight drifts onto your toes. Sit between your heels rather than forward, and try a slightly wider stance.
  • Knees caving in. Push them out in the direction of your toes; your elbows inside your knees at the bottom are a good check.
  • The weight drifting forward. Pull it back to your chest. If you can't keep it there, it's too heavy for now.
  • The chest dropping on the way up. Hips rise first and the squat turns into a lean-over. Drive chest and hips up together, and rebrace before each rep.
  • Half reps. Depth shrinks as the set gets hard. Use the bench as a marker, or go lighter.
  • Squatting through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and adding weight

For new lifters, the American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 position stand recommends loads you can lift 8 to 12 times, and adding 2 to 10% once you can do one or two reps more than your target.7 Its 2026 stand found that strength was enhanced by two to three sets and by a complete range of motion.8 So:

  • Two or three sets of 8 to 15, stopping with a few reps still in you. (How to judge reps left.)
  • First in the session, or right after a warm-up, while you're fresh.
  • Add weight at the top of the range. Dumbbells jump in steps, so when the next one is too big a step, add reps first. (How much weight to lift covers choosing the starting weight.)

When to move to a barbell

A goblet squat's limit is what you can hold at your chest, and your legs usually outgrow your arms. Signs the goblet squat has run out:

  • Your heaviest dumbbell or kettlebell gives you 15 or more clean reps with plenty left.
  • Your grip or upper back gives out before your legs do.
  • Holding the weight in place has become the hard part of the set.

Before you move, slow the lowering, pause at the bottom, or switch to one leg at a time; the three-day dumbbell plan lists the fixes in order. When those run out, the barbell back squat is the next step, and squat form walks through its setup. The front squat, which also holds the load in front, was as effective as the back squat in overall muscle recruitment in a study of 15 trained people.9 The Zercher squat holds a barbell in front too, lower, in the crooks of your elbows. And if a barbell isn't for you, the leg press loads the same muscles on a machine; the ACSM's 2026 stand found strength gains didn't differ between free weights and machines.8

There's no rush. A goblet squat with a heavy dumbbell is still a squat, and the same rules about depth and effort apply to every version.
