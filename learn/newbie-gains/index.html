Newbie gains: why early strength comes first, and how to use it

Newbie gains is gym slang for the quick progress many people make in their first weeks of lifting. Here's what the research says about why strength comes first, when muscle size follows, what it doesn't say, and how to use the early weeks.

"Newbie gains" is what lifters call the quick progress in the first stretch of training: weights going up almost every session, reps coming easier, lifts you couldn't do a month ago. It's slang, not a scientific term. The research behind it is about how strength and muscle size change in people who've just started.

This page covers what the early gains are, why strength moves before size, what the studies say about how long the fast phase lasts (no source gives a fixed number), and how to use it. For the week-by-week timeline, how long results take has it; for the first month of sessions, how to start lifting weights does.

What "newbie gains" points to

Two things, mostly: strength rises quickly, and the strength gains are bigger for people who haven't lifted than for people who have. A 2017 meta-analysis of 18 studies that compared periodized and non-periodized plans found that improvements in one-rep max were greater among untrained participants than trained ones.1

Who counts as a newbie? The ACSM's 2009 position stand defines a novice as an untrained person with no resistance-training experience or who hasn't trained for several years, and an intermediate as someone with about six months of consistent training.2 That is a definition for programming, not a countdown for your gains.

Why strength comes first

Strength depends on more than muscle size. A 1988 review explains that strength training may cause changes in the nervous system that let you activate the muscles doing a movement more fully, and coordinate all the muscles involved better, so you produce more force in the intended direction.3 In practice that means you get better at the lift before the muscle has changed much.

An 8-week study of seven young men and eight young women put numbers on the order. Neural factors accounted for the larger proportion of the first strength increase; after that both neural factors and muscle growth contributed, with muscle growth becoming the dominant factor after the first 3 to 5 weeks.4 A 2007 review reaches the same place: neurological factors may make their greatest contribution early in a program, but muscle-growth processes also begin at the onset of training.5

Muscle size starts earlier than you'd think

Strength-first doesn't mean muscle-later. In seven young, healthy volunteers who did leg extensions on a flywheel machine three times a week for 35 days, a significant increase in quadriceps size showed up after 20 days, and the authors concluded that changes in muscle size are detectable after only three weeks.6 In 25 sedentary men scanned every week for eight weeks, the authors judged that significant growth likely happened around weeks three to four, after allowing for the swelling that can inflate the earliest scans.7 Both are small studies in young, healthy people.

So the early weeks mix learning the lift with early growth, and the mix shifts toward growth as the weeks pass. You can't see most of it yet. (Here's what the timeline looks like.)

How long does it last?

None of the sources for this page gives a fixed length for the fast phase, and it would be wrong to read the 3-to-5-week figure that way: that was when muscle growth took over as the main contributor to strength in one 8-week study, not when gains stopped.

Where the research does draw a line is training experience. The ACSM's 2009 stand changes its advice at about six months of consistent training: two to three days a week and loads of 8 to 12 reps maximum for novices, three to four days and a wider loading range for intermediates.2 The ACSM's 2026 stand adds two cautions. Most of the reviews it covered examined people with little or no lifting experience, and its authors note an earlier analysis in which training experience had a minimal impact on strength and muscle-growth outcomes.8

And people differ a lot. In 585 adults who trained one arm for 12 weeks, change in biceps size ranged from −2% to +59% and one-rep max from 0% to +250%, with some people showing little to no gain and others showing large ones.9 That range is the reason this page gives you no number for how much you'll gain. Your log will tell you more than an average can.

How to make the most of it

  1. Train the whole body two or three days a week. That is the ACSM's 2009 frequency for novices, with loads you can lift for 8 to 12 reps.2 The full-body plan is built that way.
  2. Add weight in small steps. The same stand's rule is a 2 to 10 percent increase once you can do one or two reps more than your target.2 (Progressive overload, explained.)
  3. Keep the same lifts for a month or more. This is practical advice, not a finding: if much of the early gain is skill at specific movements, practice on the same movements is how you collect it. (Squat form and bench press form are the places to start learning two of them.)
  4. Write every set down. You can't add a little if you don't know what you did. (What to write.)
  5. Make showing up the plan. The ACSM's 2026 stand says untrained people will benefit from various programs, provided progression, the variables it outlines and adherence are core principles.8 Many programs work, so the one you repeat is the one that does. (Consistency over motivation.)

What it doesn't mean

  • It isn't a promise. The studies above report averages and ranges in groups of people, and none of them can say what your first months will look like.
  • It isn't a deadline. Gains don't switch off on a date. In the 2017 meta-analysis above, one-rep-max gains were greater in untrained than in trained participants.1 Your log shows what yours are doing.
  • It isn't a reason to rush. Two or three sessions a week and small steps are the plan the ACSM's 2009 stand describes for novices.

Common questions

Do women get newbie gains?

Women respond to training too: a 2025 meta-analysis of 29 studies in healthy adults aged 18 to 45 found relative increases in muscle size were similar between men and women.10 In the 585-adult arm-training study, men had only a slight advantage in relative size gains, and relative strength increases were greater in women than in men.9

Can you get newbie gains again after a break?

That's a different question; getting back into training covers it.

Do I need a special program?

No. The ACSM's 2026 stand says untrained people benefit from various programs when progression, the variables it describes and adherence are in place.8 A simple full-body week you repeat is enough.
