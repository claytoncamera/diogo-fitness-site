Dead hang: how to do it, how long people hang, and what it trains

Hang from a bar with straight arms and your feet off the floor, for as long as your grip holds. Here's how the studies set it up, the times they measured, how those times rose over weeks, how the climbing version differs, the faults, and where it fits.

Short answer: Grab a pull-up bar overhand with your thumbs wrapped around it, straighten your arms, lift your feet and hang until your grip gives out. In 30 physically inactive young men, average hang times started at about 40 to 47 seconds, and after eight weeks of pull-up training they rose by about 41% to 55%.2

A dead hang is hanging from a bar with your arms straight and your feet off the floor, holding on until your grip gives out. One study puts it plainly: "Dead hangs require supporting the entire body weight with the hands or fingertips, often with arms overhead and elbows extended."1 It's the start position of the pull-up, a grip test, and, on a small wooden edge, a staple of climbing training.

This page covers how the studies set up the hang, the times they measured, how those times rose over weeks, the climbing version, the faults and the holds. Grip work beyond the hang is on the forearm workout.

How to do a dead hang

  1. Stand on a sturdy box under the bar so you can reach it without jumping.
  2. Grip the bar overhand, palms facing away, with your thumbs wrapped around it.
  3. Straighten your arms before you start.
  4. Step off the box so both feet leave it, and hang. Keep your legs still.
  5. Hold until your grip gives out, or until you choose to let go, then step back down onto the box rather than dropping.

That is how a hang test was run in an eight-week pull-up trial: a closed overhand grip with the thumb wrapped around the bar, elbows fully straight before the start, and a stable box under the bar. Timing started once both feet left the box and stopped at grip failure or when the person let go.2 The hand width and stepping down at the end are practical cues, not part of the protocol: hands about shoulder-width apart is a common start.

How long should you be able to dead hang?

No study cited here sets a target, but two give a sense of the range in young adults:

  • 30 physically inactive male university students (average age about 21) hung for about 40, 46 and 47 seconds on average in their three groups before training.2
  • 22 physically active men (average age about 22) hung with straight arms for about 73 seconds on average, and 20 highly trained motocross riders (about 19) for about 113 seconds.3

Both spreads were wide: in the riders and the active men the standard deviations were about 45 and 25 seconds.3 In practice, bars and grips differ from gym to gym, so compare your time with your own last one, on the same bar.

Dead hang time by age: every study here tested groups whose average ages were between about 19 and 34, and none reports times by age group, so this page has no age table to give.

Does dead hang time go up with training?

It rose in both studies found that measured it over weeks, including one where nobody trained the hang itself. Neither had a group that skipped training, so neither can say how much of the rise the training caused:

  • It rose during a pull-up program. The 30 inactive men trained twice a week for eight weeks with assisted pull-ups, inverted rows, reverse-grip pull-ups and negatives, plus interval running, forearm or core work by group. No group practised hanging, yet hang time rose in all three: by about 41%, 55% and 49%. The groups didn't differ significantly from each other, and the authors note their study had low statistical power for this measure.2
  • Ledge hang time rose in climbers doing ledge hangs. 26 advanced sport climbers finished an eight-week program of dead hangs on a small wooden edge, twice a week, in three groups. Hang time on an 11 mm edge rose by about 45% in the 8 doing repeated short hangs, about 34% in the 11 doing short heavy hangs, and about 7% in the 7 who switched from one to the other, a change the study didn't find significant; the groups didn't differ significantly from each other. They climbed six days a week alongside it, and there was no group that skipped the hangs.4

Neither shows that adding dead hangs to your week will lengthen your hang more than other grip or pulling work would. In the bar trial, hang time rose during a program of pull-up progressions and inverted rows with no hanging practice.

What the dead hang trains

Mainly your grip. The bar trial used hang time as its measure of grip endurance.2 The only study found for this page that recorded muscle activity during a dead hang tested 11 male climbers, advanced to elite, hanging to failure from a 20 mm wooden edge with straight arms. It recorded six muscles in each arm: the finger flexors and extensors, the brachioradialis on the thumb side of the forearm, the biceps, the trapezius and the chest muscle (pectoralis major). As they tired, the wrist moved in a more fixed pattern, and the authors read their data as the demands of keeping the hang stable shifting from the wrist toward the shoulder.1

That was a fingertip hang on an edge, not a full-hand hang on a bar, and it measured how muscles worked together, not how hard each one worked. This page is about grip and hanging; it makes no claim about the spine, the shoulders or posture. If you have shoulder or back pain, an injury or a medical condition, ask a doctor or a physical therapist before you hang.

The bar hang vs the climbing hang

In the climbing studies, dead hangs were done with the fingertips on a narrow wooden edge, and the edge depth set the difficulty: the climbing training study used an 11 mm edge for its test and changed the edge, or added weight on a belt, to set each session's load.4 A bar hang uses the whole hand around a round bar. The climbing results show ledge hang time rising over weeks of ledge training; their times don't compare with a bar time.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Jumping up to the bar. Use a box you can step off, so the hang starts still.
  • Bent arms. Straighten them before your feet leave the box. A bent-arm hang is a different hold.
  • Swinging. Keep your legs still, so every hang you time is the same hang.
  • Dropping off at the end. Step down onto the box when your grip goes.
  • Hanging through pain. Sharp pain in the hands, elbows or shoulders is not fatigue. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, holds and where it goes

  • What the climbing study used, on an edge: either 3 to 5 sets of one 10-second hang with 3 minutes between sets, or 3 to 5 sets of four or five 10-second hangs with 5 seconds between hangs and 1 minute between sets.4 That was for advanced climbers on a small edge, not a plan for a bar.
  • As a practical start on a bar: 2 or 3 holds as long as you can, with two or three minutes between them, two or three times a week. Write down your longest time. This is practical advice, not a finding.
  • At the end of a pull session, after pull-ups or lat pulldowns and rows, so a tired grip doesn't cut your main sets short.
  • Build toward the hanging core work: once you can hang for 30 seconds (a practical marker, not a finding), the hanging knee and leg raise and toes-to-bar start from the same position.

Common questions

Do dead hangs help you do pull-ups?

No study cited here tested that. In the eight-week trial, the group that added forearm training gained the most pull-up reps; hang time rose in all three groups, which didn't differ significantly.2 If your hands give out before your back does, a hang is a simple way to practise holding the bar. How to do a pull-up has the full routine.

Overhand or underhand grip?

The bar trial used an overhand grip with the thumb around the bar.2 An underhand or mixed grip is fine for practice, but keep the same grip each time so your times compare.

Can I dead hang every day?

No study cited here tested daily hanging. The bar trial tested the hang before and after training twice a week; the climbers hung twice a week.24 Two or three times a week is a practical start.
