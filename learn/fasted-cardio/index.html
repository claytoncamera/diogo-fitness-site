Does fasted cardio burn more fat? What the trials measured during the session, over the day and over weeks

Fasted cardio gets two answers, and both are true, because they measure different things. More fat is used as fuel while you train fasted; the trials that tracked body fat for weeks found no difference. Here's what each study measured, why the two don't line up, and how to decide when to do your cardio.

Short answer: During the session, yes: across 27 studies, cardio done fasted used more fat as fuel than cardio done fed.1 Over weeks, no difference showed up: in a 2014 trial, 20 young women on a calorie cut did an hour of cardio three days a week, fasted or fed, and their body composition changed similarly.4

Fasted cardio means cardio done after an overnight fast, usually first thing in the morning before breakfast. The claim behind it is simple: with no food in the system, your body uses more of its stored fat for fuel. That part is true. Whether it means more fat comes off you over weeks is a separate question, and the studies answer the two differently.

So this page keeps three measurements apart: fat used as fuel during the session, fat used as fuel over a whole day, and body fat measured over weeks. It's a page about exercise timing, not about fasting or diets; what to eat is on calories and protein, and the whole fat-loss list is how to lose body fat.

1. During the session: more fat used as fuel

A 2016 meta-analysis pooled 27 studies with 273 adults that compared the same aerobic session, up to 120 minutes long, done fasted or fed. Fat oxidation during exercise was significantly higher in the fasted state.1 Fat oxidation is fat used as fuel, usually estimated from the gases you breathe in and out. It's what the session burns, not what leaves your body over time.

2. Over the whole day: higher in two small chamber studies

The next question is whether the body makes up for it later in the day by burning less fat. Two small studies measured that directly, with people spending 24 hours inside a room-sized metabolic chamber. In 2015, 10 young, non-obese men each did four 24-hour trials, in random order: one sedentary, and three with 60 minutes of exercise at 50% of their aerobic capacity, before breakfast, after lunch or after dinner, with food matched to what they burned. Fat oxidation over 24 hours went up only when the exercise came before breakfast.2 A 2017 study with nine young women found the same: exercise before breakfast raised 24-hour fat oxidation compared with a sedentary day.3

That's a real finding, but notice what it measured: fuel use over one day in a lab, with eating matched to burning. It didn't measure body fat.

3. Over weeks: body fat changed the same

The 2014 trial behind the short answer was built to test exactly that. Twenty healthy young women were randomly assigned to train fasted after an overnight fast or after a meal, 10 in each group. Both did one hour of steady cardio three days a week for four weeks, on diet plans designed to put them in a calorie deficit. Both groups lost weight and fat mass, and the trial found no significant differences between the groups in any outcome it measured.4 Its authors' point is the one this page rests on: fat burning has to be judged over days, not hour by hour, to say anything about body composition. They also named their main limit: four weeks is short.4

A 2017 meta-analysis gathered what else there was: five studies with 96 healthy adults, comparing exercise after an overnight fast with exercise after a standard meal. The difference between fasted and fed was trivial for body mass, and in the data on women, trivial for body fat percentage and lean mass as well. The authors asked for caution because there were so few studies.5

4. Why the session and the weeks don't match

Fat used as fuel in one hour isn't the same as fat lost from your body. The 2014 trial's authors explain why: the body continually adjusts which fuel it uses, so burning more fat at one point in the day doesn't settle what happens over weeks.4 Over weeks, what moves body fat is the balance of what you eat against what you burn, which is why the fat-loss list on how to lose body fat starts with eating a little less than you burn, and treats cardio as a helper. Where cardio sits next to lifting: cardio vs weights for fat loss.

5. How the session itself goes

Training fasted or fed can change the workout, not just the fuel. A 2018 meta-analysis of 46 studies found that eating before exercise improved performance in prolonged aerobic exercise, but not in shorter aerobic exercise.6 So a long session is the one most likely to feel the difference. If you have a medical condition or take medication, ask your doctor before exercising without eating first.

6. So should you do it?

Do your cardio at the time you'll keep doing it. If first thing in the morning, before breakfast, is the slot that survives your week, fasted is fine; the trials that measured body composition found no cost to it. If you'd rather eat first, the same trials found no cost to that either. What adds up is the amount: the World Health Organization's guideline for adults is 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 of vigorous.7 How to build to that: how much cardio per week. Which kind: HIIT vs steady-state cardio, zone 2 cardio, or simply walking.

Common questions

Does fasted cardio burn more fat?

More fat is used as fuel during the session, and over a day in two small lab studies.1,2 When a four-week trial measured body fat, fasted and fed changed about the same, and a meta-analysis of five studies found the difference trivial.4,5 Both are true; they measured different things.

Does fasted cardio burn muscle?

Not in the trials that measured it. The 2014 trial found no significant difference between fasted and fed in any outcome, fat-free mass included, and in the 2017 meta-analysis the difference in lean mass between fasted and fed, in the data on women, was trivial.4,5 What keeps muscle while you lose fat is lifting and enough protein: keep lifting.

Is fasted cardio better for women?

The studies above include women, and found the same pattern. The 2014 trial was all young women, and body composition changed the same fasted or fed.4 The 2017 chamber study was all young women, and fat oxidation over the day was higher with exercise before breakfast.3 Training for women more broadly: strength training for women.

Fasted cardio in the morning and weights at night?

That works if it's the week you can keep. Splitting the two into separate sessions is covered, with what the research found about spacing them, on cardio and lifting. The same page answers the other common version: cardio before or after weights.

How long should fasted cardio be?

The 2014 trial used one hour of steady cardio, three days a week.4 Most people do less, and that's fine: your weekly total matters more than one session. If a session runs long, the 2018 meta-analysis is the one to know: eating beforehand helped prolonged aerobic performance.6

Is fasted cardio a myth?

Half of it isn't. The extra fat used as fuel is real and measured.1 The part that hasn't shown up is the payoff people expect from it: more body fat lost over weeks.4,5
