Resistance band workout: a full-body plan at home

A few bands and a door anchor fit in a drawer and cover a full-body plan. Here's the plan, what the research says when bands are compared with weights, what bands do differently, and how to keep a set hard when you can't add a plate.

Short answer: Two or three days a week, do six band movements: a squat, a hinge, a push, a row, a pulldown and a press, plus one core move. Do two or three sets of 12 to 20 reps, stopping one to three reps short of failure. Progress by adding reps, standing farther from the anchor, then moving to a thicker band.

A resistance band is elastic resistance: the more you stretch it, the harder it pulls back. A set of bands of different thicknesses and an anchor that closes in a door cost little, weigh almost nothing, and cover every movement a full-body plan needs, including the pull, the pattern that's hardest to load at home.

This page is a band plan for two or three days a week, with sections for legs, glutes and arms, and what to do when a band gets easy. With no equipment at all, start with the bodyweight workout; with a pair of dumbbells, the dumbbell plan.

Do resistance bands build strength?

In the trials that compared them with weights, yes:

  • A review of eight studies. 224 people aged 15 to 88, from active adults and athletes to clinical groups, trained with elastic bands or tubing, or with weight machines and dumbbells. Neither method came out ahead for upper- or lower-body strength.1
  • Fit young women. In 42 physically fit women, eight weeks of three or four sets of 8 to 15 reps with elastic tubing or with machines and free weights both raised strength on three tests, and a group that didn't train didn't improve. The row test rose 19.9% with tubing and 19.8% with weights; on the squat test the weights group gained more, 28.9% against 14.1%, though the authors judged the gains equivalent.2
  • Experienced lifters. In 30 university students with advanced lifting experience, five weeks of six-rep-max push-ups, made harder with an elastic band, improved one-rep and six-rep max scores about as much as six-rep-max bench pressing did.3

What these trials don't show is muscle size: all three measured strength. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand counts band training among the forms that increased both strength and muscle size compared with no training,4 but the head-to-head comparisons with weights above are strength comparisons.

What bands do differently

The load grows through the rep. When five widths of rubber band were measured, tension climbed as each band stretched, along a curve rather than a straight line.5 So a band row is easiest at arm's length and hardest with your elbows back, which is why the start of each rep needs a band that's already slightly taut.

The label is an estimate. In the same measurements, bands of the same color differed 3.5% to 5.2% in resting length, which made their tension differ by 8% to 19%.5 Two bands sold as the same resistance may not be. Track your bands by what you can do with them, not by the number printed on the packet.

What bands don't do well: small, exact jumps in load, and heavy work for the legs. A squat outgrows a band long before a row does.

The plan: full body, two or three days

Do the whole list each session, on two or three days that aren't back to back. A session takes thirty to forty minutes.

SquatBand squat: stand on the band, handles at your shoulders3 × 12–20
HingeBand Romanian deadlift: stand on the band, hinge at the hips3 × 12–20
PushPush-up with the band across your upper back, or a chest press with the anchor behind you3 × 8–15
RowSeated or standing row, anchor at chest height3 × 12–20
PulldownKneeling pulldown, anchor at the top of the door3 × 12–20
PressOverhead press, standing on the band2 × 10–15
CorePallof press: anchor at your side, press the handle straight out and resist the turn2 × 10 each side

Rest about a minute between sets. Stop each set one to three reps short of failure. (How to judge reps left.) Light loads aren't a lesser workout when the sets are hard: a review of 21 studies found that light and heavy loads built similar amounts of muscle when every set was taken to failure, while heavy loads did better for one-rep-max strength.6 For the back of the shoulders, a band face pull uses a door anchor set at face height.

Legs and glutes with a band

The band squat and Romanian deadlift in the plan are the base. Since a band runs out first on the legs, the extra work comes from going one leg at a time:

  • Split squat with the band under your front foot, handles at your shoulders.
  • Single-leg Romanian deadlift, the band under the standing foot.
  • For the glutes: a hip bridge or a hip thrust with the band across your hips, held down by your hands or anchored under a bench, and a side walk with a loop band above your knees. Two sets of 15 to 20 after the main movements.

Arms with a band

  • Curl: stand on the band, palms up, elbows still. A palms-in grip makes it a hammer curl.
  • Triceps pushdown: anchor at the top of the door, elbows pinned to your sides.
  • Overhead triceps extension: face away from a low anchor, or stand on the band and press the handle up behind your head.

Two sets of 12 to 20 of each, at the end of the session. Rows and pulldowns already work the biceps and presses the triceps, so the direct work adds to that rather than starting from zero. The arm workout covers how much to add.

How to progress with bands

You can't add a plate, so you change everything else, roughly in this order:

  1. Add reps up to about 20 on every set.
  2. Add stretch. Stand a step farther from the anchor, or hold the band shorter. More stretch at the start means more tension all the way through.
  3. Slow the lowering to three seconds and pause where the band is most stretched. (What tempo does and doesn't do.)
  4. Go to one arm or one leg.
  5. Move to a thicker band, or use two together, and drop back to the bottom of the rep range.

Write down the band, where you stood or anchored, and the reps, for every set. That note is the only load number a band gives you. When the thickest band is easy on the squat, keep the bands for rows, pulldowns and arms, and add a dumbbell or a kettlebell for the legs.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the band snap back. Control the return; it's half the rep.
  • Starting with a slack band. If the first third of the rep feels like nothing, step back until the band is taut at the start.
  • A loose anchor. Close the door so it opens away from you, and test the anchor with a hard tug before the first set.
  • A worn band. Check for nicks and thin spots before each session, and replace a band that has them.
  • Pushing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
