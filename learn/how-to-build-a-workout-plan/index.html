How to build a workout plan: count your days, cover the movements, then write the rule for adding weight

Writing your own program is less about clever exercises than about a few decisions made in the right order. Here they are, each with what the research says and the page on this site that goes deeper: days, split, movements, sets, reps, rest, progression and the log.

Short answer: Count the days you can train, pick a split that fits them, cover a squat, a hinge, a push and a pull, do at least two sets of each, and decide in advance when the weight goes up. A workout plan is a short list of decisions: which days, which exercises, how many sets and reps, how long to rest, and when to make it harder. Get those right and the plan works whether it lives on an index card or in an app. This page takes them in order, with what the research found for each and the page on this site that covers it properly. If you'd rather start from a finished plan, the free workout plans have made these decisions already, and the workout plan quiz points to the one that fits your week.

It's a page about how a plan is put together, not a promise about what a plan will do for you. The studies below measured groups of people, not you.

1. Count the days you can actually train

Start with the calendar, not the exercises. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand makes training with high effort at least twice a week, with all major muscle groups engaged, its primary recommendation.1 Its 2009 stand set a frequency for each level of experience: two to three days a week for novices, three to four for intermediates and four to five for advanced lifters.2 Pick the number you can keep in a bad week, not the one you'd manage in a good one. More on how often to train, and on what an intermediate lifter is.

2. Choose a split that fits those days

A split is how the work is spread across the week. A 2024 meta-analysis of 14 studies and 392 people found that split and full-body routines did not significantly differ in strength gains or muscle growth when the weekly volume was equated, and its authors concluded that people can choose a routine by personal preference.3 So let the day count decide: two or three days suits full body, four suits an upper/lower split, and five or six suits push/pull/legs. The trade-offs side by side: which workout split.

3. Cover a squat, a hinge, a push and a pull

You don't need many exercises. A 2021 review of time-efficient training recommends at least one leg-pressing exercise such as the squat, one upper-body pull such as the pull-up and one upper-body push such as the bench press.4 The 2026 ACSM stand's authors judge that training the upper and lower body with push and pull exercises is enough to target the major muscle groups.1 A practical list, then: a squat or leg press; a hinge such as the Romanian deadlift; a push such as the bench press or push-up; a pull such as the barbell row, pull-up or lat pulldown; and a core move. Why those carry the plan: compound exercises.

Order matters a little. The 2009 stand recommends large-muscle exercises before small ones and multiple-joint exercises before single-joint ones,2 and the 2026 stand found strength enhanced when an exercise came at the beginning of the session.1 So the lift you most want to improve goes first.

4. Set the sets: at least two per exercise, counted across the week

The 2026 stand advises at least two sets per exercise, and its authors hypothesize that the benefit of each further set diminishes.1 For muscle size, the same stand found growth enhanced by 10 or more sets a week.1 The 2021 time-efficiency review puts the floor at four weekly sets per muscle group.4 So count the hard sets each muscle gets across the whole week, not per session: a squat on Monday and a leg press on Friday both count for the legs. How to count your own week: how many sets per week.

5. Pick a rep range and an effort target

The 2009 stand recommends loads of an 8 to 12 rep maximum for novices, and a wider range of 1 to 12 for intermediate and advanced lifters, with heavy sets of 1 to 6 eventually emphasized for strength.2 A 2021 review argues that muscular adaptations can be obtained across a wide spectrum of loads,5 so the rep number matters less than how hard the set is. The 2026 stand says sufficient effort can come from sets taken near failure or to a target of 2 to 3 reps in reserve.1 How to write that down and read it back (3x10, 3x8-12, RPE 8): reps and sets, explained. Choosing the range: how many reps. Judging the effort: reps in reserve. Choosing the weight: how much weight to lift.

6. Rest long enough between sets

A 2018 systematic review of 23 studies and 491 participants concluded that 60 to 120 seconds of rest seems sufficient for strength gains in people new to lifting, while trained lifters seem to need more than two minutes to get the most strength.6 The 2009 stand pairs its muscle-building sets with 1 to 2 minutes of rest and its heavy strength sets with 3 to 5.2 Write the rest into the plan so it doesn't drift. The full picture: how long to rest between sets.

7. Write the rule for adding weight before you start

A plan that never gets harder stops working. The 2009 stand recommends adding 2 to 10% to the load once you can do the current weight for one or two reps more than your target.2 The simplest version is double progression: work up the rep range, then add weight and start again at the bottom. That's progressive overload, and deciding the rule in advance means you never have to guess on the day. Planned blocks of heavier and lighter weeks are optional at first: the 2026 stand found that, with appropriate progressive overload, periodization was not significantly better than a non-periodized program.1 More on periodization and the deload week.

8. Write every session down

The rule in step 7 only works if you know last week's numbers. A 2016 meta-analysis of 138 studies, about goals in general rather than lifting, found that monitoring progress promoted reaching goals, with larger effects when the information was physically recorded.7 What to write and how: the workout log.

A plan built with these steps

A practical example for three days a week, not a protocol from a trial. The sets sit inside the 2026 stand's advice of at least two per exercise,1 the reps in the 2009 stand's novice range2 and the rests in the 2018 review's range for people new to lifting.6

DaySquat or hingePushPullSets, reps, rest
MondaySquatBench pressBarbell row3 sets of 8 to 12, 2 to 3 reps in reserve, 1 to 2 minutes' rest
WednesdayRomanian deadliftOverhead pressLat pulldownThe same
FridayLeg pressDumbbell bench pressDumbbell rowThe same; add weight wherever every set reached 12

Each day covers a squat or hinge, a push and a pull, so each movement comes up three times a week. Add a core move at the end of any day, and warm up before the first lift (how to warm up before lifting). With four days, the same steps give the upper/lower plan.

How long to run it, and when to change it

Keep the plan while the numbers in your log are still going up. When a lift stalls for a couple of weeks, work through the stall checklist before swapping exercises, since a new exercise means learning a new skill. Once you've trained consistently for about six months, the 2009 stand's intermediate advice applies: three to four days a week and a wider range of loads.2 Rewrite the plan for that reason, not out of boredom.

Common questions

How do you build a workout plan as a beginner?

The same steps, kept small: two or three full-body days a week and loads you can lift for 8 to 12 reps, the 2009 stand's advice for novices.2 The first month, week by week: how to start lifting weights.

How do you build a workout plan at home?

The same movements with what you have. Where the weights jump in big steps, add reps or move to a harder version before adding weight. Finished versions: the dumbbell workout plan, the bodyweight workout and the home gym workout plan.

Is it different for women?

The steps are the same. In a study of 585 adults who trained one arm for 12 weeks, men made greater absolute gains, while relative strength gains were greater in women.8 More in strength training for women.

Should you write your own plan or follow one?

A finished plan is the same decisions made for you, and for most people starting out it's the quicker way in. Writing your own is worth it when your days, equipment or goal don't match any plan you can find. Either way, the plan you follow for months beats the clever one you drop after two weeks (why that happens).

If you'd rather have the plan written for you

These are decisions you can make yourself. The harder part is the weekly judgment: when to add weight, when to hold, what to change when a lift stalls.
