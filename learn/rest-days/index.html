Rest days: how many you need, and what to do on them

A rest day isn't the part of the plan where nothing happens. Here's how many you need, what to do on them, and where they go in a three- or four-day week.

Short answer: Lifting two to four days a week leaves three to five rest days, and that's enough for most people. What matters more is a day between hard sessions for the same muscles. Training every day isn't required: with the same weekly work, six days a week built about the same strength and muscle as three.3

A rest day can feel like a day you lost. It isn't. The session is the signal, and the days after it are when your body answers. In a small 1997 study of eight untrained volunteers, muscle was still building new protein faster than at rest two days after a single lifting session.1 Skip every rest day and you keep cutting that answer short.

This page covers how many rest days you need, whether training every day is a problem, what to do on a rest day, and where rest days go in a three- or four-day week. Still choosing how many days to lift? How often to train comes first.

How many rest days a week?

Count the days you lift. The rest of the week is rest from lifting. For most people it lands here:

Days liftingRest days from liftingHow to place them
25Two or three days apart, like Monday and Thursday
34A day between sessions, like Monday, Wednesday and Friday
43Upper body and lower body on alternating days, like Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Friday
5 or more2 or fewerA split, so no muscle trains hard two days running

The number matters less than one rule: leave at least a day between hard sessions for the same muscles. How hard you push changes how long that takes. In a 2017 study of ten trained men, sets taken all the way to failure slowed recovery for up to a day or two afterward, compared with the same total work done in sets stopped at half the reps possible.2 That's one reason to stop most sets with two or three reps still in you. Is it bad to work out every day?

Lifting every day isn't required, and it doesn't add much on its own. In a 2019 six-week trial of 27 trained men, those who lifted six days a week gained about the same strength and muscle as those who lifted three days a week doing the same total work.3 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found the same in its overview of the research: muscle growth didn't depend on training frequency, from one day a week to more than five, when the total amount of training was matched.4

So extra days spread the same work out; they don't multiply it. Where daily lifting goes wrong is when every day is hard. A joint consensus statement from the European College of Sport Science and the ACSM puts it plainly: training needs overload, but must "avoid the combination of excessive overload plus inadequate recovery."5

Moving every day is a different matter. The World Health Organization's guidelines ask adults to limit the time they spend sedentary, and say that replacing it with activity of any intensity, light included, has health benefits.6 A walk every day is fine. Hard sets for the same muscles every day is the part to avoid. If a client tells me they want to train every day, my first question is what the extra days are for.

How long recovery from a hard session takes

It can be longer than a night. In 12 well-trained men, each of whom did four sets to failure at 80% of their max on the squat, the bench press and the deadlift in separate weeks, bar speed at a lighter load was measured before each session, right after it and for four days afterward. After the squat it stayed down for up to 72 hours, by about 9%. After the bench press it dropped by about 27% right after the session. After the deadlift it didn't drop significantly at any point. Compared with each other, the three lifts showed no significant differences.7

That's one small study, and every set went to failure. Sets stopped short recover faster: in the 2017 study of ten trained men above, sets to failure slowed recovery for up to a day or two compared with the same work stopped at half the reps possible.2 Both fit the rule in the table: at least a day between hard sessions for the same muscles, and longer after sets taken to failure.

What a rest day looks like

There are two kinds, and both count.

  • A full rest day. Nothing planned. Your normal day and your normal steps.
  • An active recovery day. Light movement on purpose: a walk, an easy bike ride, an easy swim, some mobility work such as ankle drills. The World Health Organization describes light-intensity activity as about a 2 to 4 on a 0 to 10 effort scale, like slow walking, that doesn't raise your heart rate or breathing much.6 If you're breathing hard, it isn't recovery anymore.

Sore from the last session? Moving usually helps for a while. A 2003 review of the research found that exercise was the most effective way to ease the pain of muscle soreness, though the relief was temporary.8 So an easy walk on a sore day is a good idea. A hard session for the sore muscles isn't.

Cardio on a rest day from lifting is fine, as long as it doesn't leave you too tired for the next session. The WHO guideline asks adults for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week (or 75 to 150 of vigorous) on top of muscle-strengthening work,6 and the days between lifting sessions are a natural place for it. What a rest day isn't: a "rest day workout" that's secretly another hard session, or a day to make up a missed session by doubling the next one. A whole lighter week is a different tool: a deload week.

How rest days fit a three- or four-day plan

Three days: full body

Lift Monday, Wednesday and Friday, the whole body each time. Tuesday and Thursday are active recovery days; a walk is enough. The weekend is yours: one full rest day, and one with something you enjoy that keeps you moving. Every muscle gets at least a day between sessions, and two over the weekend. Short on time on lifting days? A 30-minute full-body session fits this week exactly.

Four days: upper and lower

Monday upper body, Tuesday lower body, Wednesday off, Thursday upper, Friday lower, the weekend off. Two days in a row is fine here, because Tuesday's session leaves alone the muscles Monday worked hardest. Each half of the body still gets two or more days before it trains again. (The four days, written out.)

Five or six days

It can be right for a specific goal, on a split where no muscle trains hard two days running. It's rarely what an ordinary week needs, and in the research above the extra days added no extra muscle when the total work was the same.

Signs to take an extra rest day

Plans are written in advance; bodies aren't. These are reasons to move a session or make it easier, not to give up on the week:

  • Soreness that changes how you move. If a squat looks different because your legs are sore, go lighter or train something else. The 2003 soreness review above advises athletes who must train daily to cut the intensity and length of their sessions for a day or two after a session that left them very sore, or to train the body parts that are less affected.8 Muscle soreness covers how long it usually lasts.
  • Your lifts drop two sessions in a row. One bad day is just a bad day. Two in a row on lifts that were going up is a good reason to take an extra day before the next one. A short dip after hard training is normal: the same 2013 consensus statement describes short-term drops in performance that turn into improvement after recovery.5
  • A short night. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend that adults sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis.9 After a night well short of that, move the session or do it with one set fewer, and save personal records for another day.
  • You're ill. Rest. If it doesn't pass, talk to a doctor before you train again.
  • Something hurts. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist. I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor.

And if you feel worn out for weeks, even with extra rest, that's a question for a doctor, not for a training plan.
