How to do a handstand: learn it on a wall, and balance it at the wrists

A handstand is a balance upside down on your hands: arms straight, your body stacked in one line over your hands, your legs together overhead. A practical way to learn it is against a wall first, then take the wall away. The research found for this page is almost all in gymnasts, and its clearest finding is where the balance comes from: the wrists first, with the better handstands in one force-plate study appearing to rely on the wrists and shoulders and little on the hips.1,2

This page covers the wall steps, what the studies found about balance, the head and the muscles, the faults, and how to practise it. The straight-body position it uses is the one on the hollow body hold.

How to do a handstand against a wall

  1. Set your hands on the floor about shoulder-width apart, a hand's length from the wall if you face away from it, fingers spread and pointing forward.
  2. Get up. Either kick up with one leg, back to the wall, until your heels touch it; or start in a plank with your feet against the wall and walk your feet up it while your hands walk back toward it, until your chest faces the wall.
  3. Lock your arms straight and push the floor away, so your shoulders rise toward your ears.
  4. Stack your body. Squeeze your legs together, tuck your ribs in and keep your hips over your shoulders, the same line as a hollow hold.
  5. Hold, and balance by pressing through your fingers: pressing harder stops you tipping toward your back, easing off lets you come back over your hands.
  6. Come down the way you went up: walk back down the wall, or step one foot down at a time.

These steps are practical coaching cues, not study findings: the studies on this page measured gymnasts already able to hold a handstand, not ways of teaching one.

Where the balance comes from

  • The wrists, with help from the shoulders. An analysis of six handstand balances on a force plate (the abstract doesn't say who did them) found that the wrists, shoulders and hips all contributed, and that in a number of them the wrists played the dominant role. The authors write that the better balances appear to be marked by the wrists and shoulders, with little from the hips, and that the hips were increasingly involved in the less successful balances.1
  • The review agrees. A 2025 systematic review of 21 biomechanical studies of the handstand concluded that gymnasts handle disturbed balance with a "wrist strategy" that keeps the body in a fixed shape. If the wrist strategy fails, the wrists, shoulders, hips and elbows all come in.2
  • Eyes and strength. The same review found that closing the eyes made the handstand less stable, and that gymnasts with greater strength had better balance control.2

In practice, that's why step 5 says to balance through the fingers, and why a bent body is treated as a fault: in the force-plate study, more hip involvement went with the less successful balances.

Where to put your head

Two small studies of gymnasts point the same way:

  • Seven experienced female gymnasts held handstands with the neck tipped back (extended), neutral and tucked (flexed). The extended neck had the least movement of the body's centre of mass, which the authors note is the position used in practice and competition.3
  • Five expert gymnasts held handstands in four head positions, with and without vision. Balance and performance were much better with the head in the standard position or tipped further back than with it in line with the body or tucked forward. In those two better positions, performance was lower without vision.4

In practice, that means looking at the floor between or just in front of your hands rather than tucking your chin to look at your feet. These were gymnasts; no study found for this page tested head position in beginners.

What a handstand works

  • The forearms most. In young and adult gymnasts (about 14 and 23 years old on average) holding 10-second handstands on a force plate, the wrist flexors (the muscles on the palm side of the forearm) showed the highest relative activity of every muscle group measured: 60% on average and 200% at peaks, on the study's scale. Their activity was also closely linked to how steady the handstand was.5
  • Then the shoulders and arms. In the same study, the upper trapezius and the wrist flexors made the largest contributions, followed by the front of the shoulder and the triceps. The adult gymnasts balanced better than the young ones, and the young ones showed more triceps, biceps and front-thigh activity.5
  • The surface changes it. In 10 adult gymnasts (about 25 on average) and 15 young ones (about 14), the wrist flexors worked less on parallel bars (44%) and rings (46%) than on the floor (61%), the triceps didn't change, and the rest of the 13 muscles measured showed more activity on the rings. The adults, the more experienced gymnasts, showed about 19% less triceps and 18% less front-shoulder activity than the young ones.6

Those authors recommend training the motor control and strength of the front of the shoulder, the chest, the lats, the biceps and the upper traps for a better handstand.6 That's their recommendation from muscle-activity data in gymnasts, not a training study. On this site, the front of the shoulder is covered on the overhead press and the chest on push-ups; neither exercise was tested in these studies.

If you're starting from zero

One study looked at novices. In 111 novice college athletes, aged 19 to 23, scores on two standard shoulder stability and mobility tests had no relationship with how well they performed a handstand. The authors give two possible reasons: the tests load the shoulder differently from a handstand, and their novices couldn't yet do the handstand well enough.7

The practical reading, from that one study: a shoulder test may not tell you whether you're ready. Time upside down against the wall is what you'll have to put in. A practical order to learn it in:

  1. A plank with straight arms, and a hollow body hold, until both are steady for 30 seconds.
  2. The chest-to-wall walk-up, holding 10 to 30 seconds.
  3. The kick-up with your back to the wall.
  4. From the wall, pull one heel and then both away for a second or two at a time, balancing through your fingers.
  5. A freestanding kick-up, coming down by stepping one foot down, or turning out to the side.

This order is practical advice, not a finding.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Bent arms. The handstand sinks. Lock the elbows and push the floor away.
  • A banana back. The lower back arches and the legs drift over. Tuck the ribs in and squeeze the legs together, as in a hollow hold.
  • Saving it with the hips. In the force-plate study, the less successful balances leaned more on the hips.1 Correct with the fingers early, before the hips have to move.
  • Tucking the chin. As a practical cue, look at the floor near your hands: in the head studies above, gymnasts balanced better with the head tipped back than tucked forward.3,4
  • Kicking too hard. You overshoot. Kick lighter and let the wall catch you while you find the line.
  • Training through something that hurts. If you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, stop and ask a doctor or a physical therapist.

How to practise it in a week

As a practical start, not a finding: five to eight attempts of up to 30 seconds against the wall, with a minute's rest between, early in a session while you're fresh, on three or four days a week. On the days you lift, it fits after the warm-up and before pressing; on other days it can stand alone as part of a bodyweight workout.

Common questions

How long does it take to learn a handstand?

No study found for this page measured how long adults take. The studies here measured gymnasts who could already hold one, and novice college athletes at a single point in time; none followed people while they learned.7

Chest to the wall or back to the wall?

No study comparing them was found. In practice, the chest-to-wall walk-up is the easier start because you don't have to kick; the back-to-wall kick-up is closer to how you'll get up freestanding.

Do I need strong shoulders first?

The review found that gymnasts with greater strength had better balance control.2 But in 111 novice college athletes, two shoulder tests didn't predict who did a better handstand.7 In practice, if you can hold a straight-arm plank and walk up the wall, you're ready to start.

Handstand or muscle-up?

Different skills: a handstand is a balance on straight arms; the muscle-up is a pull, a turn over the bar and a dip. Both are skill goals, and fine ones if they're yours.
