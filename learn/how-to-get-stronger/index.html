How to get stronger: lift heavy, practice the lifts, and add a little at a time

Getting stronger is a different job from getting bigger, even though the two overlap. Here's the short list the research points to, what each study measured, and the page on this site that goes deeper: load, practice, progression, frequency, sets, rest and time.

Short answer: Lift heavy on a few big lifts, two or more days a week, and add a little weight when the reps get easier. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 overview of 137 systematic reviews found strength was enhanced by heavier loads (80% of a one-rep max or more), 2 to 3 sets and two or more sessions a week.3

Strength is how much force you can produce, usually measured as the heaviest weight you can lift once. It grows from two places: the muscle itself, and how well your nervous system uses it on a given lift. That's why getting stronger and building muscle share most of their rules but not all of them. What follows is the short list the research points to for strength, each in a paragraph, with the page on this site that covers it properly.

It's a page about how strength is built, not a promise about how strong you'll get. The studies below measured groups of people; your own pace depends on where you start, how you train and things a page can't see.

1. Lift heavy

Load is the clearest difference between training for strength and training for size. A 2017 review of 21 studies, all with sets taken to failure, found one-rep-max strength rose more with heavy loads than light ones, while muscle growth was similar.1 A 2021 network meta-analysis of 28 studies in 747 healthy adults found strength gains larger with heavy and moderate loads than with light ones.2 And the American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found voluntary strength enhanced by heavier loads, at 80% of a one-rep max or more.3 In a 14-athlete Brazilian jiu-jitsu trial on strength training for martial arts, the group that added squats and bench presses at 85% or more of a one-rep max three times a week raised the squat 15% and the bench 11%, while the group that only did BJJ showed no improvements.4 In practice that means sets of roughly one to six reps on your main lifts once you have been lifting consistently for a while: the heavy range the ACSM's 2009 stand eventually emphasizes for intermediate and advanced lifters, which it defines as about six months of consistent training or more.5 How the rep ranges line up by goal: how many reps. Picking the weight: how much weight to lift.

2. Practice the lifts you want to be strong at

Part of strength is skill. A 1988 review explains that strength training may change the nervous system so it activates the muscles in a movement more fully and coordinates them better.6 In an eight-week study of 7 young men and 8 women, nervous-system factors accounted for most of the first strength increase, with muscle growth becoming the main factor after the first 3 to 5 weeks.7 That skill is specific: in 36 trained men who pressed twice a week for ten weeks, the groups using dumbbells or an exercise ball improved more at the press they trained than at the ones they didn't.8 A 2021 review makes the same point about the research itself: heavy loads look better for strength partly because strength is usually tested on the exercises people trained.9 In sport, a 2021 review on strength training for golfers found general strength training did more for club head speed when fast golf-specific moves were added to it.10 So pick a few lifts, learn them well and keep them for months. Which ones: compound exercises, with form on the squat, the deadlift, the bench press and the overhead press. Why early strength comes so fast: newbie gains.

3. Add a little at a time

Strength keeps rising only if the work keeps asking for more. The ACSM's 2009 position stand recommends adding 2 to 10% to the load once you can lift the current weight for one or two reps more than your target.5 That's progressive overload, and the systems for it are on progressive overload. You can only add to numbers you remember, which is what a workout log is for. Planned blocks of heavier and lighter weeks are one way to organize it, and the 2026 stand found them less important than once thought when overload is in place.3 More on periodization.

4. Train each lift two or more days a week

The 2026 ACSM stand found strength enhanced with two or more sessions a week.3 A 2018 meta-analysis of 22 studies found strength gains rose with training frequency, but not once weekly volume was equated, and concluded that more frequent sessions can be used to fit in more training.11 The simplest way to hit each big lift twice is two or three full-body days: the full-body workout plan, or a 5x5 workout if you like the same few lifts every session. Other ways to lay out the week: how often to train.

5. Do a few hard sets, not endless ones

The 2026 stand found strength enhanced with 2 to 3 sets.3 More work still helps, but less and less: a 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 participants found strength and muscle size both rose with weekly sets, with diminishing returns that were considerably more pronounced for strength.12 So for strength, a few heavy sets done well count for more than a long list of extra ones. How to count your own week: how many sets per week. How close to the limit each set should go: reps in reserve.

6. Rest long enough between heavy sets

Heavy sets need the rest to be heavy. A 2018 systematic review of 23 studies and 491 participants found robust strength gains even with rests under 60 seconds, but concluded that trained lifters seem to need more than two minutes to get the most strength, while 60 to 120 seconds looked enough for people new to lifting.13 The ACSM's 2009 stand pairs its heavy sets with 3 to 5 minutes of rest.5 The full picture: how long to rest between sets.

7. Give it weeks, and expect your own pace

People respond differently. In 585 adults who trained one arm for 12 weeks, one-rep-max gains ranged from 0% to +250%, and the authors described some people showing little to no gain and others large ones.14 That's the reason to judge your training by your own logbook, week over week, rather than by anyone else's numbers. What to expect and when: how long to see results.

A sample week

A practical layout of the list above, not a protocol from a trial. New lifters start in the ACSM's novice range of 8 to 12 reps, and the heavier range is for later, once you're past the stand's mark of about six months of consistent training;5 the rests follow the 2018 rest review.13

DayMain liftsSets and reps
MondaySquat, bench press, barbell row2 to 3 sets each: 8 to 12 reps when new, 3 to 6 after about six months of steady lifting; rest 1 to 2 minutes when new, more than 2 once trained
WednesdayDeadlift, overhead press, pull-upThe same
FridaySquat, bench press, barbell rowThe same; add weight wherever last week's reps went up

Every lift comes up at least twice in two weeks and the squat, bench and row twice a week. Write down every set. When a lift stalls for a couple of weeks, a deload week is the usual next step before changing anything else.

Common questions

How do you gain strength fast?

Most of the first strength increase comes from the nervous system rather than from muscle growth: that's what the eight-week study above found.7 That's what was measured in 15 adults, not a schedule for you, and the 585-person study above shows how widely people differ.14 The part in your control is the list: heavy sets, the same lifts, small jumps and no missed weeks.

Why am I not getting stronger?

Check the list above against your log, in order: are the main sets heavy enough (point 1); are you training the lift you're testing (point 2); has the weight on it gone up at all in the last few weeks (point 3); is it trained two or more days a week (point 4); are the rests long enough (point 6)? If all of that is in place and the lift still stalls for a couple of weeks, that's a strength plateau: work through the plateau checklist, then take a deload week. And people differ: in the 585-adult study above, some people showed little to no gain from the same 12 weeks of training.14 Stuck on the bench in particular: bench press form.

Can you get stronger without getting bigger?

To a point. In the 21-study review, heavy loads gave more one-rep-max strength while muscle growth was similar across loads,1 and in the 2026 meta-regression strength levelled off with extra sets much sooner than muscle size did.12 So a few heavy sets capture more of the possible strength gain than of the possible size gain. They don't rule growth out: in the eight-week study above, muscle growth became the main source of further strength after the first 3 to 5 weeks.7

How do you get stronger legs or arms?

The same list, applied to the lifts that train them. For legs: the squat, the deadlift and the leg day workout. For arms, the presses and pulls do most of the work, and the arm workout counts what they already did. Strength gains are partly specific to the movement you train, so train the movement you want to be strong at.8

Is it different for women?

The list is the same. In the 585-adult arm study, men gained more in absolute terms, but relative strength gains were greater in women than in men.14 More in strength training for women, and for older lifters in strength training over 40.
