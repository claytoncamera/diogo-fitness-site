Progressive overload: the one rule behind every good program

If your workouts look the same in month six as they did in week one, your body has no reason to change. Here's how to make them harder, slowly, without wrecking yourself.

By Diogo·Updated October 26, 2026·4 min read

Every program that works, from a beginner's three-day plan to a powerlifter's peaking block, is built on the same idea: ask your body for a little more than last time, give it time to adapt, repeat. That's progressive overload. Everything else is details.

A lot of people who "work out" don't do it. They do the same weights for the same reps for months, wonder why nothing changes, and blame their genetics or their diet. Usually it's just that nothing got harder.

What progressive overload is

Muscle and strength are adaptations. Your body builds them because the current level isn't enough for what you're asking of it. Once it catches up, it stops. Progressive overload means staying slightly ahead of that by gradually increasing the demand, which the American College of Sports Medicine's position stand on resistance training treats as the basis of long-term progress.1

The key word is gradually. Overload isn't "go as hard as possible." It's "do a little more than last time, on purpose, and keep a record."

Five ways to progress (weight is only one)

  1. More weight. The obvious one. Same reps, heavier bar. A 5x5 workout runs on this one.
  2. More reps. Same weight, more reps per set. In an eight-week trial, lifters who progressed by adding reps built about as much muscle as lifters who progressed by adding load.2 Strength favored the load group slightly, by a margin of little practical size.2
  3. More sets. One extra set a week on a lift that's stalling.
  4. Better reps. Fuller range of motion, a controlled lowering phase, less momentum. Same numbers, more work.
  5. Less rest. Same work in less time. Useful for conditioning, less useful for pure strength.

For most people, most of the time, use the first two. They're easy to track and hard to fool yourself about.

How fast to add weight

The simplest system is double progression. Pick a rep range for each exercise, say 8 to 12. Start with a weight you can do for 8 good reps. Each week, try to add a rep or two. When you hit 12 on every set, add weight and drop back to 8.

The ACSM guidance is similar: once you can do one or two reps more than your target, increase the load by about 2 to 10 percent.1 I'd use the smaller jumps for upper-body and single-joint lifts. On a 100-pound bench press that's 2.5 to 10 pounds. On a 200-pound squat it's 5 to 20. Take the small jump. You can always take another one next week. (How to set up the bench itself: bench press form.)

How hard each set should be

Close to failure, not at it. In my experience, one to three reps in reserve on most working sets is where muscle growth and recovery meet. (Here's how to judge that.) If you stop at five reps in reserve, the set probably did little, no matter what the weight was.

Heavy or light matters less than people think. A meta-analysis of 21 studies found that light loads and heavy loads built similar amounts of muscle when sets were taken to failure, while heavy loads were better for one-rep-max strength.3 So pick weights you can control, work them hard, and progress them.

How much is enough

More sets generally mean more growth, up to a point. A review of 15 studies found that each extra weekly set per muscle was linked to a small additional gain, with a trend toward 10 or more sets a week per muscle doing better than fewer.4 That's a starting point, not a target to blow past. If you can't recover from it, it isn't overload, it's just fatigue. (How many rest days you need.)

A beginner on two or three full-body days a week will land around 6 to 10 hard sets per muscle. That's plenty. Add from there only when progress stops. How you spread those sets over the week is your workout split. (Counted out for one muscle group: the chest workout and the shoulder workout.)

When you stall

You will. Stalls are information, not failure. In order, check:

  • Sleep and food. Under-recovered lifters don't progress. In my experience, protein, calories and sleep fix more stalls than any program change.
  • Effort. Are the sets actually close to failure, or did they drift to comfortable?
  • Volume. If you've been adding sets for weeks, take one week at half the sets, then come back. Progress often resumes on its own. That's a deload week.
  • The rep range. Stalled at 5 reps? Spend a few weeks at 10 to 15, then come back heavier.

Don't change the exercise first. Changing exercises resets the skill, and the "progress" you see is just relearning.

Write it down

None of this works from memory. You need to know what you did last week to do a little more this week. An app, a notebook, the notes on your phone. It doesn't matter which. (What to write in it: the workout log.) My clients log every set in Trainerize, and when I look at a week of check-ins, the logbook tells me more than the scale does.
