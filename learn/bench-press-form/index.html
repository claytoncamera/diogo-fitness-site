Bench press form: set up first, then add weight slowly

A good bench starts before the bar moves: feet, shoulder blades, grip. Here's the setup and the rep, what the studies say about grip width and arching, and how to add weight without rushing it.

Short answer: Lie with your eyes under the bar, feet flat and shoulder blades pulled back. Grip the bar wider than your shoulders: in two studies, medium and wide grips moved more weight than a shoulder-width or narrower one.1,2 Lower the bar under control to touch your chest, press it back up, and add weight in small steps.

One 2021 study calls the bench press perhaps the most frequently used exercise for strength and muscle in the upper body.1 It's also an easy lift to load faster than your form can keep up. The fix is boring: the same setup on every set, a full rep, and small jumps.

This page covers the setup, how wide to grip, whether to arch, the rep itself and the common faults, then how to add to your bench without rushing it. The other two big lifts have their own pages: squat form and deadlift form.

How to set up

Most of a good rep happens before you unrack the bar. In order:

  1. Eyes under the bar. Lie down so the bar in the rack sits roughly over your eyes: close enough to unrack without a reach, far enough not to hit the hooks.
  2. Feet flat. Plant them on the floor, a little wider than your hips, and keep them there for the whole set.
  3. Shoulder blades back and down. Squeeze them together and set them down toward your back pockets before you unrack, and keep them there. Your upper back, not your shoulders, is what touches the bench.
  4. Head, upper back and hips on the bench. That's also how the 2021 grip study below ran the lift: head, shoulders and buttocks in contact with the bench for the entire lift, and the bar lowered to lightly touch the chest with no bounce.1
  5. A full grip. Thumbs wrapped around the bar, the bar over the heel of your palm rather than out in your fingers, wrists straight over your elbows.
  6. Unrack, then settle. Lift the bar out with straight arms, bring it over your shoulders, take a breath, and start the first rep from there.

How wide to grip

Two studies compared narrow, medium and wide grips head to head:

  • In trained lifters and novices. 28 men, 15 of them trained (they could bench at least 125% of their body weight) and 13 novices, lifted their six-rep max with three grips. Narrow was the distance between the armpits, wide was the widest grip allowed in competition (81 centimeters, about 32 inches), and medium was halfway between. Both groups lifted less with the narrow grip, and there was no difference between medium and wide. The chest worked about the same with all three; the wide grip asked less of the triceps than the medium and narrow grips, and more of the biceps.1
  • At a one-rep max. In 14 bench-trained men, with narrow set at shoulder width, medium at 1.4 times shoulder width and wide at 1.7 times, the maxes were about 110 kilograms wide, 109 medium and 104 narrow. The authors conclude that wide and medium grips allow more load than the narrow grip.2

So a medium grip, clearly wider than your shoulders but short of the widest you can reach, is a sensible default: it moved about as much weight as the wide grip in both studies. A narrower grip costs some weight, and a very wide one shifts a little work away from the triceps. Pick one, mark where your hands go on the bar, and use the same spot every set.

Close-grip bench press and decline bench press

The close-grip bench press is the same press with your hands about shoulder-width apart. It's typically done to put more of the press on the triceps.3 In 27 resistance-trained lifters, 21 men and 6 women, their usual grip lifted about 5% more for one rep than a grip with the hands at 95% of shoulder width, while the close grip moved the bar faster, with about 23% higher peak speed.3 On the triceps, the studies differ: in the 28 men above, the wide grip asked less of the triceps than the medium and narrow grips,1 while in 12 national- and international-level bench press athletes, triceps activity showed no significant difference between narrow, medium and wide grips, and the wide grip's six-rep max was 11.1% heavier than the narrow grip's.4

The decline bench press tilts the bench so your head sits below your hips. In the same 12 athletes, on a bench declined 25 degrees, the six-rep max (129.4 kilograms) showed no significant difference from the flat bench (132.7), and neither did chest or triceps activity.4 In 20 lifters with at least a year of training, 10 men and 10 women, the front of the shoulder worked harder on the incline barbell press than on either decline press.5 So the decline is an option rather than a requirement; in the 12-athlete study the flat press matched it on load, chest and triceps. No bench at all? The dumbbell floor press is in the chest workout's home section.

Should you arch your back?

Some arch is normal; a big one is a competition technique. Two studies tested it in strong lifters:

  • In 11 competitive male powerlifters, a flat bench, with a natural lower-back arch and a moderate shoulder-blade squeeze, and an arched bench, with a pronounced arch and squeeze, had no significant difference in one-rep max: 115.9 and 115.7 kilograms.6
  • In 15 highly trained lifters (13 men and 2 women, ten of them competitive powerlifters), the arched bench lifted about 4 kilograms more, a small difference (95% confidence interval 0.0 to 8.4 kilograms), the bar traveled further with the flatter back, and the chest muscles worked about the same either way. The authors say both styles may be used, depending on the goal.7

Both studies were in experienced competitors, so they say little about a first bench. What they do suggest is that the natural arch and shoulder-blade squeeze from the setup above is enough for the chest to do its work. Whatever arch you use, keep your hips on the bench.

The rep: lower, touch, press

  1. Lower it under control. For heavy strength work, the American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 position stand recommends a moderate speed: about one to two seconds down and one to two seconds up.8
  2. Let your elbows come in a little. As the bar comes down, your elbows travel a little toward your sides rather than straight out, so the bar lands on your lower chest with your forearms roughly upright.
  3. Touch your chest, don't bounce off it. A light touch, then press.
  4. Press up and slightly back, so the bar finishes over your shoulders where it started. Feet down, hips on the bench, shoulder blades still pinned.

Use the full range. In a ten-week trial, 50 men ranging from recreationally to highly trained were split between a full bench press, a two-thirds bench, a one-third bench and a group that stopped training; the three bench groups trained at the same relative loads. The full-range group improved the most on all three versions, and the partial groups improved less the shorter their range was.9 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand also lists a complete range of motion among the things that enhanced strength.10

If a heavy rep stalls, it usually stalls partway up. On a near-maximal bench, researchers call the stretch where the bar slows after leaving the chest the sticking region, and describe it as the region in which most lifts fail.2 A slow grind there is normal on a heavy rep; don't shorten the rep to avoid it.

How to increase your bench press without rushing it

There's no shortcut, and nothing here promises a number by a date. What the evidence supports is a handful of habits:

  • Bench at least twice a week, early in the session. The ACSM's 2026 stand found strength was enhanced by training at least two sessions a week and by doing an exercise at the beginning of a session rather than the end.10 How you fit two bench days into your week is your workout split; give each one a pull too, like the pull-up.
  • Include some heavy sets. The same stand found strength was enhanced by heavier loads, at least 80% of your one-rep max, done for two to three sets.10 A heavy set leaves you with only a few reps to spare; how close to go is reps in reserve.
  • Rest long enough to stay heavy. The ACSM's 2009 stand recommends three to five minutes between heavy sets of one to six reps.8
  • Add weight in the smallest steps you can. When to add and how much is progressive overload; the bench does well on the small jumps on that page. Add a rep before you add a plate.
  • Keep the rep the same. A heavier bench that bounces or stops shorter isn't a stronger bench.

Dumbbells, the Smith machine and benching without a spotter

In 12 trained men who tested all three, the dumbbell bench press max was 17% lighter than the barbell and 14% lighter than the Smith machine, and the barbell was about 3% heavier than the Smith machine. Chest and front-shoulder activity didn't differ between the three; the dumbbells asked less of the triceps than the barbell.11 So all three train the chest. The barbell lets you lift the most, and dumbbells take the most balance. (The same question for other lifts: dumbbell vs barbell.) The same press with the back of the bench raised, and the angle to set it at, is the incline bench press.

Training alone? Bench inside a power rack with the safety arms set just below the height of your chest at the bottom of a rep, so that if you miss one you can lower the bar onto them and slide out. Dumbbells and the chest press machine are the other options, and on heavy sets alone, leave a rep or two in the tank.

Common mistakes

  • Bouncing the bar off your chest. A light touch is the rep; the 2021 grip study didn't allow a bounce either.
  • Hips lifting off the bench. It can mean the weight is too heavy or your feet are too far back.
  • Stopping short. Half reps let you put more on the bar, but the full-range group in the trial above improved the most.
  • Pressing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.
