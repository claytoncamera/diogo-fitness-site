Thoracic mobility: the upper-back range your overhead lifts use

Your thoracic spine, the upper and middle back, bends back and turns. For a lifter, that range shows up most when your arms go overhead. Here's what the upper back does in that position, a simple test, the exercises, and what the few studies on them found.

Thoracic mobility is how freely your thoracic spine moves: the part of your back between your neck and your lower back, behind the ribs. It bends back (extension), turns (rotation) and bends forward and to the side. For a lifter, the moment it matters most is arms overhead: the top of an overhead press, the hang of a pull-up, the bar overhead in an overhead squat.

This page covers what the upper back does when your arms go up, a simple test, the exercises, and what the few studies on them do and don't show. It's about training range in a healthy back. The full ten-minute routine for ankles, hips, upper back and shoulders is the mobility routine; hip mobility and ankle mobility are its lower-body neighbors.

What your upper back does when your arms go overhead

  • It extends. In 21 men with no symptoms, X-rays taken standing and then with both arms fully raised showed the thoracic spine moving toward extension by 12.8 degrees on average. The amount varied a lot from person to person.1
  • It moves with the arm and shoulder blade. In 32 healthy women raising their arms, the upper arm, the shoulder blade and the thoracic spine moved together in a consistent, synchronous pattern, whichever direction the arm was raised in. The authors concluded that raising the arm has significant implications for the thoracic spine as well as the shoulder.2

So when the bar drifts forward at the top of a press or your ribs flare to get the arms up, the upper back is one of the places to look, along with the shoulders, which the mobility routine's shoulder section covers. Neither study tested whether more upper-back range changes a lift. And some of any limit is structure: a 2026 paper on range of motion lists bone structure among the parts of range that training can't change.3

A simple test

  1. The kneeling rotation test. Kneel, sit back onto your heels with your knees slightly apart, and put your forearms on the floor in front of you, elbows under your shoulders. Place your right hand on your left shoulder and turn your chest to the right as far as you can, then switch sides. In research it's called the lumbar-locked trunk rotation.
  2. The arms-overhead check. Stand with your back against a wall, heels a few inches out, and raise both arms straight overhead. Can your thumbs touch the wall without your lower back arching away from it?

The first is a measured test. In 17 young adults tested on three separate days, the five upper-back tests in that study, this one among them, gave mostly good to excellent repeat measurements with clinical tools.4 At home you won't have those tools, so use it the practical way: compare your two sides, and compare yourself with yourself in a month. The wall check is practical, not a research test.

For the one held stretch, the 2025 consensus statement of 20 stretching experts recommends two or three sets a day of 30 to 120 seconds per muscle for long-term range,5 and the table sits at the low end. Move slowly, to where you feel the stretch, never into pain.

What the studies found

There isn't much. The 2024 paper below opens by noting that few studies have looked at whether thoracic exercises improve thoracic range. Three recent ones in healthy people:

  • Two weeks, two exercises. 39 healthy people did either a quadruped thoracic rotation (17 people) or a standing thoracic rotation (22) for two weeks. Both achieved equivalent improvement in thoracic range of motion; the authors' takeaway was that you can pick the version that suits your setting.6 The study compared the two exercises with each other, not with doing nothing.
  • One session. In 15 physically active college-aged adults, a single session of thoracic mobility exercises increased thoracic extension and trunk rotation to the right compared with brisk walking, but the changes were too small to count as meaningful, and rotation to the left didn't change.7
  • Four weeks, added to training. In 36 adolescent female volleyball players, adding thoracic mobility exercises to training for four weeks produced results similar to training alone: both groups improved in strength, thoracic rotation, and spike and serve speed, and neither group came out ahead.8

The wider stretching research fills some of the gap. A 2024 meta-analysis of 77 studies found that regular stretching increased range of motion with a moderate effect, and that static holds and PNF stretching produced more range than dynamic stretching.9 It doesn't single out the upper back.

What none of it shows: that more thoracic range on a test makes your overhead press, pull-up or overhead squat better, or that you need a particular number of degrees. Treat the drills as a way to train a position your lifts use, and judge them by whether that position gets easier.

Where it goes in your week

  • Before pressing or pulling: the moving drills (quadruped rotations, open books, wall slides) fit into the warm-up; they move rather than hold, like dynamic stretching.
  • After lifting or on a rest day: the held lat stretch, for the reasons in the warm-up guide's stretching section.
  • With the rest of your mobility work: if your squat or hinge also stops short, hip or ankle work may matter more than the upper back.
  • Retest every four weeks with the same kneeling rotation and wall check, and write down what changed.

This is training advice for a healthy back. If your back or neck hurts, or you're coming back from a problem there, a doctor or a physical therapist is the right first stop.

Common questions

What is thoracic mobility?

The range your upper and middle back can move through: bending back, turning and bending to the side. Lifters mostly care about the first two, because raising your arms overhead extends it.1

How do you improve thoracic mobility?

With slow rotation and extension drills done regularly, like the table above. In the two-week study, a quadruped rotation and a standing rotation worked equally well.6 A single session made only a small difference,7 so repetition over weeks is the point.

Are thoracic mobility exercises worth doing if you already lift?

If your overhead lifts reach their position, there's little evidence either way. The one trial that added them to training was in adolescent female volleyball players, and in that group it found no edge over training alone.8 It didn't test adult lifters. If your arms can't get overhead without your lower back arching, they're a reasonable, low-cost thing to try for four weeks.

How often should you do them?

No study above sets a frequency. A practical start: the moving drills before upper-body sessions, and the full table on two or three other days a week.
