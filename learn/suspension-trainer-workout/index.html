Suspension trainer workout: six moves on two straps, and what the muscle-activity studies measured

Two straps from one anchor point, and your body weight is the load. Here's what a suspension trainer is, what the muscle-activity studies found for the push-up, the row, the plank and the hamstring curl, a six-move session labelled practical, and how it fits a week.

Short answer: Hang two straps from a sturdy anchor, hold the handles, and use your body weight: push-up, row, plank, hamstring curl, squat and split squat, 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 (practical). A systematic review of 18 studies found that upper-body and core muscle activity ranged from moderate to very high across these exercises.1

A suspension trainer is a pair of straps with handles, hung from one anchor point such as a pull-up bar, a beam or a door anchor. You lean toward or away from the anchor, and the angle of your body sets how hard the move is. That's a description of the equipment, not a study result.

This page covers what the muscle-activity studies measured on suspended push-ups, rows, planks and curls, one training trial, a six-move session labelled practical, and how it fits a week. It uses the generic name: it's about the session, not the gear.

What the studies measured

Nearly all of this is muscle activity, measured with electrodes on the skin during a few reps. That's a snapshot of effort in one session, not muscle growth or strength over weeks.

  • The overview. A systematic review of 18 studies, which included only descriptive studies of physically active participants, looked at suspended push-ups, inverted rows, prone bridges and hamstring curls. Upper-body and core muscle activity ranged from moderate to very high, and for these exercises other than the inverted row, activity was greater with suspension than with the comparable traditional exercise. The review also says activity varied greatly between studies.1
  • The push-up. In 21 healthy adults, 15 men and 6 women, the suspended push-up produced higher activity than the floor push-up in the chest, the front of the shoulder and the triceps, and the authors suggest it may be considered an advanced variation of the push-up.2 Another study isn't as simple: in 29 young fit men who did push-ups on four suspension systems, a pulley-type system gave the highest activity overall, but the more stable setups seemed more appropriate for the chest.3 So a suspended push-up adds a balance demand, and which muscles benefit depends on the setup.
  • The plank. In 21 participants doing planks of 3 seconds, abdominal activity was higher in every suspended version than on the floor. It was highest in the arms-suspended and arms-and-feet-suspended versions, which did not differ. Rectus femoris, a hip flexor, was greatest with the arms suspended.4
  • The core, four moves. In adults described only as young and healthy (the abstract gives no number) doing four suspension exercises with a 5-second hold, hip abduction in a plank produced the highest activity in most of the abdominal muscles measured and the 45-degree row produced the lowest.5
  • Over weeks. In 36 untrained men, a circuit of seven weeks, three days a week, that used two instability devices (suspension straps and a half-dome balance trainer) was compared with a traditional resistance circuit. Squat and bench press maximums and jump height improved in both groups, and no significant differences were detected between the groups after training.6 That's untrained men and a mixed-device circuit, so it says the straps are a workable way to train, not that they beat the alternatives.

A six-move session

Practical, not a protocol from a trial. Check that the anchor and the straps hold your weight before you lean on them, and set the strap length first. Your body angle sets the difficulty, and it works in opposite directions: on the row, walking your feet toward the anchor makes it harder; on the push-up, lowering the handles does.

Do 2 or 3 sets of 8 to 12 of each, resting about a minute, and stop with a rep or two in the tank. (How to judge reps left.) The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, which covers healthy adults, advises at least two sets per exercise.7 The push-up, row, plank and curl are the four the review covered; the squat and split squat are my additions for the legs, and no study cited here tested them on straps.

When a set gets easy, change one thing: move your feet to make the angle harder, add a rep or add a set, not all three. Progressive overload has the logic.

How it fits a week

The World Health Organization asks adults for muscle-strengthening activity that involves all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.8 Two sessions of the six moves above cover the pushing, pulling, trunk and legs, and they work in a hotel room, a park or a doorway, since you only need an anchor. Common questions

Is a suspended push-up harder than a regular one?

In one study of 21 adults, the suspended push-up produced more activity in the chest, front of the shoulder and triceps.2 In another, of 29 young men, the more stable setups seemed more appropriate for the chest.3 Activity isn't the same as difficulty, so start with an upright body and lower it as it gets easier.

Can you build muscle with a suspension trainer?

The one training trial here measured strength and jumping, not muscle size: in 36 untrained men, a seven-week circuit that used straps and a balance device improved the squat and bench press maximums about as much as a traditional circuit did.6 Muscle growth comes from hard sets that you add to over time, whatever you hang from; progressive overload explains how.

Is it good for core?

Abdominal activity was higher in suspended planks than floor planks in 21 people, and the suspended hip abduction in plank gave the most in another study of adults described as young and healthy.4,5 Those are short holds measured with electrodes. The core workout has the rest of a core block.

Where do I hang it?

From a sturdy anchor such as a pull-up bar, a beam or the top of a door, using a door anchor made for the straps. Check it before you lean on it, and start with your feet planted close to the anchor and your body more upright.
