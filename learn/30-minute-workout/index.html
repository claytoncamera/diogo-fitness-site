A 30-minute workout: the full-body session that fits

Most full sessions run forty-five minutes to an hour. When you have thirty, here's the session that keeps what matters, in a gym or at home, and what to cut first when the clock is short.

Short answer: Thirty minutes is enough for a full-body session built around the big movements: warm-up sets of the squat, the squat itself, then a push paired with a pull and a hinge paired with a core move. Two or three times a week; add a rep or a little weight when the top of the range gets easy.

Lack of time is one of the more commonly reported reasons people stay away from exercise programs, and the usual program doesn't help: with a warm-up and stretching, traditional strength sessions often run past an hour, several times a week.1 Plenty of weeks don't have that. Thirty minutes is easier to find.

This page is the thirty-minute version: one full-body session with a gym and a home option for every exercise, when thirty minutes is enough and when it isn't, and what to cut first when the clock is short. Never lifted at all? The first four weeks covers the basics first.

The 30-minute session

Four movements cover the whole body: a squat, a push, a pull and a hinge, plus a core move. A 2021 review of time-efficient training goes further, saying all the major muscle groups can be trained with as few as three exercises: a leg-pressing movement like a squat, an upper-body push and an upper-body pull.1 The squat goes first, on its own. The other four go in pairs.

  1. Warm-up, about 4 minutes: two lighter sets of the squat, at about half and then three-quarters of your working weight.
  2. Squat, about 8 minutes: three sets, resting about ninety seconds between them.
  3. Pair 1, about 9 minutes: a push and a pull. One set of the push, straight into one set of the pull, then rest about a minute. Three rounds.
  4. Pair 2, about 9 minutes: a hinge and a core move, the same way. Three rounds.

Sets and reps: two sets of eight to twelve for the first two weeks, three from week three. At home, where the loads are lighter, work up to fifteen or twenty. Every set stops with two or three reps still in you. (Here's how to judge reps left.) Sore after the first sessions? Here's whether to train through it.

Why the squat stays on its own: it's the heaviest lift of the session, and the same review that recommends pairing exercises to save time advises against it for heavy free-weight lifts like the squat and the bench press.1 So the pairs here are machines, cables, dumbbells and bodyweight.

How long should a workout be?

For most people, a full session runs forty-five minutes to an hour, which is what how often to train plans around. Thirty is enough when the session is built around the big movements and the minutes go to sets, not to wandering between machines or a long warm-up.

What matters most is the weekly total, not the length of one session. The time-efficiency review concluded that weekly training volume is more important than how often you train, and that the work can be done in one session or several shorter ones, whatever suits you.1

When thirty minutes is enough

For most people training two or three days a week, it is. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand says even "minimal doses" of lifting bring substantial gains in strength, muscle and physical function.2 The time-efficiency review suggests at least four sets per muscle group a week, adding more when time allows.1 Two of these sessions a week is six sets each for the squat, push, pull and hinge; three is nine.

When it isn't

  • You want more weekly sets than short sessions hold. The ACSM review ties ten or more sets per muscle group a week to more muscle growth.2 Three of these sessions get close. Past that, add a session or lengthen the ones you have. (For chest and shoulders, a longer day looks like the chest workout and the shoulder workout.)
  • You're training for heavy strength. Heavy sets need long rests: the ACSM's 2009 position stand recommends three to five minutes between sets for heavy loading.3 A few heavy sets of one lift can fill most of thirty minutes.
  • It's all the exercise you do. Lifting is the strength half of the World Health Organization's guideline: muscle-strengthening on two or more days a week, plus 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (or 75 to 150 of vigorous).4 The days you don't lift are a natural place for the second half.

What to cut first

When the clock is short, cut in this order:

  1. The long warm-up. Shorten it; don't skip it. The time-efficiency review found little support for a general warm-up, like five to fifteen minutes on a bike, when time is short, but says warm-up sets of the exercise itself can be useful, particularly before heavy loads.1 That's why the warm-up here is two lighter sets of the squat. (The full warm-up, for longer days.)
  2. The stretching. The same review advises making stretching a priority only if flexibility is the goal.1 If you enjoy it, do it another time.
  3. The extra exercises. Four movements and a core move cover the whole body. The third chest exercise and the second curl variation are what goes.
  4. The waiting. Pair exercises instead of standing around. In a 2025 meta-analysis of 19 studies, supersets (two exercises back to back, then rest) allowed about the same number of reps and total load as regular sets in a shorter session, with similar long-term gains in strength and muscle size, though they felt harder.5 Pairs for opposing muscles, like a push and a pull, kept the most volume.5

What not to cut: the rest between sets of the same exercise, below about a minute. A 2024 meta-analysis of nine studies found a small benefit for muscle growth from resting more than sixty seconds between sets, and no clear difference beyond ninety.6 In a pair, each muscle rests for more than a minute anyway: the other exercise's set, then the rest. And don't cut the effort: a short session of easy sets is just a short session. When a client's week gets tight, I cut exercises before I cut effort.

How to progress a 30-minute workout

The clock stays at thirty. What changes is what you do inside it.

  • Add reps, then weight. When you reach twelve on every set with reps to spare, add a little weight and drop back to eight. The ACSM's guidance is a 2 to 10 percent increase once you can do one or two reps more than your target.3 (Progressive overload, explained.)
  • At home, add reps first, then slow the lowering to three or four seconds, then go single-leg or single-arm, then add load.
  • Add a session before you add an exercise. Going from two sessions a week to three is the biggest jump in weekly sets this plan has. Rest days explains how to space them.
  • Swap an exercise inside its slot after eight weeks or so if it goes stale. Keep the slots.
  • Write it down. Weight and reps for every set. You can't add a rep to a number you don't remember.

If your week won't hold even thirty minutes

Some weeks won't. A twenty-minute day can be the squat and the first pair, and it still counts: the World Health Organization's guidelines say some physical activity is better than none.4 Coaching is the version where your workouts live in the Trainerize app, sized to the days you actually have, and a weekly call looks at the week you just had and rebuilds the next one around what's coming. Coaching for a week that doesn't hold still explains how that works, and apply tells me about your week. Otherwise: block thirty minutes, set a timer, and start the warm-up sets.
