A kettlebell workout: one bell, two or three days a week

One kettlebell, five movements, two or three short sessions a week. Here's the plan, how to learn the swing as a hinge rather than a squat, what the research says about kettlebell training, and how to keep progressing before you buy a second bell.

Short answer: Pick one kettlebell you can goblet squat for ten clean reps. Two or three days a week, do five movements: a goblet squat, a swing taught as a hip hinge, a one-arm press, a one-arm row and a carry. Two or three sets of each, stopping with two or three reps left, and add reps before you add weight.

A kettlebell is a weight with the handle on top, so the load hangs below your grip instead of beside it. That makes it good at two things a dumbbell does less naturally: the swing, and holding the weight against your chest for a squat or a carry. One bell is enough for a real full-body plan.

This page is that plan: five movements, two or three days a week, with sets, reps and how to progress when you own only one weight. If you have dumbbells instead, the dumbbell plan runs the same idea; the squat itself is on the goblet squat page.

What kettlebell training does, and what it doesn't

  • Swings built strength. In 21 healthy men aged 18 to 27, a six-week trial of swing training twice a week, 12 rounds of 30 seconds with a 12- or 16-kilogram bell, raised half-squat strength about as much as jump-squat training did. Across both groups, strength rose 9.8% and jump height 19.8%.1
  • A barbell built more squat strength. In 30 healthy men training twice a week for six weeks, a kettlebell program (swings and goblet squats) and a barbell program (pulls, power cleans and back squats) both raised strength and power, but the barbell group gained more on the back squat. Jump height didn't differ between the groups.2
  • It carried over to other lifts. In 37 adults aged 18 to 72, ten weeks of group kettlebell training twice a week improved bench press strength compared with a group that didn't train. The clean and jerk only trended up, and the vertical jump didn't change.3

So a kettlebell is a real strength tool, and a barbell is still the better one for a heavy squat. The trials are small and short, and they measured strength and jumping rather than muscle size.

The swing is a hinge, not a squat

The swing looks like a squat with a weight in front, and that's the most common way to get it wrong. In seven men swinging a 16-kilogram bell, the swing was a hip-hinge pattern, with glute activity around 80% of a maximal contraction.4 In 30 recreationally active college-age adults doing hip-hinge swings, a heavier bell raised the demand at the hips, the lower back and the ankles, mostly because they pushed harder into the floor.5 And in 12 young adults who learned the swing from a video alone, a week of practice moved most of them to a pattern where the hips led the shoulders.6

  1. Set up. Feet a little wider than your hips, the bell on the floor about a foot in front of you. Hinge down: hips back, knees slightly bent, back straight, the same hinge as in deadlift form.
  2. Hike it back. Tip the bell toward you and pass it back between your legs, high, wrists close to your thighs.
  3. Snap the hips through. Stand up hard by driving your hips forward, glutes squeezed. The arms only guide the bell; it floats up to about chest height on its own.
  4. Let it fall and hinge again. Wait for the bell to come down, then push your hips back as your forearms meet your thighs. Stand tall at the top; don't lean back.

The plan: two days, alternated

Two sessions, A and B, each about thirty minutes. On two days a week, do A then B. On three days, alternate: A, B, A one week and B, A, B the next.

Stop every set with two or three good reps left. (How to judge reps left.) Rest about a minute between sets, a little longer after swings. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand advises at least two sets per exercise,7 so start with two sets in the first sessions and move to three once the movements feel familiar.

The press. A kettlebell press isn't a lesser shoulder exercise. In 28 college baseball players pressing the same 11.3 kilograms, side-delt activity didn't differ between a dumbbell and a kettlebell held the usual way; holding the bell bottom-up lowered it compared with the dumbbell.8 That's activity in one session, not growth. If your bell is too heavy to press with one arm, do pike push-ups in that slot until it isn't.

The carry. Walk tall without leaning toward the bell or away from it. The core workout covers what carries train, and the forearm workout what they do for your grip.

Which bell to start with

Pick the bell for your squat and your swing, since they're the biggest movements: one you can goblet squat for about ten clean reps with two or three left. The press usually needs a lighter bell than that, and the pike push-up covers it until you're ready. If you're between two sizes, take the lighter one and earn the swing with good reps.

How to progress with one kettlebell

With one weight, you progress by everything except the weight, roughly in this order:

  1. Add reps to the top of each range. In an eight-week trial of trained lifters, adding reps at the same load built muscle and strength about as well as adding load; the authors call both viable.9
  2. Add a set, up to three of everything.
  3. Slow the lowering on squats, lunges and Romanian deadlifts, or pause at the bottom. (What tempo does and doesn't do.)
  4. Go to one leg or one arm. A single-leg Romanian deadlift, a split squat with the back foot raised, a one-arm swing.
  5. Then a heavier bell, when the top of every range comes easily.

Staying on one bell longer than feels necessary isn't wasted time. In 28 people who already did high-intensity functional training, six weeks of swings with the same 20-kilogram bell raised deadlift strength and jump height about as much as adding 4 kilograms every two weeks (8.7% against 7.8% on the deadlift). Only the group that added weight improved deadlift endurance.10 One small trial in people who were already training, so it's a reason to be patient, not a rule.

Who a one-bell plan suits

  • Beginners who want one tool to learn the basic movements with, at home or in a corner of a gym.
  • People short on space or time. The bell lives under a desk, and a session fits in half an hour.
  • Lifters on the road or between gyms who want something that keeps their squat, hinge, press and row going.

It suits less well if your goal is a heavy squat or deadlift, where a barbell does more, or if your legs outgrow the heaviest bell you can get. Then keep the bell for swings and carries and add a gym day for legs. A kettlebell trains the whole body like any other load, and this plan is written for strength, not for one body part.

Common mistakes

  • Squatting the swing. Knees forward, hips straight down. Push the hips back instead, shins nearly vertical.
  • Lifting with the arms. If your shoulders are tired after swings, the hips aren't doing the work.
  • Leaning back at the top. Finish standing tall, ribs down, like the top of a deadlift.
  • Swinging too soon. Learn the hinge with a Romanian deadlift first, then swing.
  • Turning every session into a circuit. Rest between sets so each one can be hard.
  • Pushing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
