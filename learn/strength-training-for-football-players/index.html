Strength training for football players: what the studies in American football players measured, the lifts, and an off-season week

American football asks for strength, power and speed in short bursts, and the weight room has been part of the game for decades. Here's what the studies in college football players measured, what changed with years of lifting and what didn't, the lifts, an off-season week, and the version without a gym.

Short answer: Lift three or four days a week in the off-season: squats, presses and Olympic lifts, plus sprints and jumps. This page is about American football: the lifting a player does alongside practice and in the off-season. The strength and conditioning profession in North America can trace its roots to the sport, but a 2008 review notes that only recently had studies confirmed the links between strength, speed and power and success in it, and that most studies look at players' physical performance and training, with few on what happens over a season.1 So the evidence below is mostly college players, mostly men, and mostly what changed with training.

The game's demands depend on the position. In a 2016 study that tracked 33 college players with GPS across 12 regular-season games, wide receivers and defensive backs covered more total distance, more high-intensity running and more sprint distance, with more hard accelerations and decelerations, than the other position groups on their side of the ball.2 A lineman's week and a receiver's week can share the lifts and still differ in the running.

What separates players, by the numbers

Jumping stands out. In 40 college players, vertical jump correlated with playing ability, as ranked by their coaches, in every position group, and was the main predictor in each group's model.3 In 321 players tested at a professional league's pre-draft combine in 2004 and 2005, the drafted skill players were faster over 40 yards, jumped higher and were quicker in an agility shuttle and a cone drill than those not drafted; drafted linemen were faster over 40 yards, did better on a 225-pound bench press test and were quicker in the cone drill.4 Both are comparisons, not training trials: they show what better players had, not what made them better.

What years of lifting changed, and what it didn't

Strength climbs; speed is stubborn. Over eight years of testing, 289 Division III college players raised their one-rep max bench press 31.0% and squat 36.0% across their careers, and vertical jump was higher in the fourth year than in the first three, but 40-yard sprint and line drill times didn't change significantly.5 Seven years of preseason tests of Division I players showed the same pattern: every strength measure (bench press, squat, power clean and a 225-pound rep test) improved over four years, while 40-yard sprint speed didn't change significantly for linemen or skill players.6 Strength rose over the players' careers and sprint speed didn't, so the week below adds sprints as a practical choice, not a tested fix.

The off-season dose. In 51 experienced college players coming off 10 weeks of active rest, a 15-week off-season program raised the one-rep max squat, bench press and vertical jump by week 7 in all three groups, whichever way the weights and sets were varied, with no further gains from week 7 to week 15. The study didn't find a clear winner among the three programs.7

Olympic lifts or powerlifts. In one small trial, 20 college players trained four days a week for 15 weeks with either Olympic lifts or traditional powerlifts. Both groups improved their squat. Neither group's bench press, 40-yard sprint, agility or vertical jump changed significantly from start to finish, though the Olympic lifting group's change in vertical jump was significantly greater than the powerlifting group's.8 One trial isn't a rule: the Olympic lifts are a reasonable option, not a requirement.

An off-season week

A practical layout, not a protocol from a trial. Four lifting days match the Olympic-versus-powerlifting trial.8

DayLiftRun and jump
MondayLower: squats, Romanian deadliftsShort sprints, box jumps
TuesdayUpper: bench press, rows, pull-upsNone
WednesdayNoneConditioning or rest
ThursdayPower: power cleans, split squatsShort sprints, broad jumps
FridayUpper: push press, incline or dumbbell press, rowsNone
WeekendNoneRest or position work

Receivers and defensive backs, who cover the most ground in games,2 keep more of the sprint work; linemen keep the same lifts with shorter sprints. In season, a practical cut is two short lifting days around practice and games. Cardio and lifting covers the order and the gap between sessions.

Workouts for football players at home

Without a bar you lose the heavy squat and bench the studies tracked, so the home version leans on what's left. Practical picks: split squats and single-leg Romanian deadlifts with a loaded backpack, push-ups with the feet raised, pull-ups or inverted rows, broad jumps, and short sprints outside. The bodyweight workout lays out a full session, and the home gym workout plan shows what a pair of dumbbells adds.

Common questions

Will lifting make me slower?

Not in these studies, but it didn't make the players faster either: strength rose over college careers while 40-yard sprint times didn't change significantly.5,6 The week above adds sprints as a practical choice; the sprint workout covers them.

Do football players need Olympic lifts?

They're optional. In one small trial, the Olympic lifting group's change in vertical jump beat the powerlifting group's,8 and the power clean was one of the tested lifts in the Division I study.6 Practically, squats, presses and jumps can carry the program if the cleans aren't coached well.

How long is a football off-season program?

The off-season trials ran 15 weeks.8,7 In the periodization trial, strength came back within the first seven weeks after time off, and the authors suggested the rest period may need to be much shorter.7

What about in season?

There's less to go on: the 2008 review notes that only limited studies have looked at what happens to players over a season.1 A practical approach is to keep two shorter sessions and keep the main lifts in them.

An off-season only works when the lifting, sprinting and practice days fit together, and that's a scheduling problem as much as a training one.
