Foam rolling: what the research found, and where it fits around lifting

A foam roller is cheap, it feels like it's doing something, and most gyms have a pile of them by the stretching mats. Here's what the reviews of foam rolling actually measured, how big the effects were, how long to roll, whether to roll before or after you lift, and a five-minute routine.

Foam rolling is self-massage: you lie or lean on a firm foam cylinder and roll a muscle slowly over it, using your body weight as the pressure. A handheld roller stick does the same job with your arms. The research on both is larger than most people expect, and its verdict is plainer than most gym advice: real effects, mostly small, and mostly short-lived.

This page covers what the reviews found, how long to roll, where it fits around a lifting session, and a five-minute routine. The rest of what happens before your first heavy set is the warm-up, and the few minutes after your last one is the cool-down; each has its own page. One line before the details: if you're thinking about rolling because your lower back, a knee or the outside of your thigh hurts, that's a question for a doctor or a physical therapist. This page is about rolling healthy muscles as part of training.

What the research found

Six reviews cover the questions people actually ask. Each number below is the effect as its authors report it, with their own word for its size where they give one.

Rolling right before exercise

A 2019 meta-analysis pooled 21 studies, 14 of which rolled before exercise. Rolling first gave a small improvement in sprint performance (+0.7%) and flexibility (+4.0%), while the changes in jump height (−1.9%) and strength (+1.8%) were negligible.1 So rolling before you lift doesn't measurably help your strength, and it doesn't measurably cost you any either.

Rolling after exercise

In the same analysis, seven studies rolled after exercise. That slightly softened the drop in sprint (+3.1%) and strength (+3.9%) performance that hard exercise causes, and reduced how much muscle pain people reported (+6.0%), while its effect on jumping was trivial. The authors' overall reading: the effects of foam rolling on performance and recovery are "rather minor and partly negligible", and the evidence fits its use as a warm-up activity better than as a recovery tool.1

Range of motion, in the minutes after

A 2020 multilevel meta-analysis of 26 trials found that, compared with doing nothing, a bout of foam rolling had a large positive effect on range of motion right afterward (a standardized difference of 0.74). It was not better than stretching, though: compared head to head, the difference was essentially zero. How long or how fast people rolled made no significant difference, and rolling may have worked less well in men.2

Range of motion, over weeks

A 2022 meta-analysis of 11 studies (290 participants, average age 23.9) found that foam rolling programs increased range of motion compared with control groups (a moderate effect, 0.823). Programs longer than four weeks did better than shorter ones, and the effect depended on the muscle: rolling the hamstrings and the quadriceps added range, while rolling the calves didn't improve how far the ankle bent.3 A newer network meta-analysis of ankle studies ranked foam rolling more kindly for the ankle; the ankle mobility page covers it.

Against regular static stretching, a 2024 meta-analysis of 85 studies (72 of stretching, 13 of foam rolling) found both increased range with a moderate effect (1.006 for stretching, 0.729 for rolling), with no significant difference between them overall. For programs of four weeks or less, stretching did better: it showed a significant change, and rolling didn't.4

Performance, over weeks

A 2022 meta-analysis of the eight foam rolling training studies that lasted more than two weeks found no significant difference in performance between rolling and control groups, and no age, program length or amount of rolling under which it showed up.5

Put together: foam rolling is a fair way to get a bit looser for a session, a fair way to add range over a couple of months, and about as good as stretching at both. It isn't a performance tool, and it isn't much of a recovery tool.

Foam rolling before or after a workout?

  • Before lifting: fine, briefly. It loosens you up for the session without costing strength in the studies above. Treat it as part of the general warm-up and follow it with your warm-up sets, which do the job rolling can't. The warm-up guide covers both parts.
  • After lifting: if it helps you feel less sore. That's the one recovery outcome with a consistent signal: a 2019 systematic review of 22 studies found seven of the eight that measured soreness saw a short-term reduction.6 Whether to do anything at the end of a session is the cool-down page's question, and its answer applies here too: optional.
  • On a rest day or an evening: for range. If more range over weeks is the goal, rolling the same muscles for more than a month is what the 2022 analysis found worked.3 A stretching routine does the same job, and, in programs of four weeks or less, did it better.4

How long to foam roll

The 2019 review of rolling duration, which grouped 22 studies by outcome, found that about 90 seconds per muscle appeared to be the minimum for a short-term drop in soreness, with no upper limit found. For range of motion it couldn't recommend a dose: the results were inconsistent.6 The 2020 analysis agrees on that second point, finding that rolling longer didn't significantly change the gain in range.2

In practice: 60 to 90 seconds on each muscle you're about to train, and at least 90 seconds on each one that feels sore. Three or four muscles is five minutes. More time isn't wrong; the studies just don't show it buying more.

A five-minute foam rolling routine

Pick the rows for the muscles your session uses. Roll slowly, pause on a spot that feels tight for a few breaths, and keep the pressure firm rather than painful: shift weight onto your hands or the other leg to make it lighter.

MuscleHowBefore
CalvesSit with the roller under one calf, the other leg crossed on top for more pressure, hands behind you. Roll from just above the ankle to just below the kneeSquats, lunges, cycling
HamstringsThe same seated position, the roller under the back of the thigh. Roll from above the knee to below the glutes, toes pointed in, then outRomanian deadlifts, deadlifts
QuadricepsFace down on your forearms, the roller under the front of the thighs. Roll from above the knee to the hip; one leg at a time for more pressureSquats, leg press, lunges
GlutesSit on the roller, cross one ankle over the other knee and lean toward the crossed sideHip thrusts, squats
Upper backLie with the roller across your shoulder blades, hands behind your head, hips on the floor or lifted. Roll from mid-back to the top of the shoulder bladesPressing, rows
LatsLie on your side with the roller under the armpit and the bottom arm stretched overhead. Roll down a hand's length and backPull-ups, pulldowns, overhead pressing

Keep the roller on muscle, not on the lower back or on the joints themselves.

What foam rolling won't do

  • Make you stronger or faster over time. The training studies found no significant change in performance.5 Lifting does that; progressive overload is how.
  • Replace warm-up sets. Strength barely moved after rolling first.1 The sets of the lift itself are what prepare you for the heavy ones.
  • Speed up recovery much. The effects on recovering performance were slight.1 Rest between sessions does the bulk of the work; rest days covers where it goes.
  • Treat pain or an injury. Sharp pain, pain that lasts, or pain in a joint isn't something to roll out. See a doctor or a physical therapist.

Common questions

Is foam rolling better than stretching?

About the same. In the minutes after, the 2020 analysis found no difference between the two for range of motion,2 and over weeks the 2024 analysis found none overall, with stretching ahead in programs of four weeks or less.4 Do the one you'll keep doing.

Foam roller or massage stick?

Either. In the 2019 meta-analysis, 14 studies used foam rollers and 7 used roller massage bars or sticks. Foam rollers tended to do more for the recovery of strength, and before exercise the differences between the two didn't seem to matter in practice.1

Should you foam roll every day?

You can, but nothing here says you need to. The range gains came from programs that ran longer than four weeks;3 the soreness gains came from rolling after a hard session.6 Roll the days it serves one of those.

Does foam rolling hurt?

Firm pressure on a tight muscle is uncomfortable; it shouldn't be painful. Ease the pressure with your hands or the other leg. If a spot is sharply painful, stop rolling it and get it looked at.
