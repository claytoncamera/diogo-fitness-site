Holiday workout: what keeps your strength and fitness over a two-week break

The holidays take two weeks out of most training calendars: travel, family, a gym that keeps odd hours. Here's what the trials found two weeks off actually costs, the least training that held strength and fitness in them, and a two-week plan built on that minimum.

Short answer: Over a two-week holiday, one or two full-body sessions a week with the sets kept heavy is the least that held strength in the studies, and two weeks off cost little strength. In 20 resistance-trained young men, leg press strength didn't drop after two weeks without lifting.2

A two-week holiday is the most common break in a training year, and the question it raises is narrow: what does it cost, and what's the least you can do to keep what you've built? This page answers that from the studies that measured it. It's about training only. Food over the holidays has its own pages, and nothing here is a reason to skip the holiday.

The short version: two weeks off costs little, and a very small dose of training holds even that.

What two weeks off actually costs

  • Strength barely moved. In a 1993 study, 12 power athletes stopped lifting for 14 days. Their bench press (down 1.7%), squat (down 0.9%) and vertical jump didn't change significantly. Eccentric knee-extension force, the thigh's strength while the muscle lengthens under load, did drop, by 12%, and their type II muscle fibres shrank by 6.4%.1
  • The same in a newer trial. Twenty resistance-trained young men trained four days a week for four weeks, stopped for two, then trained four more. Leg press strength did not drop after the two weeks off, and it rose across the 10-week study.2
  • Under four weeks, strength holds better than size. A review of short breaks, under four weeks, found that training-induced gains in muscle fibre size are reversed but that strength declines are limited.3
  • Endurance fades a little faster. In seven endurance-trained people who stopped training, maximal oxygen uptake fell 7% in the first 21 days.4
  • Longer breaks cost more. A 2013 meta-analysis of 103 studies found the loss of strength grows with the length of the break, and is larger in people over 65.5

So two weeks is not a reset, and you don't need to train through a holiday at any cost. If you want to keep the habit, or you're older and the break matters more, the next section is how little it takes.

The minimum dose: how little holds it

A 2021 review asked exactly this question: what's the least exercise that maintains performance when time is short?6 Its answers, with the trials behind them:

What you want to keepThe least that held it in the studies
Strength and muscle size, younger adultsOne strength session a week, one set per exercise, with the load kept heavy for you: held for up to 32 weeks6
Muscle size, older adultsUp to two sessions a week and two or three sets per exercise6
EnduranceAs little as two sessions a week, or a third to two-thirds less volume (13 to 26 minutes a session), at the same intensity: held for up to 15 weeks6

Two trials show the pattern. In 70 adults who lifted three days a week for 16 weeks, cutting to a third or a ninth of that dose for 32 weeks preserved the muscle gained in the 20 to 35-year-olds but not in the 60 to 75-year-olds, and strength was largely retained even in the group that stopped altogether.7 And in 12 young adults who had trained six days a week for 10 weeks, the group cut to two days a week, at the same intensity and session length, kept their maximal oxygen uptake at the trained level for at least 15 weeks.8

The common thread, in the review's own conclusion: intensity seems to be the key variable, even when how often and how much you train drop a lot.6 Over a holiday, keep the sets heavy and let the number of sets and sessions fall.

A two-week holiday plan

A practical plan built on that minimum (the session layout is this page's choice, not a study's):

Each weekWhat to do
Session 1Full body, 20 to 30 minutes: a squat, a hinge, a push, a pull. One or two hard sets each, stopped with one to three reps left.
Session 2 (optional; keep it if you're older)The same session, two or three days later. Older adults: two or three sets each, as the review suggests.
CardioTwo sessions you'd call hard work, like intervals, a hilly walk or a run, at your usual effort, shorter than usual.
The other daysWalk if you can. Rest without guilt if you can't.

Where to do it depends on where the holiday is:

  • A gym nearby: your usual exercises, your usual weights, fewer sets.
  • A hotel or rental: the hotel workout has the session for a room with nothing in it and for a hotel gym with dumbbells.
  • At home, no equipment: the bodyweight workout, picking the harder version of each move so the sets stay hard. Short on time? The 30-minute workout fits a busy day.

Your usual plan uses a split? Run full body for the two weeks, then go back. On a split, a missed day is a missed muscle. (Which split for the days you have.)

The first week back

Pick up where the log stopped. If the first session feels heavy, take a set off each exercise and build back over the week; there's no need to start the plan over. Common questions

Will I lose muscle in two weeks off?

Some fibre-level shrinkage showed up in one study (type II fibres, 6.4% smaller after 14 days),1 and a review of breaks under four weeks found gains in fibre size are reversed while strength declines are limited.3 In these studies, size slipped before strength did.

Should I take the whole holiday off?

You can. The trials above say two weeks off costs little. If you like the routine, one or two sessions a week keep it going at almost no cost to the holiday.

Is a holiday the same as a deload?

It can stand in for one. The deload week explains the difference between a lighter week and a week off, and when each fits.

How many sets a week keep muscle?

How many sets per week covers maintenance volume across a normal training year, not just a holiday.

I'm older. Does the break matter more?

The studies say it does: the detraining meta-analysis found larger losses in people over 65,5 and keeping muscle size took more training in older adults.7 Keep both sessions in the plan above. (Workouts for seniors.) If you have a medical condition or a medical question, ask a doctor.
