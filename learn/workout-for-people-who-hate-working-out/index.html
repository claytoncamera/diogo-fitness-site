A workout for people who hate working out

You don't have to love it. You have to repeat it. Here's how small the useful version is, which of the free plans fits someone who'd rather not, what to leave out, and a way to keep it.

Short answer: If you hate working out, make it smaller, not better: two short full-body sessions a week from one of the free plans, one or two hard sets per lift, and a walk on the other days. You don't have to like working out. You have to do a small version of it often enough that it stops being an event. When I turned things around, the plan wasn't fancy: I started small, built small habits and showed up to the gym even on the days I didn't feel like going. Motivation was never the problem, and it won't be your solution.

So this page won't try to make you love training. It covers how small the useful version is, which free plan to pick if you'd rather not, what to drop, and how to keep it. The reasons the cycle repeats are on why you keep starting over and consistency vs. motivation.

Why most beginner plans fail people who hate training

Because they're written for people who like it: five days, an hour each, cardio at the end and a diet to match. If you hate working out, that plan lasts about two weeks, and then the gap between meaning to and doing takes over. In a meta-analysis of 10 studies and 3,899 people, 36% of everyone studied intended to be active and didn't follow through, an intention-behaviour gap the authors put at 46%.1

The guidelines are more forgiving than the plans. The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines say that for all populations, doing some physical activity is better than doing none, and that people who aren't meeting the recommendations should start with small amounts and gradually increase.2 The full adult recommendation is 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week (or half that much vigorous activity) plus muscle-strengthening work for all the major muscle groups on two or more days.2 Nothing in there about an hour, a split, or enjoying it.

How small the useful version is

Smaller than you'd think. A 2020 systematic review looked for the minimum training dose that raises one-rep-max strength in resistance-trained men. All six studies it found showed that a single set, done one to three times a week, was enough for significant strength gains, and the authors' conclusion was a single set of 6 to 12 reps at roughly 70 to 85% of a one-rep max, two or three times a week, taken to a high effort, for 8 to 12 weeks: smaller gains than a fuller program, but real ones, on the squat and bench press.3 Those were trained men, so for a beginner it's the direction rather than the number: one hard set per lift is a real workout, not a warm-up.

On which lifts, a 2021 narrative review on time-efficient training recommends big, two-sided, multi-joint movements and, at minimum, one leg-pressing exercise such as a squat, one upper-body pull and one upper-body push; it also advises keeping the warm-up to the exercise itself and only making stretching a priority when flexibility is the goal.4 Three movements, a short warm-up, done.

Which free plan to pick

Don't build one. Take one of these, do the shortest version it allows, and change nothing for a month:

  • Hate the gym more than the training? The bodyweight workout: a floor, a chair and a doorway, two or three times a week, at two sets of each until that's easy.
  • Have twenty or thirty minutes, not an hour? The 30-minute workout, and read its "what to cut first" section on the days you have less.
  • A pair of dumbbells at home? The dumbbell workout plan, at two sets instead of three.
  • Never lifted? How to start lifting weights, which is already built at two sets with reps to spare.

Whichever you pick, two sessions a week is the number. The WHO asks for muscle-strengthening on two or more days,2 and two is the one you'll keep. Each plan has a printable PDF; the plans page compares them by days and equipment.

What to drop

  • Cardio you hate. Walk instead. Walking is one of the guidelines' own examples of aerobic activity,2 and walking doesn't need a change of clothes. If you want a machine, pick the one you mind least.
  • The hour. One or two hard sets per lift, three lifts, and leave. The long version is for people who want to be there.
  • The elaborate warm-up and the stretching. The time-efficient review restricts the warm-up to one specific to the exercise you're about to do and treats stretching as a priority only when flexibility is the goal.4
  • The idea that it should feel good first. It may not. A systematic review of 24 studies found that feeling better during moderate exercise was linked to future activity, while how people felt afterwards had no relationship with it.5 That's an argument for keeping the session easy enough to tolerate while you're in it, not for waiting until you enjoy the idea.
  • Daily weigh-ins and the scale as the scoreboard. Count sessions, not pounds. The scoreboard is the log.

How to keep it without liking it

  1. Fix the days. "When I can" means never. Two days that already exist in your week, same times.
  2. Write it down. In a meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies with 19,951 people, prompting people to monitor their progress toward a goal helped them reach it, more so when the information was physically recorded.6 A log takes thirty seconds a session.
  3. Tell someone. The same review found larger effects when progress was reported or made public.6 A friend who asks on Sundays is enough.
  4. Shrink it on bad days, never skip it twice. One set of each lift still counts. Two misses in a row is the start of a new habit, so the next session happens no matter what.
  5. Add nothing for a month. When two sessions a week are boring, add a set. Boredom is the sign it's working.

Common questions

What's the best workout for someone who hates working out?

The one you'll repeat. In practice that means two short full-body sessions a week from one of the free plans above, one or two hard sets per lift, and walking on the other days. The research on minimum doses was done in trained men,3 so treat "one hard set works" as the direction and keep the effort honest.

Is 20 minutes enough?

For strength, a single hard set per lift two or three times a week was enough in the studies of trained men,3 and three lifts at one or two sets each fits inside 20 minutes. For the aerobic half of the guidelines, brisk walking on the other days can cover it.2

I hate exercise. Do I have to do cardio?

Not the kind you're picturing. The guidelines list walking among their examples of aerobic activity,2 so brisk walks can be your aerobic minutes, and the muscle-strengthening part is the lifting. If walking is the only part you can stand, start with that.

What if I hate the gym, not the training?

Train at home with the bodyweight workout or the dumbbell plan. If the problem is walking into a gym, the first day, step by step is written for exactly that.

Will a coach make me like it?

No, and I wouldn't promise it. What a coach does is write the plan around your real week, read your numbers and change the plan when your week changes, so the small version survives. With me, that's workouts in Trainerize, two weigh-ins a week, progress photos every two weeks, a weekly check-in call and direct access to me for questions. Here's how the accountability works.

If two sessions a week from a free plan is all you need, I'll say so. Otherwise: pick a plan, print it, and do the first session this week at a weight you could lift ten times.
