Sleep and muscle growth: what the studies measured

Sleep won't build muscle on its own, but short nights make the job harder. Here's how much sleep adults need, what lab studies found when sleep was cut, and how to train around a bad night.

Short answer: Adults should sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis.1 In lab studies, one night without sleep lowered muscle protein building the next day by 18%,2 and five nights of four hours in bed lowered it too.3 Those studies measured protein building over days, not muscle size over months, but they point the same way: short sleep works against the training.

Lifting is the signal to build muscle, and the hours after it are when the body answers. Sleep is a large share of those hours. This page covers how much sleep adults need, what happened to muscle protein building when sleep was cut in lab studies, how sleep loss changed performance, and what to do with your training after a short night.

How much sleep you need

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend that adults sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis.1 They add that more than nine hours may be appropriate for young adults, for people recovering from sleep debt and for people who are ill.1 There isn’t a separate number for people who lift; seven or more is the target to build the week around.

What short sleep did to muscle protein building

Two lab studies cut sleep and measured muscle protein synthesis, the rate at which muscle builds new protein:

  • One night without sleep. In 13 healthy young adults, a night of total sleep deprivation lowered the rate of muscle protein synthesis the next afternoon by 18% compared with a normal night.2
  • Five short nights. In 24 healthy young men, five nights of four hours in bed lowered the rate at which muscle built its contractile protein compared with a normal-sleep group.3 A third group had the same short nights but did three high-intensity interval sessions during the same five-day period, and their rate stayed at the level of the normal-sleep group.3

Both studies measured protein building over a day or a few days, not muscle size after months of training, and neither of the studies cited here followed lifters for months to measure muscle growth. So the honest reading is a direction, not a size: short sleep made the muscle-building process slower in the lab, and interval exercise during short sleep kept it going in one study.

What short sleep did to training

A 2022 review pooled 69 publications on acute sleep loss and physical performance, with 959 participants, 89% of them men. On average, sleep loss lowered performance by about 7.6%.4 The effect showed up in every category of exercise the review looked at, strength included.4

Two details matter for planning:

  • Which kind of short night. Consistent drops came after a night of no sleep, or after a night cut short at the end (a late bedtime was less clear-cut than an early alarm). After those two kinds of sleep loss, performance fell by about 0.4% for every hour awake before the exercise.4
  • When you train. The drops were consistent for afternoon and evening sessions, and morning sessions were less affected.4

What to do after a short night

These are practical rules, not study protocols:

  • Train, but don’t test. Do the session at your usual weights, or with one set fewer. A short night isn’t the day for a personal record. Leaving a few reps in the tank is easier with reps in reserve.
  • Train earlier if you can. Morning sessions were less affected in the review above.
  • Don’t chase the lost sleep with a lost session. One night won’t undo a block of training. The pattern across weeks is what counts, so protect the next night instead.
  • Fix the schedule, not the willpower. If short nights are the norm, move your training days to the ones that follow your better nights. How often to train and rest days help with the layout.

If you sleep badly for weeks, snore loudly or wake up exhausted after a full night, that’s a question for a doctor, not a training plan. I’m a fitness coach, not a doctor.

Common questions

Does sleep affect muscle growth?

The studies point that way. One night without sleep lowered muscle protein building the next day by 18%,2 and five nights of four hours lowered it too.3 They measured the building process over days, not muscle size over months.

How many hours of sleep do I need to build muscle?

The consensus statement gives no separate number for lifters. Its adult recommendation is seven or more hours a night on a regular basis.1

Should I skip the gym if I slept badly?

Usually not. Train at your usual weights or with one set fewer, and skip the max attempts. In the five-night study, the group that did three high-intensity interval sessions during the short nights kept its muscle protein building at normal levels.3 If you’re ill or exhausted, rest.

Do muscles grow while you sleep?

The studies above measured protein building across whole days, not only during sleep, so they can’t say what happens at night alone. What they show is that cutting sleep slowed it down.2,3
