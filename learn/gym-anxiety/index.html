Nervous about the gym? Your first day, step by step

You can feel nervous and still go. Here's what to do before you walk in, a first workout that's hard to get wrong, and what to do when it feels like the whole room is watching.

Short answer: You can feel nervous and still go. Decide your workout before you walk in, keep the first session short and easy, and leave when you planned to. If it's more than nerves, a licensed professional is the right call.

The hardest part of a first day at the gym might be the walk from the car to the door. After that, you're standing next to a machine, wondering what to do with it.

You can feel nervous and still go. One 2026 sport-psychology paper opens by noting that many gym-goers feel no such barrier, while some feel anxious and intimidated around other users.1 If that's you, a plan helps. Coming back after a long break? Getting back into training covers that. This page is about the nerves and the first day.

What to do before you walk in

  • Write the workout down first. Five or six exercises, in order, in your phone's notes or on paper. With a plan in hand, you're never standing around deciding what comes next.
  • Pick your time. Ask the front desk when the gym is quietest, and whether they offer a quick tour. Knowing where the water and the weights are answers a few questions before they come up.
  • Bring the basics. Water, a small towel, headphones, your phone with the workout on it, and shoes you can lift in.
  • Decide when you're leaving. Thirty to forty minutes is plenty for day one. Leaving on time is part of the plan, not a failure.

A first workout that's hard to get wrong

You don't need a perfect program on day one. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand says even "minimal doses" of lifting can bring substantial strength gains,2 so a short first session isn't wasted. Here's a simple one:

  1. Five minutes of easy walking or cycling to get moving. (More on warming up.)
  2. Leg press or goblet squat.
  3. Chest-press machine or push-up (hands on a bench if the floor is too hard).
  4. Seated row or lat pulldown.
  5. A hip hinge with light dumbbells (a Romanian deadlift).
  6. A plank, for as long as you can hold good position.

Do two sets of about ten reps on each, and stop every set with three or four reps left. If it feels like not enough, good. Next time is for adding. (Here's how to judge reps left.) If a machine has a diagram on it, follow it. If you don't know how to use something, skip it today. When you're ready for a plan past day one, how to start lifting weights covers the first four weeks.

Gym etiquette, in six lines

  • Put weights back where you found them. Rack the bar and the plates.
  • Wipe the bench or machine when you're done, with the spray and towels if the gym provides them.
  • If someone is waiting, don't sit on a machine scrolling your phone. Let them work in, or offer to alternate.
  • Give people space. Don't stand right next to someone mid-set.
  • Ask first. "Are you using this?" is an ordinary question, and a staff member can show you how a machine works.
  • Rules differ from gym to gym, so read the posted ones.

If you break one by accident, "sorry, still learning" is a fine thing to say.

Spotters: when to ask for one

A spotter stands by on a lift you might not finish, such as a heavy bench press or squat, ready to help you get the bar back on the rack if a rep stalls. On a first day with light weights you can skip this section. Later, if you take a heavy set close to failure, ask someone between their sets, tell them how many reps you're going for, and agree on when they should take the bar.

It may help the set, too. In a 2019 study, 12 recreationally trained people, average age 21, did three bench press sets to failure at 60% of their one-rep max, once with spotters in plain view and once with the spotters hidden. With the spotters in view they did 4.5 more reps in total, rated the effort lower and rated their confidence in the lift higher.3 One small study, on one lift. Training alone? What to do when it feels like everyone's watching

That feeling is worth taking seriously. In an online survey of 279 women, mostly current gym-goers, the researchers found that women often feel judged for their appearance and their performance.4

Here's the other side. In experiments, people asked to wear a T-shirt with a flattering or embarrassing image overestimated how many others would recall it.5 In four more experiments, people expected to be judged more harshly for a social blunder or public failure than they actually were.6 Neither paper is about gyms, and none of it means nobody ever looks. It does suggest you notice yourself more than anyone else does. Some things to do with that:

  • Give your eyes a job. Check your written plan between sets.
  • Headphones in. An easy way to keep to yourself.
  • Pick your corner. Start in the quieter part of the gym, not the middle of the floor.
  • Make an if-then. "If I feel watched, I look at my next exercise." Deciding ahead gives you something to do.
  • Bring someone. A friend, or someone who already trains there.
  • On a hard day, shrink it. Take the tour, do ten minutes, go home. That still counts as a visit.

One more thing, and it isn't nerves: if someone makes you feel uncomfortable or unsafe, tell a staff member. That's on them, not you.

The day after, and the week after

You may be sore. Soreness is common when you're first introduced to certain kinds of activity, and one review of the research advises easing into new kinds of exercise over a week or two.7 That's why day one is easy. Go back in two or three days: the World Health Organization's guidelines for adults include muscle-strengthening on two or more days a week.8 Here's what the days between are for. When it's more than nerves

A plan helps with everyday nerves. Some worry is different. The National Institute of Mental Health says feeling anxious is a normal part of life, and describes anxiety disorders as more than occasional worry or fear: the anxiety doesn't go away, shows up in many situations and can interfere with daily life, such as work, school and relationships.9 If that sounds like what's going on, or the worry follows you well beyond the gym, a licensed professional is the right call. I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor or therapist, so I can't tell you what's going on. Someone who can is worth talking to.

The same goes for your body. If you have an injury, a medical condition or a medical question, check with your doctor first. The World Health Organization's guidelines say adults with chronic conditions may wish to consult with a physical activity specialist or healthcare professional about what's appropriate.8
