Running for beginners: walk breaks, a talking pace, and minutes before speed

A first run goes better slower than you think. Here's how a beginner can start: walk breaks, a pace you could talk at, minutes added before speed, and what the studies of novice runners measured, with the pages that go deeper.

Short answer: Run-walk three times a week at a pace you could talk through, and add minutes before you add speed. The World Health Organization asks adults for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week and advises starting with small amounts, then increasing how often, how hard and how long.4

Running needs little more than a pair of shoes, and it's easy to start badly: a first run at the pace you remember from school, then a lot of walking home. A gentler start (a practical suggestion) is slower than that: alternate running and walking, keep the running at a pace you could talk at, and add time before you add speed.

This page is the starting point. It covers what the studies of beginners did, a sample first month, and where each next question lives. It's a page about how people start running, not a promise about how far or how fast you'll get; the studies below measured groups, and your own start depends on things a page can't see.

1. Start with run-walk, three times a week

Alternating running and walking isn't a lesser way to run; it's how the beginner program in the research was built. A Dutch 6-week program for adult novice runners, aimed at learning to run 3 km without stopping, had its participants train three times a week, with the running time and distance gradually increased through the weeks.1 A 2016 trial notes that alternating run and walk periods are often recommended to novice runners; in 42 non-elite runners who did a marathon either with a run/walk strategy or running only, both groups finished with similar times.2 The first month on this page uses the same shape. A first mile with the walking built in, week by week: how to run a mile without stopping. The longer version, eight weeks to 30 minutes: a beginner 5K plan.

2. Run at a pace you could talk at

The talk test is the beginner's pace gauge: if you can't say a sentence, slow down. In 16 physically active volunteers, 30-minute runs held at the last stage where they still passed the talk test produced intensities the authors judged compatible with training in healthy adults.3 That was a lab study of active people, not of first-time runners, so treat it as a rule of thumb. For a beginner that pace is often slower than it feels like it should be, and that's the point. What the pace is called and how to find it: zone 2 cardio.

3. Add minutes before pace

The World Health Organization's advice to anyone not yet meeting its numbers is to start with small amounts of physical activity and gradually increase frequency, intensity and duration.4 A practical order, not a protocol from a trial: first the third run in the week, then a few more running minutes inside a session, and only then a faster pace. Change one of the three at a time. When a week feels like too much, repeat the week before it; nobody is checking.

A sample first month

A practical layout, not a protocol from a trial. Three sessions a week with a day between them, each started with 5 minutes of brisk walking and finished with 5 minutes of easy walking. "Jog" means your talking pace.

WeekEach of the three sessions
1Jog 1 minute, walk 2 minutes, eight times
2Jog 2 minutes, walk 2 minutes, six times
3Jog 3 minutes, walk 2 minutes, five times
4Jog 5 minutes, walk 2 minutes, four times

The mile page picks up the same shape for a first mile, and the eight-week plan keeps going from week 4. Rest or walk on the other days (rest days), and keep a note of how each session felt (the workout log).

4. Where to run

In one lab study, 24 people running 1,600 m at 2.82 m/s (about 6.3 mph) used 481 kJ on a treadmill and, in a subgroup, 480 kJ on a track.5 One study at one speed doesn't pick for you; treadmill vs outdoor running has the rest of what the studies measured. If you'd rather walk first, running vs walking compares them and the treadmill workout has a walk-jog session.

5. Lift two days a week, too

The WHO's guidelines also ask adults for muscle-strengthening activity that involves all the major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.4 For runners that's a short lower-body session twice a week, and the evidence that it helps running performance sits mostly in trained runners (summarised on strength training for runners). If you've never lifted, the first four weeks of lifting is the place to start, and cardio and lifting covers how the two share a week.

What the studies of novices counted

The research on novices also counted injuries. In the Dutch 6-week program, 1,696 adult novice runners aged 18 to 65 kept a weekly log; 10.9% of them had a running-related injury during the program, which the study defined as a musculoskeletal complaint of the lower body or back, blamed on running, that hampered running for three consecutive sessions.6 That's a count in one program, not a prediction for you, and it's the only injury figure on this page. I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor: if something hurts and won't settle, or you have a medical condition, stop and ask one.

Common questions

How often should a beginner run?

Three times a week is what the Dutch beginner program used,1 and it leaves a day between sessions. That day-between spacing is practical, not a finding.

Is it okay to walk during a run?

Yes. Run-walk is the shape of the beginner program above,1 and in the marathon trial the runners who took walk breaks finished in a similar time to those who didn't.2 Walk as soon as you can't talk, and start running again when you can.

How long until I can run for 30 minutes?

No page can promise a timeline. The Dutch program was 6 weeks and aimed at 3 km without stopping.1 The eight-week plan on this site ends with 30 minutes of jogging, and it says to repeat any week that felt too hard. Your timeline is the number of weeks you keep doing it.

What pace should a beginner run at?

One you could talk at. The talk test is a rule of thumb that held up in a 30-minute lab study of physically active adults.3 A watch isn't needed; a sentence is.

Do I need special shoes or gear?

This page doesn't cover gear: none of the studies cited here tested a product, so there's nothing for me to cite.

If you'd rather have the week planned

Running three days a week, lifting two and still having a life is a scheduling problem as much as a training one. Coaching is the version where your workouts are built around the days you actually have, and a weekly call looks at the week you just had and rebuilds the next one around what's coming.
