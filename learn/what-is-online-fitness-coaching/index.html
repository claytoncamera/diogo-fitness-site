What is online fitness coaching, and how does it work?

A coach you may never meet, a plan on your phone, weekly check-ins.

Short answer: Online fitness coaching is a service where a real coach builds your training plan and nutrition guidance, you follow it in an app, and the coach reviews your numbers and adjusts the plan on a set schedule, all from a distance. It fits people who can get to a place to train but struggle to stay consistent.

Online fitness coaching is a service where a real person builds a training plan and nutrition guidance around your life, you follow it through an app, and that person reviews your numbers and changes the plan on a regular schedule. The coach and the client work together from a distance instead of in the same room.

"Online fitness coaching" sounds vague, and a lot of what's sold under that name is. A PDF with twelve workouts and a group chat is not coaching. So here's what it means when I say it, and what I'd expect from anyone who calls themselves an online fitness coach: a plan written for you, a place to follow it, someone who looks at your numbers every week, and a plan that changes when you do.

Below: what you get, what a week looks like, how online coaching compares with the other ways to get a plan, what the research says, how coaches charge, what to watch out for, and who it's for (and not for). If you only have a minute, read the first table and the "who it's for" section.

What an online fitness coach actually does

Strip the marketing away and online coaching is five things:

  • A program built for you. Your goal, your experience, the days you can train and the equipment you have. Not a template with your name typed in.
  • A place to follow it. Mine lives in Trainerize. You open the app, it tells you what's next, you log what you did.
  • Nutrition you can live with. Practical guidance on calories and protein, with no forbidden foods. (Here's how I think about the numbers.)
  • Check-ins. Weigh-ins, progress photos and a weekly conversation about what's working and what isn't.
  • Adjustments. The plan changes as you do. If you stall, travel, get hurt or get bored, the plan bends instead of breaking.

Underneath all five is the part that actually matters: someone is watching, and you know it.

Notice what's missing from the list: information. Workout plans and nutrition basics are a search away. What you're getting is the part that's hard to do alone, which is a plan that fits your real week and a person who notices when it stops working.

What a normal week looks like

With me, a typical week is two to four workouts on the days you picked, two weigh-ins (say Tuesday and Friday morning), progress photos every two weeks, and a weekly call where we look at the trend and decide what to change. In between, you message me when something comes up: a question about a lift, a restaurant, a week that fell apart.

That's it. No daily homework, no 5 a.m. ice baths. The job is to make the plan easy to follow and hard to quietly abandon. (Here's the first month, week by week.)

Here is how that breaks down:

  • In the app: your workouts, which you log as you go.
  • Twice a week: a weigh-in.
  • Every two weeks: progress photos.
  • Once a week: a call to look at the trend and decide what to change.
  • Whenever you need it: a message about a lift, a meal or a week that went sideways.

What the research says

I can't point you to a study of my coaching. What I can point to is the research on the pieces coaching adds: supervision, tracking and remote support.

Supervision

In a twelve-week study from 2000, men who did the same program under a trainer's supervision increased their training loads faster and gained more maximal strength than men who trained on their own.1 In a larger 2010 study of 124 untrained young men, the group with more supervision made greater strength gains, probably because more of them trained at maximum intensity.2

Tracking

On the nutrition side, a systematic review of self-monitoring found a consistent link between tracking (food, exercise or bodyweight) and weight loss. The same 2011 review called the level of evidence weak because of methodological limits.3 And a 2022 review of randomised trials found that adding a digital component to a home rehab exercise program improved adherence in seven of the ten trials (three found no difference), with the clearest benefit in the short term, from studies of low to moderate quality.4

Remote support

A 2025 randomized trial compared three ways of doing the same 10-week, three-times-a-week strength program in trained men and women (112 started, 79 were analysed): supervised, app-guided or self-guided from a PDF. The share who completed at least 26 of the 30 sessions was highest in the supervised group (88.2%), then the app group (81.2%), then the PDF group (52.2%).5 The supervised group also had greater squat gains than the other two. The authors concluded that app-based training "shows promise for maintaining adherence" and is a viable alternative when full supervision is not feasible.5

In a separate randomized trial of novice adults, people who got weekly coaching sessions over Zoom did more days of resistance training at four weeks than a comparison group that got no further contact. At the eight-week follow-up, the difference was no longer statistically significant.6

How to read this

Read the fine print. None of these studies tested a coach's full online service. The app group in the 2025 trial was app-guided, not a coach with a weekly call, and the paper compares supervised, app-guided and self-guided training. Both recent trials were short and about strength training. The tracking review rated its own evidence as weak. Online coaching is one way to get supervision, tracking and remote support into your week. The research doesn't say a coach is magic. It points one way: people who are watched, and who track things, tend to do better than people who aren't and don't, and a trainer in the room still came out ahead on the squat in that trial.

How coaches price it

Prices vary a lot, and I'm not going to put numbers on a web page. What I can describe is how coaches tend to structure it, so you can compare like with like:

  • Monthly. You pay each month and can usually stop when you choose.
  • A fixed-length program. You commit to a set number of weeks, often with a start and an end date.
  • What moves the cost. How often the coach talks with you, how much of the plan is built for you rather than reused, how quickly they reply to messages, and how many clients the coach takes on.

Ask any coach what is included each week, what happens if you want to stop, and whether the price covers everything or has add-ons. I don't hand out a price list. I'd rather talk with you first, and that conversation happens in the DMs. (The FAQ explains how I handle it.)

Red flags when choosing a coach

There's no license you can look up for "online fitness coach," so look at how they work:

  • Promised results, or any number of pounds attached to a number of days.
  • A plan you receive before anyone asks about your week.
  • Pressure to sign up today, or a price that drops if you decide right now.
  • Nowhere to see your plan or log your workouts.
  • No defined check-in rhythm.
  • Banned foods, "detoxes" or a very low-calorie plan with no doctor involved.

If any of those apply, keep looking. Here is the full checklist, with the questions to ask in a first conversation: how to choose an online fitness coach.

Who it's for, and who it isn't

It's a good fit if you keep starting over, your schedule is unpredictable, you want to understand the plan instead of just following it, and you're ready to do the work between check-ins.

It isn't a good fit if you need hands-on supervision for every session, you're rehabbing an injury that needs a physical therapist, you have a medical condition that needs clinical supervision for exercise or diet, or you want results without changing what you do during the week. A coach can't spot a heavy lift from another city, and a fitness coach is not a doctor. WHO's guidelines say adults with chronic conditions may wish to consult a physical activity specialist or healthcare professional about what's appropriate.7

In my experience, the people who do best online are the ones who can get themselves to a place to train but struggle to stay consistent and to know what to do once they're there. A good coach will tell you which group you're in. I'd rather tell you no than take your money and watch you quit. Worth it for you? Here's the honest version.

Fitness coach vs. personal trainer: the difference

The two titles get used for the same job, and neither one tells you what the person actually does. In everyday use, "personal trainer" means someone who trains you in sessions, usually at a gym, and is paid for the hour. "Fitness coach" tends to mean someone who runs your plan over weeks, often from a distance, and is paid for the plan, the check-ins and the adjustments. Plenty of trainers coach and plenty of coaches train, so the word on the business card tells you less than the substance.

Compare that instead: who writes the plan, who changes it and how often, whether anyone sees your form, and what happens between sessions. By that test, I'm a coach. Your program lives in Trainerize, I read your weigh-ins and photos, we talk once a week and the plan changes when your week does; nobody stands beside you for the hour. If the question underneath is whether you need either one, start with that.

Online coaching vs. a trainer at your gym

A personal trainer at a gym stands next to you for an hour, fixes your form in real time and makes sure you work hard for that hour. That's valuable, especially when you're new. It also only covers the hour. The other 167 hours of the week, including everything you eat, are on you. Online coaching flips that: less hands-on time, but the plan, the nutrition and the accountability cover the whole week.

If you've never touched a barbell and feel unsafe in a gym, a few sessions with a trainer at your gym first can be a smart investment, and online coaching can take it from there. Common questions

Is an online fitness coach the same as an online personal trainer?

Mostly, yes. "Online personal trainer", "virtual fitness coach", "online workout coach" and "online fitness coach" are used for the same kind of service, and the names don't tell you what's included. Check the substance instead: whether a person writes the plan for you, whether it changes as you go, and how often someone looks at your numbers. With me, that's a program in the Trainerize app, two weigh-ins a week, progress photos every two weeks and a weekly call.

How does online personal training work?

The same way online coaching does: a real person writes your program, you follow it in an app, and the coach reviews your numbers and adjusts the plan on a set schedule, all from a distance. With me, that's a program in the Trainerize app, two weigh-ins a week, progress photos every two weeks, a weekly call, and messages in between. "Virtual personal training" and "online personal training" describe much the same thing.

What are the benefits of online fitness coaching?

The practical ones: your plan is built around your week and the equipment you can get to, a person looks at your numbers instead of leaving you to guess, and someone notices when you go quiet. The limits are real too: nobody is in the room, so form feedback comes by video, and a few sessions with a trainer at your gym can be a good start if you've never touched a barbell and feel unsafe in a gym.

Is online fitness coaching worth it?

It can be, if what's missing is structure, feedback and someone who notices when you go quiet. If you already train consistently on your own, it's probably not worth it yet. I wrote out how I'd decide: is an online fitness coach worth it?

Does online fitness coaching work?

No study has tested a coach's full online service, so the honest answer is about the pieces it adds. Supervised men gained more strength than men training on their own,1,2 and an app-guided group completed more of its sessions than a group following a PDF.5 A trainer in the room still came out ahead on the squat in that trial.

Can a beginner do online coaching?

Yes, with one caveat. If you've never touched a barbell and feel unsafe in a gym, a few sessions with a trainer at your gym first can be a smart investment, and online coaching can take it from there. See coaching for beginners and how to start lifting weights.

Do I need a gym?

Your program is built around the equipment you have, so the answer depends on what you can get to. If you're short on equipment, see what a plan looks like with a pair of dumbbells or no equipment at all.

How do I get form feedback online?

If I need to see your form, you send a video and I send notes back. That is slower than a trainer in the room, and in the 2025 trial of 79 trained men and women, the supervised group's squat gains were greater than those of the app and PDF groups.5 It's one reason some people start with a few sessions with a trainer at your gym.

How often will I talk to my coach?

With me, there's a weekly call, and you can message me in between with questions. Whatever coach you choose, get the rhythm in writing before you pay: when the next call is, and the one after that.

How long until I see results?

I can't give you a date, and no coach honestly can. What I can say is that your numbers are tracked from the first week, so you'll know whether things are moving. (Here's how I think about timelines.)
