Kettlebell swing: a hinge you snap, not a squat you lift

Hike the bell back between your legs, snap your hips forward, and let it float to chest height. It's a hip hinge done fast, not a squat and not a front raise. Here's how to do it, what the studies found it works, the two styles, one arm or two, what weeks of swings changed, the faults, and the sets.

A kettlebell swing is a hip hinge done fast. You hold the bell in both hands, hike it back between your legs, then stand up hard so your hips send it forward and up to about chest height. It falls back, you hinge again, and the next rep starts. Your arms hold on; your hips move the bell.

This page covers the steps, the muscles the studies measured, why the hinge matters, chest height or overhead, one arm or two, what weeks of swing training changed, the faults, and the sets. Where the swing sits in a full plan for one bell is on the kettlebell workout.

How to do a kettlebell swing

  1. Set the bell about a foot in front of your toes, with your feet a little wider than your shoulders.
  2. Hinge to reach it: push your hips back, bend your knees a little, keep your back flat, and take the handle in both hands.
  3. Hike it back between your thighs, high, like snapping a football, until your forearms touch the insides of your thighs.
  4. Snap your hips forward. Stand up hard, squeezing your glutes, knees straight. The bell floats forward on straight, relaxed arms.
  5. Stand tall at the top, the bell at about chest height, your body in a straight line. Don't lean back.
  6. Let it fall. Wait until your forearms meet your thighs, then hinge and let the bell travel back between your legs. That's one rep. To stop, let it swing back once more and set it down in front of you.

The bell should feel weightless for a moment at the top. If you have to lift it there with your shoulders, the hips didn't do enough, or the bell is too heavy for now.

Kettlebell swing muscles worked

The swing is a back-of-the-body exercise. In seven men swinging a 16-kilogram bell, the swing was a hip-hinge pattern, with glute activity around 80% of a maximal contraction and the lower-back muscles around 50%.1 In 23 healthy college-age adults doing one-hand and two-hand swings, glute activity averaged about 75% of a maximal contraction, the side glute about 55%, and the biceps femoris, one of the hamstrings, about 79%; the hamstring switched on first.2

Within the hamstrings, the swing leans to the inner side. In 16 elite female handball and soccer players tested on 14 exercises, the swing and the Romanian deadlift both worked the inner hamstring, the semitendinosus, harder than the outer one, by 17 to 22%, at very high levels of activity.3 So the swing trains the same chain as the Romanian deadlift: glutes and hamstrings moving the hips, with the back muscles holding the position.

Hinge, don't squat

A common way to get the swing wrong is to squat it: knees forward, hips straight down, the bell lifted with the arms. In 14 people who swung three ways, the hip-hinge swing produced more hamstring activity than a squat-style swing or a swing driven by straightening the knees.4 That's one session of activity, but it fits what the swing is for. If your thighs burn more than your glutes and hamstrings, push your hips back further and keep your shins closer to vertical. Learning the hinge slowly first, with a Romanian deadlift, makes the fast version easier to find.

Chest height or overhead

The swing on this page stops at about chest height, sometimes called the Russian swing. The American swing carries the bell all the way overhead. In 15 healthy adults, the overhead swing took 34% longer per rep, while peak hip loading was close in the two kettlebell styles (2.34 against 2.32, in units of body weight times height) and peak joint angles didn't differ.5 In 15 physically active women swinging 12- and 16-kilogram bells, the overhead swing moved the bell further and produced more peak power and work at the hip. The authors suggest moving up in bell weight before switching to the overhead style.6

So learn the chest-height swing first and progress it with a heavier bell. The overhead version is an option, not a step you have to take.

One arm or two

The one-arm swing is the same hinge with one hand on the handle and the other arm free. In 16 healthy men swinging a 16-kilogram bell, the one-arm swing worked the upper part of the muscles along the spine 24% harder on the side opposite the bell than on the bell's side; activity in the obliques and the lower back muscles was similar across the versions.7 Learn it with two hands. Once those feel smooth, one-arm sets add a twist your trunk has to resist; switch hands every set. The slow one-arm skill with a bell is the Turkish get-up.

What weeks of swings changed

  • Strength and jumping. In 21 healthy men aged 18 to 27, a six-week trial of swing training twice a week, 12 rounds of 30 seconds with a 12- or 16-kilogram bell, raised half-squat strength, and the gain didn't differ significantly from jump-squat training's. Across both groups, strength rose 9.8% and jump height 19.8%.8
  • A barbell did more for the squat. In 30 healthy men training twice a week for six weeks, a kettlebell program (swings and goblet squats) and a barbell program (pulls, power cleans and back squats) both raised strength and power, but the barbell group gained more on the back squat. Jump height didn't differ between the groups.9
  • Men and women both gained. In 28 men and 25 women who already did high-intensity functional training and had used kettlebells for at least six months, twice-weekly swings for 12 weeks went with deadlift max gains of 12.6% in the men and 11.7% in the women, and jump height gains of 12.9% and 6.8%. Everyone kept up their usual program, and there was no group without swings, so the gains can't be put on the swings alone.10
  • Not sprinting, in one trial. In 18 recreationally active women, eight weeks of swings didn't significantly change 30-metre sprint times, and neither did stiff-legged deadlifts done in the same interval format; jump height rose across both groups.11

Why the swing is used as a power exercise: in 16 men, swing power was greater than back squat power and largely comparable with jump squat power, and the heaviest bell tested, 32 kilograms, gave the highest net impulse, the total push into the floor.12 The trials are short and small, and none of them measured muscle growth. Read the swing as a strength-and-power exercise for the hips, not a replacement for a heavy squat or deadlift.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Squatting the swing. Knees forward, hips down. Push your hips back, keep your shins nearly vertical, and let the hamstrings stretch.
  • Lifting with the arms. If your shoulders are tired after a set, the hips aren't doing the work. Think "stand up," not "raise the bell."
  • Leaning back at the top. Finish standing straight, glutes squeezed, ribs down. The bell stops at chest height because your body is upright, not because you pulled it.
  • Hiking low. The bell drops toward your knees on the way back and drags your back round. Keep the hike high, wrists close to your groin.
  • Hinging too early. Wait for your forearms to meet your thighs before you bend. Hinging while the bell is still up throws it at your knees.
  • Swinging through something that hurts. Tired hamstrings and glutes are normal; a sharp or lasting hurt in the back or the hip isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • Three to five sets of 10 to 15, resting about a minute, or the format from the trials above: 30 seconds of swings, 30 seconds of rest, building toward 12 rounds. Stop a set when the hike gets low or the top gets sloppy.
  • Early in the session. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found power was enhanced by moderate loads and a fast lifting phase, and strength by doing an exercise at the beginning of a session.13 Swings fit after a warm-up and before slower lifting.
  • Pick the bell for the hinge. Heavy enough that your hips have to work; light enough that it floats at the top. Add reps or rounds before you buy a heavier bell. (Progressive overload covers the options.)
  • Count it as a hinge. On a day with heavy deadlifts or Romanian deadlifts, swings are extra hinge work; keep the sets lower.
