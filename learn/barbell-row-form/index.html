Barbell row form: hinge, hold still, pull to your stomach

A barbell row is a hinge you hold while you pull. Get the hinge right and the row is simple: the bar travels close to your legs up to your stomach and back down. Here's the setup, the grip questions, the Pendlay version, and the faults that show up as the weight climbs.

Short answer: Hinge forward with soft knees until your torso is between about 45 degrees and parallel to the floor, back flat, bar hanging under your shoulders. Pull it in a straight line to your lower ribs or upper stomach, then lower it to straight arms, torso still the whole set. Two or three sets of 6 to 12.

A barbell row is a horizontal pull done from a hinge: you bend forward at the hips, hold that position, and pull the bar from arm's length to your stomach. The pull is the easy part to describe. What makes or breaks the lift is holding the hinge still for a whole set, which is why the back workout says to learn the hinge before you load this row.

This page covers the setup and the bar path, overhand or underhand, how wide to put your hands, whether the row can lean toward your upper back, the Pendlay row, the common faults, and where the lift fits in a week. Its one-arm cousin is the dumbbell row. Pulling the bar straight up the front of your body, standing tall, is a different lift: the upright row.

How to set up and pull

  1. Start standing with the bar. Deadlift it from the floor or lift it out of a rack at about mid-thigh. Feet about hip-width apart, the bar over the middle of your feet.
  2. Hinge. Push your hips back with your knees softly bent and let your chest come forward, as in the Romanian deadlift. Stop somewhere between about 45 degrees and parallel to the floor. Lower is harder to hold and gives a longer pull; pick the angle you can keep for every rep.
  3. Set your back. Flat, from your head to your hips, with your midsection braced. Look at the floor a little ahead of your feet rather than up at the mirror.
  4. Let the bar hang at arm's length, under your shoulders, close to your legs.
  5. Pull it in a straight line to your lower ribs or upper stomach. Your elbows travel back past your torso, and the bar stays close to your thighs on the way up.
  6. Lower it under control until your arms are straight again. Your torso stays where it was the whole time. That's one rep.

The bar path is the check: if the bar swings forward away from your legs, or you have to stand up to get it to your body, the weight is ahead of you.

Overhand or underhand?

Overhand (palms facing you, toward your legs) is the default. An underhand grip, palms facing forward, usually keeps the elbows closer to your sides and the bar a little lower on your stomach. Both are fair choices.

What the research says is thin, because almost nobody has measured it on the barbell row itself. On the lat pulldown, in 12 men, a palms-away grip produced more lat activity than a palms-toward-you grip, with no difference in the muscles between the shoulder blades or the biceps.1 In another 12 men who held each pull still for ten seconds, a reverse-grip pulldown had a lower lat-to-biceps ratio than the wide-grip pulldown or the seated row.2 Those are pulldowns, not rows, so read them as a hint: overhand may lean slightly more on the lats. If underhand lets you pull more weight with a steadier torso, use it, and rotate between the two if you like.

Where to put your hands

Just outside shoulder width is the usual starting point, with your forearms roughly vertical at the bottom. Two studies looked at width:

  • In 20 active male university students rowing at 60% of their max, a wide grip, one and a half times the distance across the shoulders, produced more lat activity than a shoulder-width grip.3
  • On a chest-supported barbell row, a conference abstract reported that a wide overhand grip worked the middle trapezius more than narrow or underhand grips, with no difference in the lats.3 A 2026 review that reports both calls the width finding a single study and the abstract preliminary; the abstract doesn't report its sample size in full.3

So width matters a little, and the two studies don't agree on the lats. Pick a width where your wrists stay straight and the bar meets your body without your elbows flaring straight out to the sides, and keep it the same every session so the weights you log are comparable.

Barbell row for your upper back

The row already works the upper back: the middle and lower trapezius and the rhomboids pull your shoulder blades back, the rear deltoids help pull your arms back, and the lats pull your upper arms toward your hips. Two changes shift some of the work upward:

  • Hands wider, elbows out. The bar meets you higher, around the bottom of your chest, and the elbows travel out at roughly 45 degrees. A wide overhand grip is the setup the chest-supported abstract above linked with more middle-trapezius work.
  • Hands closer, elbows in. The bar meets you lower, at your stomach, and the elbows stay close to your sides. In separate studies of the seated row, a narrower grip and elbows kept lower and closer to the body were each linked with more lat activity.3

Should you squeeze your shoulder blades together at the top? In the held-pull study above, actively pulling the shoulder blades back on the seated row didn't change activity in the muscles between them.2 Pull your elbows back, let your shoulder blades follow, and don't force an extra squeeze.

Pendlay row vs bent-over row

  • Bent-over row. The bar stays off the floor for the whole set, and you hold the hinge from the first rep to the last. Your torso can sit anywhere from about 45 degrees to parallel.
  • Pendlay row. Torso about parallel to the floor, and every rep starts from a dead stop with the plates on the floor. You pull the bar up to your stomach, lower it back to the floor, reset your back, and go again. The pause lets you reset your position each rep.

No study has compared the two: a PubMed search for "Pendlay" turns up no study of the lift. Treat them as two ways to load the same pull. The Pendlay row suits anyone whose back position slips on a long bent-over set; the bent-over row suits anyone who wants constant tension and a hinge they can hold.

A Smith machine row runs the bar on a fixed track, a T-bar row pivots it from the floor, and a chest-supported row takes the hinge away entirely. In a free-weight row, more of you has to hold still: in 15 resistance-trained men, a two-arm free-weight bent-over row worked the muscles along the spine about a third more than a two-arm machine row did.4 That's also why a chest-supported row makes sense on a day that already has a heavy hinge. And free weights aren't required for results: in an eight-week trial of 38 resistance-trained men, a free-weight program and a machine program that included a prone bench pull produced similar strength gains, and similar growth in the thigh, chest and abdominal muscles measured.5

Common faults, and a fix for each

  • Standing up as you row. Your torso rises with every rep until the row is half a shrug. Pick a weight you can pull without your chest coming up, and keep your hinge angle from rep one to the last.
  • Using momentum. A hip snap at the bottom throws the bar up. Pull smoothly; lower it in about two seconds.
  • The bar drifting forward. It swings out over your toes. Keep it close to your legs the whole way.
  • The back rounding as the set gets hard. Keep it flat and in the position you set, and end the set there. Film a set from the side if you can't tell.
  • Cutting the range short. Arms straight at the bottom, bar touching your body at the top. In 16 resistance-trained men on a chest-supported barbell row, rowing only the top half or only the bottom half changed muscle activity by medium and inconsistent amounts compared with the full range.6 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found strength was enhanced by training through a complete range of motion.7 Use the full row.
  • Rowing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

How it fits the back workout

The barbell row is one option for the horizontal-pull slot in the back workout, alongside a vertical pull such as the lat pulldown and a hinge. A few numbers to start from:

  • Two or three sets of 6 to 12, ending each with a rep or two in reserve. (How to judge reps left.) The ACSM's 2026 stand advises at least two sets per exercise, and found muscle growth was enhanced by ten or more sets per muscle group a week.7
  • Add weight in small jumps once every set reaches the top of your range with your hinge unchanged. (How much weight to start with.)
  • Mind the hinge budget. If the same session has heavy deadlifts or Romanian deadlifts, your back has already held a hinge for several sets. Row second, go lighter, or swap in a chest-supported or one-arm row that day.

Common questions

Can you do barbell rows on a Smith machine?

Yes. A Smith machine row runs the bar on a fixed track; in a free-weight row, more of you has to hold still.4
