Bodyweight squat: the form, the progression, and when it stops working

No weight, no rack: a floor and a chair. The bodyweight squat is where most people learn the pattern, and it carries you further than you'd think if you keep it hard. Here's the form in short, the to-bench progression, tempo and pauses, what the studies say it builds, and when to move on.

Short answer: Feet about shoulder-width apart, toes turned out a little, arms forward for balance. Sit down between your heels as deep as you can keep them on the floor, then stand. Start to a bench and lower it in steps. Sets of 8 to 20; once 20 is easy, slow the lowering, pause at the bottom, or add a weight.

A bodyweight squat is the squat with nothing in your hands and nothing on your back: you sit down between your heels and stand back up. It's the standard squat in the bodyweight workout, the step before a loaded squat, and a quick look at how your hips and ankles move.

This page covers the form in short, the to-bench progression, tempo and pauses, what bodyweight squats build and what they don't, and what comes after. The checkpoints for a barbell squat are on squat form, and the first squat with a weight is the goblet squat; the pattern here is the same one, without the load.

The form, in short

  1. Feet about shoulder-width apart, toes turned out a little. Adjust until the bottom position feels balanced.
  2. Arms straight out in front, or hands together at your chest. Reaching forward gives you something to balance against as you sit back.
  3. Brace your midsection, then sit down between your heels. Hips and knees bend together, and your knees travel the way your toes point.
  4. Go as deep as you can with your heels on the floor and your chest up.
  5. Stand by pushing the floor away, chest and hips rising together. That's one rep.

Your knees will move forward past your toes, and that's normal; squat form has the research on it. If your heels lift or your chest drops before you reach depth, the limit may be your ankles or your hips: ankle mobility and hip mobility each have a simple check.

The to-bench progression

If a full squat feels unsteady, or your heels won't stay down at depth yet, squat to a bench and lower the bench in steps:

StepSquat down toMove on when
1A chair seat at about knee heightFour sets of 10 clean reps, two sessions in a row
2A lower bench or step, a few inches below the kneeThe same, touching without sitting
3A low box or a stack of books, thighs about level with the floorThe same
4No bench: a full squat20 clean reps, then the tempo and pauses below

Touch, don't sit. In 8 resistance-trained men lifting a bar, a squat to a box and a regular squat showed minimal differences in force, power and muscle activity.1 A light touch on the bench, then straight back up, keeps it a squat with a marker at the bottom. It's the same rule as the goblet squat to a bench. Sitting and pausing on the box is a separate lift, the box squat.

The move-on rule comes from the one training trial of progressive bodyweight squats below, where people moved up a level after two sessions in a row with four sets of 10 clean reps.2 And the reason to keep lowering the bench is depth: a 2021 meta-analysis of 16 studies found that training through a full range of motion built more strength and more lower-body muscle than partial reps.3

Tempo and pauses

With no weight to add, time is the lever. Two ways to make the same squat harder:

  • Slow it down. In 16 healthy young men squatting a light load, half their max, eight weeks of slow reps, three seconds down and three up without a pause, grew the thigh muscles by 6% to 10% and raised the squat max by 10%. The same load at normal speed, with a one-second pause, changed none of the measures.4
  • Pause at the bottom. In 26 men who trained the squat for ten weeks, a pause of about two seconds at the bottom of each rep brought larger gains on most of the strength and bar-speed tests, by effect size, than bouncing out of the bottom.5

Neither is magic. A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 studies found that rep tempo, on its own, had minimal effect on muscle growth.6 What slow reps and pauses do is make a light set hard in fewer reps, which is exactly the problem a bodyweight squat has. A practical version: three seconds down, a two-second pause, then stand. Held against a wall, it's the wall sit. Count the seconds out loud; it keeps the reps honest.

What bodyweight squats build, and what they don't

  • Strength and muscle, if the version keeps getting harder. In a small trial, 13 sedentary young women trained twice a week for six weeks with either barbell back squats at 60% to 80% of their max or bodyweight squats that progressed through ten levels, from two legs to one. Both groups gained knee strength and muscle thickness, with no significant difference between them.2 Six people did the bodyweight version, so read it as a sign, not a verdict.
  • Effort matters more when the load is light. In 25 untrained men doing one-leg knee extensions with a light load, sets taken to failure, about 34 reps, built muscle; sets stopped at about 20 reps showed no significant growth.7
  • A load does more per rep. In 19 recreationally active men, every loaded squat produced more muscle activity than the squat with no load.8
  • It isn't the tool for maximal strength. A review of 21 studies found that light and heavy loads built similar muscle when sets went to failure, but heavy loads built more one-rep-max strength.9

Read together: the bodyweight squat builds the legs as long as each set ends close to failure and the version keeps getting harder. It runs out first for maximal strength, and later for muscle, once even the hardest version you can do takes more than 20 reps to get hard.

When it stops working, and what comes next

The signs: 20 clean reps with a slow lowering and a pause still leave reps to spare, or your breathing gives out before your legs do. To train your heart and lungs instead, try burpees. The next steps, roughly in order:

  • Add a weight. One dumbbell or kettlebell at your chest is the goblet squat, and the same form carries over.
  • Go to one leg. A split squat, then the Bulgarian split squat with your back foot on a couch, as in the bodyweight workout table. The far end is the pistol squat, a full squat on one leg.
  • Pair it with a hinge. As the leg day explains, a squat leaves most of the back of the thigh to a hinge. At home, that's a glute bridge or a bodyweight good morning.
  • Push something. If your gym has a sled, the sled push is hard leg drive with no bar on your back.

Sets and reps

  • Two or three sets of 8 to 20, stopping with one to three reps left. (Here's how to judge reps left.) The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand advises at least two sets per exercise.10
  • Fewer than 8 good reps: go back up a bench height. More than 20 with reps to spare: slow the lowering, add the pause, or move to the next step.
  • Two or three times a week, on days that aren't back to back, as in the bodyweight workout.
  • Write it down: the bench height, the tempo and the reps, so the next session starts where this one ended.

Common mistakes

  • Heels lifting. Your weight drifts onto your toes. Sit back between your heels, and try a slightly wider stance.
  • Knees caving in. Push them out in the direction of your toes.
  • The chest dropping. Reach your arms forward and brace before each rep.
  • Depth that shrinks as the set goes on. Use the bench as a marker until every rep reaches it.
  • Bouncing out of the bottom. Control the lowering; the pause is a fix as well as a progression.
  • Staying at 20 easy reps. Once a set stops being hard, it stops doing much. Take the next step.
  • Squatting through something that hurts. Sore the next day is normal; a sharp or lasting hurt isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
