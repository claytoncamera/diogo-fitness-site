Can't afford a personal trainer? Four honest options, three of them free

You can train well without paying anyone. Here's what the free options cover, what they don't, and the one case where help is the thing that stops costing you. No price list on this page, and no sales pitch dressed up as one.

Short answer: If you can't afford a personal trainer, you can still train well: pick one free plan, keep a log, and tell someone your numbers each week. That covers the plan and most of the tracking. What it doesn't cover is someone who changes the plan when your week breaks and notices when you stop, which is the part people end up paying for in restarts.

You don't need to pay anyone to get in shape. The guidelines are public, the plans on this site are free and printable, and the rule for getting stronger fits on one page. I'm a coach, so you'd expect me to argue the opposite. I'd rather be useful: here's what the free options cover, what they don't, and the one case where a coach is the thing that stops costing you.

One thing this page is not: a price page. I don't publish a price list, and nothing here compares what anyone charges. If you want to know how I handle the money question, the FAQ explains it.

First, what a trainer would actually give you

When people say "personal trainer" they usually mean someone in the room: a plan, someone watching the session, a booked appointment you'd feel bad skipping, and a pair of eyes on your technique. Some of that is free to replace. Some of it isn't, and it's worth being clear which is which before you decide what to do.

  • The plan: free. The World Health Organization's guidelines for adults are 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week (or half that much vigorous activity), plus muscle-strengthening work for all the major muscle groups on two or more days.1 A lifting program that meets that is below.
  • The progression: free. Add reps, then weight. One page.
  • The tracking: free, if you do it. A log and a weekly weigh-in average cost nothing.
  • The appointment and the watching: not free, but partly replaceable. Someone has to see your numbers. It doesn't have to be a professional.
  • Technique, live: hard to replace for free. Video of your lifts and a careful read of a form guide get you part of the way.

Option 1: a real plan (free)

Pick one and stop shopping. Every plan on this site is written out in full, with a printable PDF, and none of them asks for an email address:

  • How to start lifting weights: never lifted, two or three days a week, the first month.
  • The dumbbell workout plan: three days at home with a pair of adjustable dumbbells.
  • The bodyweight workout: no equipment at all.
  • The 30-minute workout: short on time, gym or home.
  • The hotel workout and the college workout routine for the weeks that don't look normal.

The plans page has a table for picking one by days and equipment, and the free resources page lists everything free on this site (the plans, their PDFs, the log and the calculators) in one place; the workout plan quiz asks the table's questions and hands you the plan. The form guides for each lift (goblet squat, push-ups, dumbbell row and the rest of the exercise library) are free too, and if you'd like a second pair of eyes without paying for a session, filming a set and comparing it with the guide is a start.

Option 2: a log, and a rule for adding weight (free)

This is the one most people skip, and it's the closest free thing to what a coach does with your numbers. In a meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies with 19,951 people, prompting people to monitor their progress toward a goal helped them reach it, and the effect was larger when the information was physically recorded.2 Those studies were about goals in general, not lifting, but a notebook with last week's numbers in it is exactly that.

The workout log page has the five columns and a blank sheet to print. Before each session, read last week's line and beat it by a rep or a little weight. Once a week, write down your average bodyweight. That's the tracking a coach would do for you, minus the person reading it.

Option 3: a witness (free)

The same meta-analysis found larger effects when progress was reported or made public.2 Having someone see your numbers is a big part of what you'd pay a trainer or a coach for, and it's free if you ask for it: a training partner, a friend you text your log to every Sunday, a family member who asks how the week went. Pick someone who will actually ask. The point isn't guilt; it's that you stop negotiating with yourself on the days you don't feel like it.

If the gym itself is the obstacle, the first day, step by step is written for that, and consistency vs. motivation covers the rest of the system: fixed days, a floor for bad weeks, never miss twice.

What the free options don't cover

Honesty cuts both ways. A plan on paper is the easiest thing in the world to stop following, and the research is blunt about it. In a 2025 randomized trial, 112 trained men and women started the same 10-week strength program three ways: with a coach beside them, app-guided with delayed video feedback on technique, or from a PDF. Of those who started, 88% of the supervised group and 81% of the app group completed at least 26 of the 30 sessions. In the PDF group it was 52%.3 The supervised group also gained more on the squat than the other two; bench-press gains didn't differ.3 It's one short trial in trained people, so it can't tell you what will happen to you. It does say what a free plan is up against: nobody notices when you stop.

That's the pattern more broadly. A meta-analysis of 10 studies and 3,899 people found that 36% of everyone studied intended to be active and didn't follow through, an intention-behaviour gap the authors put at 46%.4 Information was never the missing piece for those people. Structure was.

So the three free options above cover the plan and the tracking. What they can't cover on their own:

  • Changing the plan when your week breaks. A PDF doesn't bend. You can learn to adjust it yourself, and the plans here say how, but it takes a few restarts to get good at it.
  • Someone who notices when you go quiet, unless your witness is a very good one.
  • Technique feedback on the lifts where it matters most. In the trial above the supervised group's edge showed up in the squat, not the bench press.3 If you've never done the main lifts and feel unsafe in a gym, a few sessions with a trainer at your gym to learn them can be a smart move, because a few sessions is enough to learn the lifts and online coaching can take it from there.

Option 4: coaching, when the restarts are what's costing you

Here's the honest version of the sales pitch. If you've run the free options properly, meaning a plan, a log and a witness, and you're still training two months later with the numbers climbing, you don't need me. Keep going. The people coaching is built for are the ones who keep starting over: the plan was never the problem, nobody was reading the numbers, and every restart cost them months.

What a coach adds is the part the free options leave out: a plan written around your real week, a person who reads your numbers every week, and a plan that changes when you do. With me, that's workouts in Trainerize, two weigh-ins a week, progress photos every two weeks, a weekly check-in call and direct access to me for questions. It covers the whole week, not an hour in a gym. That's the difference between coaching and a trainer, and it's spelled out on online coach vs. personal trainer.

What it costs depends on the level of support you need, and I'd rather talk with you first than hand you a price list for a plan that isn't built for you. That conversation happens in the DMs. If a free plan is all you need, I'll say so.

Common questions

What should I do if I can't afford a personal trainer?

Three things, in order: pick one free plan from the plans page and print it; keep a log and read it before every session; and tell one person your numbers every week. Give it eight weeks before you judge it. That covers more of what a trainer does than most people expect.

Can I get fit without a personal trainer?

Yes. The guidelines are public,1 the plans are free and the progression rule is simple. What's hard isn't knowing what to do. It's doing it in week six, and that's a structure problem, not a knowledge problem. The free fixes for structure are a log and a witness; the paid one is a coach.

Are free workout plans any good?

A good free plan is as good as a good paid one on paper. The difference shows up in whether you follow it. In the trial above, the group training from a PDF completed the sessions at the lowest rate of the three.3 Pick a plan with a floor for bad weeks, and pair it with the log and the witness.

Is a personal trainer worth it if money is tight?

I can't answer that for your budget, and I won't pretend to. What I can give you is the test: if you've never done the main lifts and feel unsafe in a gym, a few sessions with a trainer at your gym to learn them is the version of help that's most worth it, because the squat is where being watched paid off in the trial above. The longer answer is here.

Is online coaching an option when a personal trainer is too expensive?

I won't tell you how it compares in money, because I don't publish prices and I'm not going to compare them here. What I can tell you is what it covers, which is the week rather than the hour (what online fitness coaching is), and that whether you need it at all is a question worth settling first: do I need a fitness coach?
