Do I need a fitness coach (or a personal trainer)?

Most people don't need one. Some people keep paying for not having one, in restarts. Here's how to tell which you are, from a coach who would rather you got the answer right than hired him.

Short answer: No, most people don't need a fitness coach to get in shape: the guidelines are public and a good plan is free. You may need one if you know what to do and keep not doing it, if your week never fits the plan, or if nobody notices when you stop. If you've never lifted and feel unsafe in a gym, a few sessions with a trainer at your gym may be the better first step.

Most people don't need a fitness coach. The guidelines are public, a good plan is free, and plenty of people get strong with a notebook and a gym membership. I'm a coach, so take that into account as you read. The honest answer is that most people don't need one, some people would save themselves years with one, and the difference has almost nothing to do with how much you know.

This page is about whether. Whether it's worth the money once you've decided you want help is a separate question, and the three-way comparison of an app, an AI chatbot and a coach is here.

What you need, and what's already free

You need three things to get in shape: a plan, a way to make it harder over time, and a reason to show up next week. The first two are free. The World Health Organization's guidelines for adults are 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week (or half that much vigorous activity), plus muscle-strengthening work for all the major muscle groups on two or more days.1 The plans on this site cover the lifting half with six free programs and printable PDFs, there is a log you can copy, and the rule for adding weight is one page long. None of that needs a coach.

The third thing is where it goes wrong. In 2020, 24.2% of U.S. adults met the guidelines for both aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity, according to the National Health Interview Survey.2 It isn't that three in four people don't know the number. A meta-analysis of 10 studies and 3,899 people found that 36% of everyone studied intended to be active and didn't follow through, an intention-behaviour gap the authors put at 46%.3 Wanting to train and training are different things, and the second one is what a coach is actually for.

So the question isn't "do I need someone to tell me what to do?" You don't. It's "do I need someone in the loop?"

Signs you don't need a coach

  • You've trained consistently for the last couple of months and your numbers are moving. You don't have the problem a coach solves. Keep going.
  • You already log your training and read the log. In a meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies with 19,951 people, prompting people to monitor their progress toward a goal helped them reach it, and the effect was larger when the information was physically recorded.4 If you do that on your own, you've built half of what coaching provides.
  • Someone already sees your training. A training partner, a trainer at your gym, a friend you send the log to. The same meta-analysis found larger effects when progress was reported or made public.4 A coach is one way to get that. It isn't the only way.
  • Your question is medical. An injury, a condition or a medical question goes to your doctor or a physical therapist first. The WHO guidelines say adults with chronic conditions may wish to consult a physical activity specialist or healthcare professional about what's appropriate.1 I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor.

Signs you might

  • You know roughly what to do and keep not doing it. Every restart began with a new plan, and the plan was never the problem.
  • Your weeks are unpredictable, and every program you've tried assumed a perfect one.
  • Nobody notices when you go quiet. If you miss two weeks, the only person who knows is you.
  • You want to understand the why, so you can run it yourself later. Good coaching is meant to make you need it less over time; I answer that on the FAQ.

If three of those four are you, a better plan on its own probably isn't the missing piece. A system around the plan is. You can build a basic one yourself (here's how), and a coach is the version where someone else holds the other end.

Do I need a personal trainer instead?

When people type "personal trainer" they usually mean someone in the room with them. That is a different kind of help from a coach who runs your week from a distance (what online fitness coaching is), and for some people it's the right first step. If you've never done the main lifts and feel unsafe in a gym, a few sessions with a trainer at your gym to learn them can be a smart move, and online coaching can pick up from there.

The research backs the technique part. In a 2025 randomized trial, 112 trained men and women started the same 10-week strength program three ways: with a coach beside them, app-guided with delayed video feedback, or from a PDF. Of those who started, 88% of the supervised group, 81% of the app group and 52% of the PDF group completed at least 26 of the 30 sessions, and the supervised group gained more on the squat than the other two; bench-press gains didn't differ.5 The squat is the lift where technique matters most, and that's where being watched paid off. A coach's weekly call is a different kind of watching: in a randomized trial of novice adults, people who got weekly coaching sessions over video did more days of resistance training at four weeks than a group that got no further contact, though the difference was no longer statistically significant at eight weeks.6

Both trials are short and about strength training, and neither tests a coach's full service. The full comparison, including when a trainer is the better call, is on online coach vs. personal trainer.

What a coach can't do

A coach can't lift for you, can't eat for you and can't promise results. No coach honestly can, and I won't. What a coach does is write the plan around your real week, read your numbers and change the plan with you. With me, that's workouts in Trainerize, two weigh-ins a week, progress photos every two weeks, a weekly check-in call and direct access to me for questions. Here's how it works, step by step.

Common questions

Do I need a personal trainer to build muscle?

No. Muscle comes from lifting for all the major muscle groups on two or more days a week,1 making the lifts harder over time and eating enough protein. A trainer or a coach helps with the plan, the effort and the technique; the trial above found the supervised group's edge on the squat, not on every lift.5 If you can already do the main lifts, start with progressive overload and a log. If you want the plan built and watched, that's coaching to build muscle.

Do I need a personal trainer to lose weight?

No. Fat loss comes down to calories, protein, steps and lifting, and all four are explained for free on why you can't lose fat and calories and protein. What a coach adds is the part that keeps failing on your own: a weekly look at the numbers and a plan that changes when the week does. Nobody can promise you a number of pounds, and anyone who does is a red flag.

Do I need a personal trainer as a beginner?

It depends on what kind of beginner. If you've never touched a barbell and feel unsafe in a gym, start with a few sessions with a trainer at your gym. If you can do the basics but have never kept a routine going, structure and check-ins matter more than someone in the room, and that's what coaching for beginners is built for. Do I need a coach, or can I just follow a program?

Follow the program first. Pick one of the free plans, keep a log and give it eight weeks. If you're still at it and the numbers are climbing, you've answered the question. If the plan died in the first bad week, that's information, not a character flaw: the structure or the accountability wasn't there, and that's what coaching is for.

Do I really need a fitness coach?

If you're asking because you've tried on your own a few times and keep ending up at day one, probably. If you're asking because you're not sure you'd do the work between check-ins, not yet: a coach can't do that part, and paying for one won't change it.
