Lifting belt: what it changes, what was measured, and how to wear one

A stiff belt around the waist, worn on heavy sets, that your brace pushes against. Here's what the squat studies measured with and without one, when it's worth putting on, and how to wear it.

Short answer: A lifting belt is a stiff belt worn tight around the waist on heavy sets, for your brace to push against. In squat studies, people did more reps with the same weight, moved the bar faster and reached a slightly heavier one-rep max with a belt, and one small study measured 25 to 40% more pressure in the abdomen.

A lifting belt is a wide, stiff belt buckled tight around your waist, over your stomach, for heavy sets. It doesn't hold you up by itself. You take a breath, brace, and push your midsection out into the belt, and the belt pushes back.

Every study on this page tested the squat. Here's what they measured, when a belt is worth putting on, and how to wear one. A belt squat is a different thing, an exercise where the weight hangs from a belt at your hips: that's the belt squat.

What the studies measured

  • More reps with the same weight. In a 2026 study, 29 men and 20 women, some used to belts and some not, did two sets of squats to exhaustion with the weight each could lift six times without a belt, once with a belt and once without. With the belt they averaged 8.1 reps against 6.5 without, and the lifting part of each rep was quicker, 1.99 seconds against 2.09. The rise held for men and women and for people with and without belt experience.1
  • A slightly heavier one-rep max. In a 2026 study of 25 resistance-trained athletes, 14 men and 11 women, the back squat one-rep max averaged 119.2 kg with a belt and 114.0 kg without. At the same lighter weights the bar moved faster with the belt, though at 85 to 100% of each condition's own max it moved slightly slower.2
  • Faster, with the same muscle activity and joint angles. In a 2001 study, 14 healthy men squatted to parallel with 90% of their one-rep max, with and without a belt. The belted squats were faster, down and up. Activity in the five thigh, hip and lower-back muscles measured didn't differ, and neither did the joint angles. The bar travelled further forward and further up and down with the belt.3
  • More pressure in the abdomen. In a 1992 study, five people squatted eight reps at their eight-rep-max weight with and without a belt. The pressure in the abdomen was 25 to 40% higher with the belt, and the belted reps were generally quicker, most of all in the later reps. Activity in the lower-back and side-abdominal muscles measured didn't differ; some thigh muscle readings were higher with the belt.4

What it didn't change

Two things the belt didn't change in these studies:

  • Balance. In the 49-person study, people felt more stable with the belt, but the force plates under their feet didn't show steadier balance. The authors say the extra reps can't be put down to more stability.1
  • Muscle activity in the trunk. Neither the 2001 nor the 1992 study found a difference in lower-back muscle activity with a belt, and the 1992 study found none in the side-abdominal muscle it measured either.3,4

When to wear one

These are practical guidelines, not findings.

  • Heavy sets of the big lifts. Squats, the deadlift and heavy overhead presses, when the weight is near the top of what you can do for the reps. The studies above tested only the squat.
  • Not for everything. Warm-up sets, light sets, machines like the leg press, and isolation work like curls don't need one.
  • After you can brace without it. A belt works with a brace, so learn the brace first: how to brace, step by step.

How to wear one

Practical cues:

  1. Over the stomach, around the level of your belly button, not up on the ribs or down on the hips.
  2. Tight, but with room for a breath. You should still be able to breathe in low and push your stomach out into it.
  3. Brace into it. Breathe in, tighten your midsection and press it out against the belt all the way around, then lift.
  4. Loosen it between sets. Undo it, or loosen it a notch, while you rest.

Common mistakes

  • So tight you can't breathe in. No breath, no brace to push into the belt (practical).
  • So loose there's nothing to push against (practical).
  • Wearing it for the whole session, warm-ups and curls included (practical).
  • Using the belt instead of a brace. The belt adds to the brace; it doesn't do the bracing (practical).

Common questions

Does a lifting belt help you lift more?

In the squat studies here, yes: 8.1 reps against 6.5 with the same weight in 49 men and women, and a one-rep max of 119.2 kg against 114.0 kg in 25 trained athletes.1,2

Is a lifting belt for women too?

Women were in both 2026 studies: 20 of the 49 people in one and 11 of the 25 in the other. In the first, the extra reps showed up in women as well as men.1,2

Should beginners wear a lifting belt?

No study here tested beginners. In the 49-person study, people who had never used a belt also got more reps with one.1 Learn the squat and the brace without a belt first, then add one for heavy sets if you want it (practical).

Do you wear a belt for deadlifts?

You can, on heavy sets (practical). None of the studies on this page tested the deadlift, so the numbers above are for the squat only.
