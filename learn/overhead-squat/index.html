Overhead squat: the setup, what holding the bar overhead changes, and where it fits

A squat with the bar locked out overhead, arms straight, all the way down and back up. Here's the setup, what the muscle-activity studies found against the back and front squat, why the weights are lighter, the dumbbell and dowel versions, the faults, and the sets.

Short answer: Hold a bar overhead, wide grip, arms straight, over the middle of your feet, and squat as deep as you can keep it there. An overhead squat is a squat with a barbell held straight above your head, arms locked, for the whole rep. It's one of the hardest squats to set up and one of the lightest to load, because your shoulders and upper back have to hold the bar in place while your legs and hips do the squat. It's the same position as the bottom of a snatch in weightlifting.

This page covers the setup, what the studies found it works compared with the back squat and the front squat, why the weights are lighter, the dumbbell and dowel versions, the faults, and the sets. The overhead squat is also used as a movement screen; that use isn't covered here.

How to do an overhead squat

  1. Start with a dowel or an empty bar. Take a wide grip, wide enough that the bar sits a few inches above your head with your arms straight.
  2. Get the bar overhead, by pressing it from behind your neck or by snatching it, and lock your elbows.
  3. Set the bar over the middle of your feet, slightly behind your head, and press up into it, shoulder blades pulled together and up.
  4. Take your squat stance, feet about shoulder width, toes turned out a little. Brace.
  5. Squat down as deep as you can while the bar stays over the middle of your feet and your arms stay straight.
  6. Stand up, keeping the bar in the same place over your feet. That's one rep.

If the bar drifts forward, stop the rep there. That depth is your working depth for now; the squat itself is on squat form.

What it works, compared with the back and front squat

The overhead squat is often described as a core exercise. The studies give it a smaller edge than that:

  • Against the back squat, at real weights. In 14 adults squatting at 60%, 75% and 90% of a three-rep max, the overhead squat had more activity in the abs and obliques on the way down, but by only about 2 to 7%. The back squat had more activity in the muscles along the spine and in every leg muscle measured on the way up, and produced more peak force. The authors' conclusion: the results don't support the idea that the overhead squat is a substantially greater stimulus for the trunk.1
  • Against the front squat. In seven recreationally trained men, the front shoulder worked harder in the front squat, at 65% and 95% of a three-rep max, and the middle trapezius, between the shoulder blades, harder in the overhead squat, at 80% and 95%. A plank worked the abs and obliques harder than either squat.2
  • At light weights. In 12 healthy adults, six women and six men, doing back, front and overhead squats with low weights, the overhead squat had the highest trunk muscle activity of the three.3

Read together: the overhead squat asks the upper back and shoulders to hold the bar, and at light weights it works the trunk harder than the other squats. At heavier weights, the back squat works the legs harder and the difference in the front of the trunk is small. So it's a skill and upper-back lift with a squat in it, not a replacement for your main squat. Planks and other trunk exercises are on the core workout.

Why the weights are lighter

Holding the bar overhead limits the load long before your legs do. The seven men in the front squat study had a three-rep max of about 125 kilograms on the front squat and about 91 on the overhead squat.2 And adding weight changes where the work goes: in 14 male college students who overhead-squatted with a plastic pipe and with 20% and 40% of their body weight, the extra load raised peak turning force at the hip but not at the knee.4 So expect your overhead squat to be well below your back squat, and add weight in small steps.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • The bar drifting forward. Stop the rep where it starts to drift, and press up harder into the bar with your shoulder blades.
  • Bent elbows. Lock your arms before you squat and keep them locked; a wider grip makes it easier.
  • Chest dropping at the bottom. Squat only as deep as you can keep the bar over your feet.
  • Heels lifting. Keep your weight over the middle of your feet. Ankle range is one reason heels lift; ankle mobility covers it.
  • Adding weight before the position is there. The empty bar at full depth, steady, comes before plates.
  • Pushing through something that hurts. Sore muscles the next day are normal; a sharp or lasting hurt in your shoulders, back, hips or knees isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • As a warm-up: one or two sets of 8 to 10 with a dowel or an empty bar before your squats.
  • As a lift: three to five sets of 3 to 6 early in a session, while you're fresh, stopping well short of failure. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, which covers healthy adults, advises at least two sets per exercise.5
  • Keep your main squat. Count the overhead squat as extra leg work, not a swap for your back or front squat.
  • Add weight in small steps. When every rep stays over your feet at the same depth, go up by the smallest jump you have. (Progressive overload covers the options.)
