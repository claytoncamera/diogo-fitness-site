Strength training for women: the week, the weights and what the research found

The studies that put women and men on the same program found similar muscle growth relative to starting size, so build the same kind of program around your days and your equipment. Here's how to set the week up, how heavy to go, how to add to it, and what the research in women actually measured.

Short answer: Train each major muscle at least twice a week, in sets of about 6 to 15 taken close to failure, and add a little over time. In women, a 24-study meta-analysis found large strength gains and medium muscle growth.2

Strength training for women is strength training: a few compound lifts, two to four times a week, with weights that get a little heavier over months. What differs is the evidence. Women are underrepresented in resistance exercise research,1 so it matters which findings come from studies that recruited women. This page is built from those, and from the studies that put men and women on the same program.

It covers the week, the weights, progression and rest. How men and women compare on the same program is read in full on the coaching page for women; here it gets a line, not a repeat. How many women report muscle-strengthening on two or more days a week, by the CDC's survey, is on strength training by age.

What the research in women found

Four analyses matter most here, and they point the same way.

  • Women get strong on normal programs. A 2020 meta-analysis pooled 24 studies of women only, lasting from four weeks to a year. It found large effects on upper- and lower-body strength and a medium effect on muscle growth; after correcting for publication bias, the lower-body effect was medium and the upper-body effect stayed large.2
  • The dose matters, as it does for anyone. A 2024 meta-analysis of 40 randomized trials in healthy women aged 18 to 35, 1,312 participants, found a strength effect of 1.2 standard deviations and a muscle-growth effect of 0.4. More sessions went with more muscle growth, and more weekly volume tended to go with more strength.3
  • On the same program as men, the growth is similar. A 2025 analysis of 29 studies in adults aged 18 to 45 found muscle growth, as a percentage of starting size, similar between the sexes, and slightly larger in men in absolute units, in the upper body but not the lower body.4 A 2020 meta-analysis of studies that used one protocol for both sexes also found no significant difference in muscle growth5 (the coaching page reads it in full).
  • What this page can't tell you. The findings from women-only samples come from three meta-analyses. Everything else here comes from studies of adults of both sexes, or from a single study.

How to structure the week

Start from the days you can repeat, then make sure every major muscle gets trained at least twice in them.

  • Each muscle twice a week. The ACSM's 2026 position stand's primary recommendation is lifting with high effort at least twice weekly, with all major muscle groups being engaged,6 and for muscle size, frequency made little difference once weekly sets were equal: a 2019 meta-analysis of 25 studies found no significant difference between higher and lower frequency on a volume-equated basis.7 For strength, a meta-analysis of 22 studies found gains rose with weekly frequency (not when total volume was equated), and its sex subgroups reached significance for women (p = 0.03) but not men (p = 0.19).8 The women-only meta-analysis also found frequency and volume shaped strength gains.2
  • Two or three days: full body. Every session has a squat, a hinge, a push, a pull and something for the trunk, so each muscle gets two or three sessions a week. (The first four weeks lays this out exercise by exercise; the dumbbell plan is the home version.)
  • Four days: upper/lower. Two upper days and two lower days: more exercises per half, still trained twice. Which workout split compares the options day by day.
  • Give the upper body its share. The 2023 review of 31 studies in healthy young women recommended training the lower body twice a week and the upper body two to three times.1 In the women-only meta-analysis, the number of sets per exercise moderated upper-body strength gains.2 Presses, rows and pull-downs belong in every week.

The weekly set counts page explains the range. If a week is short, cut exercises before you cut effort: the thirty-minute session is the fallback.

How heavy, and how many reps

The load rules come from studies of adults of both sexes, set out on how many reps and how much weight to lift: heavier sets build more one-rep-max strength, and a wide range of loads builds muscle when the sets are hard.9 The one analysis added here, a 2021 network meta-analysis of 28 studies in 747 adults, found strength gains were larger with moderate and heavy loads than with light ones.10

  • One women-only finding. The 2023 review in healthy young women reported its largest lower-body strength gains with sets of 1 to 6 reps and its largest upper-body gains with sets of 13 to 20.1 It's one analysis, and the pooled evidence still favours heavier sets for strength, so read it as a reason to include some lighter upper-body sets, not a rule.

How to progress

The ACSM's 2026 stand is looser than its 2009 one:11 progression isn't needed to benefit from lifting, but continued long-term progress likely needs the stimulus to rise in some way, and for people new to lifting, whose loads start low, progression is a requirement to achieve a meaningful benefit.6 How to add to it and what to write down: progressive overload and the workout log.

Rest between sets: one study of men and women

The usual advice is at least a minute, and two to five after heavy compound lifts (rest between sets). One 2026 study is worth knowing. Twenty-one men and twenty-one women with a year or more of bench press experience did sets of five at 75% of their one-rep max, 90 seconds apart, until a set failed. The women completed about twice as many reps; soreness and strength recovery over three days did not differ; and the authors attributed the gap, in an exploratory analysis, to the women recovering more quickly during the rest intervals.12 A squat study the paper cites found the reverse: males recovered faster after five sets of five at 80% of a one-rep max, followed by one set to failure.12 One trial of 42 people, one lift, and a squat result pointing the other way: it changes nothing about the heavy sets. For moderate ones, if 90 seconds feels like enough, it may be, though that rests on one trial.

Two things you'll hear, and what the studies say

  • "Women should use light weights and high reps." There's no separate rep range for women. In the pooled studies, heavy loads built more one-rep strength, and light loads built similar muscle in studies where every set was taken to failure.9 If the sets are easy, they're easy for everyone.
  • "Women need more rest and recovery." In the bench press study, soreness and strength over three days did not differ significantly between the sexes.12 It's one lift in one study, so it's no case for adding rest days by sex alone: plan them by how much you did. (How many rest days.)

Common questions

Will lifting weights make me bulky?

These trials recorded muscle size and strength, not appearance, so they don't answer how you'll look. What they recorded: in the 2020 meta-analysis of 24 women-only studies lasting four weeks to a year, the effect on muscle growth (measured in 15 of them) was medium, while the effect on upper-body strength was large.2 In the 2025 meta-analysis of 29 studies that put healthy adults aged 18 to 45 on the same program, women's growth as a percentage of starting size was similar to men's, while men's absolute growth was slightly larger.4 How much you train sets the dose: the example weeks are where to start.

Do women need a different program from men?

Not according to the studies that put both on the same program: muscle growth, as a percentage of starting size, came out similar.4 Build it on your days, equipment and starting strength; the coaching page reads the other main analysis.

Should women lift heavy or light?

Both, for different jobs, and sources define "heavy" differently. In the 2017 meta-analysis it meant loads above 60% of a one-rep max, and those built more one-rep-max strength than lighter ones.9 The ACSM's 2009 stand recommends 8 to 12 reps for new lifters and, for experienced ones, an eventual emphasis on heavy loading of 1 to 6 reps.11 For muscle, moderate and light sets build it just as well when taken to failure.10 The example weeks above sit between 6 and 15 reps; how much weight to lift covers when to go heavier.

How much rest between sets do women need?

The same as anyone: longer after heavy compound lifts, shorter after smaller exercises (rest between sets has the ranges). One 2026 study of the bench press suggested, in an exploratory analysis, that women recovered more quickly between moderate sets,12 a reason not to stretch the short rests, not to cut the long ones.

If you'd rather not plan it alone

In the women-only meta-analysis, supervised training had a larger effect on lower-body strength than unsupervised training, at p = 0.05, right at the usual threshold.2 It compared supervised with unsupervised training in the studies, not online coaching, so it's a reason to value a second pair of eyes, not a result for any one service. Coaching is the version where your workouts are built around the days you actually have, and a weekly call looks at the week you just had and rebuilds the next one. If you want a person beside you, start with a few sessions with a trainer at a gym. Coaching for women explains how the coaching runs, and here's how to apply. Otherwise: pick the three-day week above, choose weights that leave a rep or two at the top of the range, and write the first session down.
