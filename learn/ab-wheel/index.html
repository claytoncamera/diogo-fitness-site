Ab wheel rollout: how to do it, how far to roll, and what the roll-out studies measured

Kneel, hold the wheel under your shoulders, and roll it out in front of you without letting your back sag, then pull it back. Here's the setup, what the roll-out studies measured, how far to roll, how to build up to it, the alternatives, the faults, and the sets.

Short answer: Kneel holding the wheel under your shoulders, brace, and roll it forward as far as you can without your lower back sagging, then pull it back with your midsection. An ab wheel is a small wheel with a handle on each side. The exercise is the rollout: you kneel, hold the wheel on the floor under your shoulders, and roll it forward until your body is stretched out low over the floor, then pull it back. Your abs work to stop your lower back from sagging as the wheel goes out, the same job as the plank's, done while you move. It's the "stop the arch" job in the core workout, made harder.

This page covers the setup, what the roll-out studies measured, how far to roll, how to build up to a full rollout, the alternatives, the faults, and the sets. The hold it grows out of is on how to do a plank.

How to do an ab wheel rollout

  1. Kneel on a mat with the wheel on the floor in front of your knees, hands on the handles, arms straight, shoulders over the wheel.
  2. Brace: tighten your midsection, squeeze your glutes, and tuck your hips slightly, so your back is flat or a little rounded, not arched.
  3. Roll the wheel forward, letting your hips travel with it and your arms reach out in front, keeping that brace.
  4. Stop where your back is about to sag, or when your body is nearly straight from knees to hands, whichever comes first.
  5. Pull the wheel back toward your knees by pulling with your arms and curling your midsection, hips following, until you're back at the start. That's one rep.

The rule of the rollout is the same as the plank's: your range ends where your position slips. Keep the rollout short with a flat back rather than long with a sagging one.

What the roll-out studies measured

The roll-out studies found for this page used wheels, wheeled devices and training balls, and every one measured muscle activity during a few reps:

  • Among the highest for the abs. In 21 healthy men and women aged 23 to 43, a wheel roll-out was among the exercises with the most activity in the upper and lower rectus abdominis, the obliques and the latissimus dorsi, the big back muscle.1
  • On a ball too. In 18 people, a roll-out on a training ball produced 63% of a maximal contraction in the upper rectus abdominis and 53% in the lower part, more than most of the ten exercises tested, and little activity in the rectus femoris, a hip flexor.2
  • Against a crunch, with less hip. In 22 healthy young adults, 10 men and 12 women, a wheeled roll-out device produced the most ab activity of the four exercises tested, the others being the crunch, a side bridge and a double-leg thrust, and the least rectus femoris activity.3 In 14 people testing two common exercises and seven commercial ab machines, a wheeled roll-out device was one of the two most effective at working the abs and the upper-body muscles measured, with little hip-flexor activity.4
  • The edge comes on the way out. In 45 people who regularly did ab exercises, the roll-out device produced more activity than the crunch in all three ab muscles measured during the lowering phase, rolling out, and the crunch more in two of the three during the lifting phase. The authors called the roll-out a credible variation and the crunch the standard.5

Read together: the rollout is among the most demanding exercises measured for the front of your trunk, it brings your lats and upper body in, and it keeps the hip flexors fairly quiet. In the one study that split the rep, its edge over the crunch came while rolling out. None of these studies trained anyone with rollouts for weeks.

How far to roll, and the standing rollout

Roll only as far as you can keep your back from sagging. No study found for this page compared rollout distances or kneeling against standing, so the ladder below is practice, not a finding. Each step makes the lever longer, the same principle the long-lever plank uses.

  • Roll to a wall. Kneel facing a wall a short distance away and roll out until the wheel touches it. Move back a little each week.
  • Partial kneeling rollouts, stopping well short of full stretch.
  • Full kneeling rollouts, body nearly straight from knees to hands.
  • Standing rollouts, from your feet with straight legs, the hardest version. It's optional.

For the abs' other job, curling the trunk forward, the gym option is the cable crunch.

What it changes over weeks

No trial that trained people with ab wheel rollouts and measured strength or muscle turned up in a PubMed search for this page; every roll-out study found measured activity in a single session. For core work in general, a 2016 meta-analysis of trained people aged 16 to 44 found that core strength training made the trunk muscles much stronger, with only small-to-medium gains in other measures of fitness and athletic performance, from studies of low quality.6

So the rollout is a hard way to train your trunk at resisting an arch. This page makes no claim about how your midsection will look.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Lower back sagging. Shorten the roll and tuck your hips; roll to a wall until you can hold the position.
  • Hips staying back. If your hips stay over your knees while your arms reach, it's a shoulder stretch, not a rollout. Let your hips travel forward with the wheel.
  • Pulling back with your hips first. Sticking your hips up to get back turns the return into a hinge. Keep the brace and pull the wheel back toward your knees.
  • Rolling out too fast. The way out is where the work is; take two or three seconds.
  • Knees on a hard floor. Kneel on a folded mat or a pad.
  • Training through something that hurts. Sore muscles the next day are normal; a sharp or lasting hurt in your back or shoulders isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • Two or three sets of 5 to 10 rollouts, stopping when your back starts to sag, at the end of a session after your main lifts. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, which covers healthy adults, advises at least two sets per exercise.7
  • Use it in the plank's slot, the "stop the arch" job, once you can hold a plank in good position for 45 seconds. Where core work goes in your week covers the rest.
  • Progress the distance before the reps. Move from the wall to partial to full kneeling rollouts, then add reps. (Progressive overload covers the options.)
