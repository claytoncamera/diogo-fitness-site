T-bar row: the setup, which handle, and free-standing or chest-supported

One end of a barbell anchored to the floor, a handle under the plates, and a row from a hinge. Here's the setup, what the studies found about grip and the elbows, the free-standing and chest-supported versions, what to use when there's no T-bar, the faults, and the sets.

Short answer: Straddle a barbell anchored at one end, hook a handle under the plates, hinge until your chest faces the floor, and row the handle to your stomach. Grip studies disagree by row: on a seated row a narrower grip worked the lats more, and on a bent-over barbell row a wider grip did.1

A T-bar row is a row with one end of a barbell fixed to the floor, in a landmine attachment or a corner, and the plates on the other end. You stand over the bar, hook a handle under the plates, hinge forward and pull. Because the bar pivots from the floor, the weight travels in an arc toward you, and the handle lets you choose a close or a wide grip. Gyms also have T-bar machines, free-standing or with a chest pad.

It trains the same muscles as other rows: the lats, the big muscles down the sides of your back, the muscles between your shoulder blades, the rear of the shoulders and the biceps, with the muscles along your spine holding the hinge. This page covers the setup, which handle to use, free-standing or chest-supported, the alternatives, the faults and the sets. The straight-bar version is on barbell row form, and the back day is on the back workout.

How to do a T-bar row

  1. Anchor one end of a barbell in a landmine or a corner, load the other end, and stand straddling the bar, facing the plates.
  2. Hook a close-grip handle (a V-handle or a double-D handle) under the bar, right behind the plates.
  3. Hinge by pushing your hips back with a slight bend in your knees, until your chest faces the floor or close to it. Brace and keep your back flat.
  4. Row the handle toward your stomach, leading with your elbows and keeping them close to your sides.
  5. Pause for a moment when the plates are near your chest or the handle touches your stomach.
  6. Lower under control to straight arms without letting your back round or the plates touch down. That's one rep.

On a T-bar machine the steps are the same; the machine sets the arc, and on the chest-supported kind you lie face down on the pad instead of hinging.

Which handle: close or wide

The research doesn't settle it, and no study found for this page measured grip on the T-bar row itself. A 2026 review of 23 muscle-activity studies of pulling exercises reports three rows that point different ways:1

  • Seated row. In 14 resistance-trained men, a narrow grip produced more lat activity than a wide one, and the wide grip more in the trapezius and the side of the shoulder. In 21 recreationally trained adults, 11 men and 10 women, elbows kept lower and closer to the sides favoured the lats.
  • Bent-over barbell row. In 20 active male university students rowing at 60% of their max, a wide grip, one and a half times the distance across the shoulders, produced more lat activity than a shoulder-width grip.
  • Chest-supported barbell row. A conference abstract reported that a wide overhand grip worked the middle trapezius, between the shoulder blades, more than narrower or underhand grips, with no significant difference in the lats.

The review describes each as a single study and calls the findings a starting point, not a prescription. The practical reading: the lats work with either handle, a wide grip brings in more of the upper back, and the close V-handle in the steps above is the classic T-bar setup. Use the one you have, or alternate them from block to block.

Free-standing or chest-supported

A free-standing T-bar row makes your back and hips hold the hinge; the chest pad takes that job away. In 15 resistance-trained men, a two-arm free-weight bent-over row worked the muscles along the spine about a third more than a two-arm machine row did.2 That's a barbell row against a machine row, not the T-bar versions, but it's the same difference: one you hold up yourself, one that holds you.

Neither is required for results. In an eight-week trial of 38 resistance-trained men, a free-weight program and a machine program, one of its four exercises a face-down bench pull, raised strength and grew the measured muscles similarly.3 The back wasn't one of the muscles it measured, so read it as free weights and machines in general. Choose the free-standing row when you also want the hinge trained, and the chest-supported one on a day that already has a heavy hinge.

How far to row

Row from straight arms until the handle reaches your stomach. In 16 resistance-trained men on a face-down barbell row, rowing only the top half or only the bottom half of the range changed muscle activity by medium amounts that weren't consistent across the muscles measured, compared with the full range.4 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found strength was enhanced by training through a complete range of motion.5 Use the full row on every rep.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Standing up as you row. If your chest rises with every rep, the weight is too heavy. Lighten it and hold the hinge.
  • Rounding your back at the bottom. Brace before each rep and lower only as far as your back stays flat.
  • Heaving with your hips. A small hip drive on the last rep is fine; using it on every rep moves the work away from your back.
  • Cutting the range short. Straight arms at the bottom, handle to your stomach at the top.
  • Loading big plates. Large-diameter plates hit your chest before the handle reaches you. Use smaller plates to keep the full range.
  • Pulling through something that hurts. Sore muscles the next day are normal; a sharp or lasting hurt isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • Three sets of 8 to 12 early on a back or pull day, stopping one or two reps short. (How to judge reps left.) The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, which covers healthy adults, advises at least two sets per exercise.5
  • Count it with your other rows and pulldowns. The same stand found muscle growth was enhanced by ten or more sets per muscle group a week.
  • Put it before your curls. The row works your biceps too, so tired biceps cut the row short.
  • Add weight in small steps. When 12 clean reps come easy, add the smallest plates you have. (Progressive overload covers the options.)
