Summer heat by metro: 90-degree days and July highs

How many 90-degree days a year your city gets, and how hot July runs, at a named NOAA weather station in each of 48 large metros. Then what that changes in a training week, and what it doesn't.

Short answer: NOAA's 1991–2020 normals put the days a year at or above 90 F between 2.1 (Seattle) and 173.4 (Phoenix) at principal-city weather stations in 46 large U.S. metros. Where the count is high, move hard outdoor work to the early morning or evening in those months and keep lifting indoors exactly as planned.

Heat changes when you can train outside, not whether you can train. The useful question is how many weeks a year it matters where you live, and NOAA answers it: its 1991–2020 climate normals give, for each weather station, the average number of days a year that reach 90 F and the average July high.1,2

The table takes one station for each of 48 large U.S. metro areas: a station within 25 km of the Census Bureau's internal point for the metro's first-named city, preferring one with a complete record. That makes each row a reading at one spot near that city, not a metro average, so the station's name and distance are printed with it. San Francisco and San Jose have no figures, for the reasons given in their rows.

What stands out

  • Phoenix is in a class of its own. Its Sky Harbor station averages 173.4 days a year at or above 90 F, the most of the 46 stations and 50.6 more than the next (Austin-Camp Mabry, 122.8), and a 106.5 F July high, also the highest.
  • Nine stations average more than 90 days a year at or above 90 F, about three months: Phoenix, Austin, San Antonio, Riverside–San Bernardino, Orlando, Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, Tampa and Miami. San Antonio's and Riverside's are partial records, and Sacramento (89.9) just misses.
  • The same July high can mean a different summer. Atlanta (90.1 F), Denver (90.2 F), Charlotte (90.3 F) and Miami (90.6 F) have July highs within half a degree of each other, but Miami's station averages 91.4 days at or above 90 F against 42.2 to 49.2 at the other three. The July high tells you how hot the peak is; the day count tells you how many weeks you'll be planning around it.
  • A station is not a metro. The Los Angeles row is the UCLA station, 6.3 km from the city's Census point: 10.4 days a year at or above 90 F. The Riverside station, in the neighboring metro to the east, averages 110.0. If you live far from your row's station, your summer may look more like another row's.
  • Where 90 F is rare. Seattle's Sand Point station averages 2.1 days a year at or above 90 F, the fewest of the 46, and a 76.5 F July high, also the lowest. Milwaukee (9.1 days) and Grand Rapids (9.9) are next. Ties are common: Atlanta and St. Louis at 47.1 days, Kansas City and Raleigh at 47.3, and eight pairs on the July high, from Milwaukee and Portland at 81.9 F to San Antonio and Salt Lake City at 94.0 F.

How to read your own station's row

Read the day count as a calendar, not a forecast. A normal is a 30-year average: any given summer can run hotter or cooler, and the reading is taken at the station, not at your door. A few dozen 90-degree days means a few weeks of adjusting. A hundred or more means the summer months need their own version of your week.

What heat changes in a training week

  • Move hard outdoor work to the cooler hours. Runs, hill walks, sprints and outdoor conditioning go to the early morning or the evening on the hot days, not the middle of the afternoon. Not sure which slot you'd keep? Pick the time you can repeat.
  • Lifting doesn't change. If you lift indoors, the plan is the same in July as in January: same days, same sessions, same progression. Heat is a reason to move the outdoor part of your week, not to skip the gym part.
  • Keep the walk; move the walk. A daily walk in a hot month goes to the morning, after sunset or indoors. Don't drop it because the afternoon is too hot.
  • Plan it once. On the first hot week, decide which sessions move and where they go, and keep that schedule through the hot months. Rebuilding the week every hot day is how a summer of training turns into a summer of skipped sessions.
  • Short is fine. On the worst days, a 30-minute session indoors beats an outdoor session you cut short or skip.

Where the numbers come from

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information publish the U.S. Climate Normals for 1991 to 2020. The days at or above 90 F are the annual normal from the Annual/Seasonal Climate Normals; the July high is the July value of the normal daily maximum from the Monthly Climate Normals.1,2 Each value was read from the station's own normals file, with NOAA's completeness flag and years of record, on October 22, 2026, and checked the same day by a second, independent re-fetch: every value, flag and year count matched.

How the station was chosen: the metros are the 25 largest metropolitan statistical areas by the Census Bureau's 2024 population estimates and the 23 other metros among the 50 largest that have a page on this site. For each, up to six stations within 25 km of the Census Bureau's internal point for the first-named principal city were considered, preferring one with standard flags on every value; the distance in the table is from that point. A station describes its own spot, and normals apply less well the farther you are from the station. San Francisco has no station within 25 km of its point, and San Jose's only qualifying station is on a mountain, so neither has figures here.
