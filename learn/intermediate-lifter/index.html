Intermediate lifter: what the term means, and what changes in the training

There's no test you pass to stop being a beginner. There is a working definition, and a short list of things the research-based guidance changes once you cross it: how many days, how heavy, how long to rest, and how much planning the weeks need.

Short answer: An intermediate lifter, in the American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 definition, has about six months of consistent resistance training; a novice has none or hasn't trained for several years. For intermediates the stand moves from two to three days a week to three to four, and widens loads from an 8 to 12 rep maximum to 1 to 12.1

"Intermediate" sounds like a strength level, but the American College of Sports Medicine's guidance defines it by time. Its 2009 position stand on progression sorts lifters into three groups: novices, with no resistance-training experience or none for several years; intermediates, with about six months of consistent training; and advanced lifters, with years of it.1 It's a label for programming, not a test you pass. This page covers what the stand changes at that point, what newer evidence adds, and a sample week.

If you're still in your first months, the page for you is newbie gains, and the plan is how to start lifting weights.

What changes when you're no longer a beginner

The 2009 stand's advice by level, for training aimed at strength:1

NoviceIntermediateAdvanced
WhoNo resistance-training experience, or none for several yearsAbout six months of consistent trainingYears of training
Days a week2 to 33 to 44 to 5
LoadsAn 8 to 12 rep maximumA wider range, 1 to 12 rep maximum, in a periodized fashion, eventually emphasizing heavy sets of 1 to 6 with 3 to 5 minutes' rest

For muscle growth, the same stand keeps the exercise choice and frequency similar, uses loads of 1 to 12 reps with the emphasis on 6 to 12 and 1 to 2 minutes' rest, and recommends higher-volume, multiple-set programs.1 Its rule for adding weight doesn't change with level: add 2 to 10% when you can do the current weight for one or two reps more than your target.1 How that works day to day: progressive overload.

Put plainly: more days, a wider spread of heavy and moderate sets instead of one rep range, longer rests on the heavy ones, and some planning across weeks.

What the newer evidence adds

The ACSM's 2026 stand, an overview of 137 systematic reviews, adds two cautions. Most of the reviews it covered examined people with minimal or no lifting experience, so much of the evidence comes from beginners; and its authors note an earlier analysis of theirs in which training experience had a minimal impact on strength and muscle-growth outcomes.2 The same stand found that, with appropriate progressive overload, periodization was not significantly better than a non-periodized program.2 So the "intermediate" changes are a sensible way to organize more training, not a different set of rules.

More training still helps, with smaller returns. A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 participants, adjusted for training status, found muscle size and strength rose with weekly sets, with diminishing returns that were considerably more pronounced for strength.3 Rest needs can change too: a 2018 review of 23 studies concluded that trained lifters seem to need more than two minutes between sets to get the most strength, where 60 to 120 seconds looked enough for people new to lifting.4

Signs you've moved past the beginner stage

A practical list, not a research cut-off:

  • About six months of steady training, the stand's working line,1 counted in weeks you actually trained, not months since you joined.
  • Adding weight every session has stopped working on your main lifts, even with good sleep and effort; the jumps now come every week or two.
  • You know the main lifts: you can set up, brace and finish a squat, deadlift and bench press without thinking through every step.
  • You keep a log and can say what you lifted a month ago (the workout log).

People respond to the same training very differently. In 585 adults who trained one arm for 12 weeks, one-rep-max gains ranged from 0% to +250%.5 Judge your own stage by your own log, not by anyone else's numbers.

A sample intermediate week

A practical layout of the stand's intermediate advice, not a protocol from a trial: four days, heavy and moderate days for the upper and lower body, with the heavy days inside the 1 to 6 range and its 3 to 5 minutes' rest, and the moderate days in the 6 to 12 range with 1 to 2 minutes.1 A 2024 meta-analysis of 14 studies found split and full-body routines did not significantly differ in strength or muscle growth when weekly volume was equated,6 so this split is one choice, not the only one.

DayMain liftsSets and reps
Monday: upper, heavyBench press, barbell row, overhead press, pull-up3 sets of 4 to 6; rest 3 to 5 minutes
Tuesday: lower, heavySquat, Romanian deadlift, calf raise3 sets of 4 to 6; rest 3 to 5 minutes
Thursday: upper, moderateIncline bench press, seated cable row, lateral raises, curls and triceps work3 sets of 8 to 12; rest 1 to 2 minutes
Friday: lower, moderateLeg press, Bulgarian split squat, leg curl3 sets of 8 to 12; rest 1 to 2 minutes

The upper and lower body are each trained twice a week. Add weight by the 2-to-10% rule above, write every set down, and take a deload week when the lifts stall for a couple of weeks despite good sleep and effort. The full four-day version: the upper/lower split workout plan. Five or six days instead: push/pull/legs. Planning heavier and lighter weeks: periodization.

Common questions

How long does it take to become an intermediate lifter?

The 2009 stand's line is about six months of consistent training.1 That's a definition for writing programs, not a countdown, and the 585-adult study above shows how much people differ.5

Are newbie gains over once you're an intermediate?

The fast early phase doesn't end on a date. The 2026 stand's authors found training experience had a minimal impact on strength and muscle-growth outcomes in an earlier analysis, while noting that most of the evidence is from beginners.2 More on newbie gains.

What's a good intermediate program?

One that gives you three to four days a week, the stand's intermediate frequency,1 a mix of heavy and moderate sets, and a written rule for adding weight. The sample week above is one; upper/lower, push/pull/legs and the 5-day split are others. Building your own: how to build a workout plan.

Is an intermediate lifter defined by how much you lift?

Not in the 2009 position stand, which defines the levels by training experience.1 If you want a number to compare against, a powerlifting total adjusted for bodyweight is one: the DOTS calculator.
