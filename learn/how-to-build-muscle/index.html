How to build muscle: the short list that drives growth, and where each piece is explained

Building muscle comes down to a short list, and none of it is a secret. Here's each piece in a paragraph, what the research behind it measured, and the page on this site that goes deeper: sets, effort, progression, reps, how often, protein, sleep and time.

Short answer: Do enough hard sets for each muscle every week, take them close to failure, add a little weight or a rep over time, eat enough protein and sleep. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 overview of 137 systematic reviews tied 10 or more sets per muscle group a week to more growth.3 Then repeat it for weeks.

Muscle grows when your training asks it for more than it can comfortably do and the rest of your week lets it recover. That's the whole idea. What follows is the short list of things the research says move it, each in a paragraph, with the page on this site that covers it properly. If you've never lifted, start with how to start lifting weights; this page is the map behind it.

It's a page about how muscle is built, not a promise about how much you'll build. The studies below measured groups of people; your own pace depends on where you start, how you train and things a page can't see.

1. Do enough hard sets for each muscle

The number that matters most is hard sets per muscle per week. A 2017 meta-analysis of 15 studies found a graded dose-response: each extra weekly set went with a little more growth.1 A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 participants found that muscle size kept rising with weekly sets, with diminishing returns.2 And the American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, an overview of 137 systematic reviews, found that 10 or more sets per muscle group a week enhanced muscle growth.3 How to count your own week, and where the returns flatten: how many sets per week.

2. Take most sets close to failure

A set only counts if it's hard. A 2024 series of exploratory meta-regressions found muscle growth improving as sets ended closer to failure, while strength gains were similar across a wide range.4 Close isn't the same as all the way: in a 2024 eight-week trial, 18 trained men and women grew their quads about as much stopping leg sets one or two reps short as going to failure.5 How to judge it from inside the set: reps in reserve.

3. Ask for a little more over time

Muscle adapts to what you ask of it, and then it has no reason to keep changing. Progressive overload keeps asking: a little more weight, a rep more, a set more. Weight isn't the only way. In an eight-week trial of 43 lifters with at least a year of lower-body training, one group added load and the other added reps at the same load; muscle growth differed little between them, and the authors judged both viable ways to progress.6 The systems for doing it, and what to do when you stall: progressive overload. You can't add to what you don't remember, which is what a workout log is for.

4. Use a rep range you can push hard

There's no single muscle-building rep range. A 2017 review of 21 studies found that light and heavy loads built similar muscle when sets were taken to failure, with heavy loads better for one-rep-max strength.7 So the range matters less than the effort inside it: heavier sets on the main lifts if strength matters to you, moderate sets on the rest, and nothing so light that the set never gets hard. The ranges in detail: how many reps.

5. Spread the sets however your week allows

How often matters less than how much. A 2019 meta-analysis of 25 studies found that once the weekly volume was equated, how often a muscle was trained made no meaningful difference to its growth, and concluded that people can choose how often to train a muscle based on personal preference.8 Two or three full-body sessions a week is the simplest way to start (the full-body workout plan); how often to train covers the other options.

6. Eat enough protein

This site's number for people who lift is 0.7 to 1 gram of protein per pound of bodyweight a day, about 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram. A meta-analysis of 49 studies found that protein supports muscle gain during training up to around 1.6 grams per kilogram of bodyweight a day, with no clear benefit beyond that.9 How to reach it, and what to do about calories: calories and protein. Whether to eat above maintenance or build muscle while losing fat is its own question: body recomposition.

7. Sleep, and leave room to recover

Training is the signal; the hours after it are when the body responds. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend that adults sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis.10 In a lab study of 13 healthy young adults, one night without sleep lowered the rate of muscle protein synthesis the next afternoon by 18% compared with a normal night.11 That was one night of no sleep, and it measured protein synthesis, not muscle growth, so it shows a direction rather than a size. Sleep and muscle growth covers these studies and a second one on five short nights. Days off are covered in rest days, and what soreness does and doesn't tell you in muscle soreness.

8. Give it weeks, and judge it by the logbook

Muscle changes before the mirror shows it. In 7 young volunteers on a hard leg-extension program three times a week, an increase in thigh muscle size was measurable after 20 days.12 In 25 sedentary men whose thighs were scanned every week through eight weeks of hard training, the authors placed the start of real growth, beyond early swelling, around weeks three to four, and the scans kept rising to week eight.13 Those are small groups on hard programs, measured by scanners; they describe what was measured, not a schedule for you. In the meantime, the weights and reps in your logbook are the thing to watch. More on timelines: how long it takes to build muscle.

Common questions

How do you build muscle fast?

The fastest version is the list above done every week. In the two small studies above (7 and 25 people on hard programs), scanners picked up growth at about three to four weeks; that's what was measured in those groups, not a schedule for you.12,13 The part in your control is the gap between the plan and the weeks you actually train.

Why am I not building muscle?

Check the list above against the logbook. Count each muscle's hard sets for a week against the 10 or more the 2026 stand tied to more growth;3 check that most of them end close to failure, not comfortably short of it (point 2); check that the weights or reps in the log have gone up at all in the last few weeks (point 3); then protein and sleep (points 6 and 7). Two cautions before changing anything. Growth takes weeks to show: in the 25-man scan study above, the authors placed significant growth around weeks three to four.13 And people differ: in 585 adults on the same 12 weeks of arm training, change in biceps size ranged from −2% to +59%.14

Is it different for women?

The list is the same. In a 2025 analysis of 29 studies that put healthy men and women aged 18 to 45 on the same program, muscle growth as a percentage of starting size was similar between the sexes.15 More in strength training for women.

How do you build muscle at home?

The same way as in a gym: enough hard sets each week, taken close to failure, with a little more over time. What changes at home is how you make a set hard. Load mattered less than effort in the studies: light and heavy loads built similar muscle when sets were taken to failure.7 In an eight-week trial of 18 young men, push-ups adjusted to the same load as a 40% bench press built muscle and strength about as well as the bench press did.16 So with no equipment, move to a harder version once a set passes about twenty reps, slow the lowering, and use one-leg and one-arm versions; a pair of dumbbells or a band adds load. Those steps are practical, not from the trials. Protein and sleep are the same as above. Plans: the bodyweight workout and the dumbbell workout plan.

Does it change after 40?

Much of the research above was done in younger adults: the participants in the 2026 meta-regression averaged about 25.2 Strength training over 40 covers the studies in older lifters.
