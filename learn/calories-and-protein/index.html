Calories and protein: the two numbers that matter most

You don't need a complicated meal plan. For most people, two numbers do most of the work. Here's how to find yours and what to do with them.

By Diogo·Updated October 8, 2026·3 min read

In this article

  1. Step 1: Find a starting calorie target
  2. Step 2: Set a protein goal
  3. Step 3: Track for a few weeks, then adjust
  4. Common mistakes
  5. Want it built around your life?

Nutrition advice is loud and contradictory. Keto, carnivore, fasting, six small meals, no carbs after 6 p.m. It's enough to make anyone give up.

Here's the thing: for most people trying to lose fat or build muscle, two numbers do most of the work. Calories decide whether your weight goes down, up or stays put. Protein helps you keep muscle, stay full and recover from training. Get those two roughly right and the rest is detail.

Step 1: Find a starting calorie target

Your body burns a certain number of calories a day just by existing, moving and training. That's your maintenance. Eat less than that and weight tends to drop. Eat more and it tends to rise.

A simple starting estimate, based on how active you are:

How active you areRough maintenance (per lb of bodyweight)
Mostly seated, lighter training12 to 14 calories
Moderately active, lifting 3 to 4 days a week14 to 16 calories
Very active job or lots of training16 to 18 calories

Example: someone at 180 lb who's moderately active starts around 180 × 15 = 2,700 calories for maintenance. It's an estimate, not a law. Your real number gets found by watching what happens.

Then pick a direction

  • Fat loss: start about 300 to 500 calories below maintenance. In the example, around 2,200 to 2,400.
  • Muscle gain: start about 200 to 300 above maintenance, so roughly 2,900 to 3,000.
  • Recomposition (lose fat and build muscle slowly): eat near maintenance, train hard and keep protein high. It's slower, but it works for many beginners.

Step 2: Set a protein goal

A solid, practical range for people who lift is 0.7 to 1 gram of protein per pound of bodyweight per day (about 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg). Aim toward the higher end if you're dieting, because protein helps protect muscle when calories are lower.

At 180 lb, that's about 125 to 180 grams a day. Don't panic about hitting an exact number. Landing close, most days, is the win.

Making it easy

  • Build every meal around a protein source: chicken, fish, lean beef, eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, tofu, whey.
  • Aim for roughly 30 to 40 grams in each of three or four meals.
  • If you're short at the end of the day, a shake or yogurt closes the gap.

Step 3: Track for a few weeks, then adjust

Your starting numbers are a hypothesis. The data tells you if it's right.

  • Track what you eat for a couple of weeks. You'll learn what portions really look like, which is the real skill here.
  • Weigh in a couple of times a week and watch the trend, not the day. Water, salt and food in your system can move the scale a few pounds overnight.
  • Give it two to three weeks before changing anything. If the trend isn't moving the way you want, adjust calories by about 100 to 200 and wait again.
GoalReasonable pace
Fat lossAbout 0.5% to 1% of bodyweight per week
Muscle gainAbout 0.25% to 0.5% of bodyweight per week

Faster isn't better. Crash dieting costs muscle, wrecks your energy and almost always ends in a rebound.

Common mistakes

  • Eating "healthy" and ignoring portions. Olive oil, nuts, granola and smoothies are good foods that add up fast.
  • Going too low, too soon. A big deficit feels productive for about a week, then falls apart.
  • Judging by one weigh-in. Look at weekly averages.
  • Never eating your favorite foods. That's how binges happen. Fit them in on purpose.

Not medical advice

These are general starting points for healthy adults. If you have a medical condition (including kidney disease), a history of disordered eating, or you're pregnant, nursing or on medication, talk with your doctor before changing how you eat.
