Squat form: setup, depth and the common faults

A good squat is a handful of checkpoints, not a feeling. Here's the setup step by step, what the research says about depth and knee travel, the barbell, dumbbell and machine versions, and the faults that show up as the weight climbs.

Short answer: Feet about shoulder-width apart, toes turned out a little, brace, then sit down between your heels with your knees following your toes, as deep as you can hold that position, and stand back up. Depth matters: a 2021 meta-analysis found full range of motion built more strength and leg muscle than partial reps.1

The squat is the lower-body lift many plans are built around. Common problems include a setup that skips a step, depth that shrinks as the weight climbs, heels that come off the floor. This page covers the setup step by step, how deep to squat and how to squat deeper, whether your knees can go past your toes, the barbell, dumbbell and machine versions, how to add weight over time, and the faults worth watching for.

Brand new to lifting? The first four weeks starts with a goblet squat or a leg press, and this page is the next step.

The setup, step by step

These are the checkpoints for a barbell back squat. The dumbbell and machine versions further down use the same ones, minus the rack.

  1. Set the bar at about upper-chest height in the rack, so you can lift it off with a small knee bend rather than on your toes.
  2. Get under it. The bar rests on your upper back, either high on the top of the shoulders or a few inches lower (the two positions are compared below). Hands just outside your shoulders.
  3. Stand it up and take two or three short steps back.
  4. Feet about shoulder-width apart, toes turned out a little. Adjust until the bottom position feels balanced; there's no single correct width.
  5. Breathe in and brace. A big breath, then tighten your midsection as if you were about to be poked in the stomach. Do it before every rep.
  6. Sit down between your heels. Hips and knees bend together, your knees travel in the direction your toes point, your whole foot stays on the floor, and the bar stays over the middle of your foot.
  7. Stand up the same way, with your chest and hips rising together, and breathe at the top.

Then film a set from the side, about level with your hips. Most of the faults below are easier to see on video than to feel mid-rep.

How deep to squat

As deep as you can control. The research on range of motion points one way:

  • The reviews. A 2021 meta-analysis of 16 studies found that training through a full range of motion built more strength and more lower-body muscle than partial reps.1 A 2020 systematic review of six studies (135 people, 127 of them men) found similar or greater lower-body muscle gains with full range of motion and called the evidence for the upper body limited and conflicting.2 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found that a complete range of motion enhanced strength, but too little data to say whether it changed muscle growth.3
  • Deep vs shallow squats. In a 12-week trial, 17 male students squatted either deep (knees bent to 120 degrees) or shallow (to 60 degrees). The deep group's front-thigh muscle grew more.4
  • Full vs half squats. In a 10-week trial, 17 men squatted twice a week, either full or half squats. The front of the thigh grew about the same in both groups, but the full-squat group's glutes and inner-thigh muscles grew more, and neither group's hamstrings grew.5

Those two trials are small and in men only, so read them as a direction, not a rule. A practical target: thighs at least parallel to the floor, and deeper if you can keep your heels down, your back in the position you set, and the bar over your feet. And since squats didn't grow the hamstrings in that last trial, a week of training needs a hinge as well: that's deadlift form.

Can your knees go past your toes?

Usually, yes. The old advice was to keep your shins vertical so your knees never pass your toes. In a small study of seven weight-trained men, blocking the knees from moving forward lowered the turning force (torque) at the knee by about a fifth but raised it at the hip about tenfold, and made the lifters lean further forward. The authors concluded that the knees may need to move slightly past the toes.6 A common cue is to keep your knees following your toes, not caving in toward each other.

Squatting deeper

The depth section above makes the case for a full range. If your squat stops short of it, the usual limits are your ankles and your hips:

  • In 101 healthy adults (64 men, 37 women), the men's squat depth went with ankle bend with the knee bent and with hip flexion range; the women's went with ankle bend with the knee straight and the strength of the muscles that pull the foot up.7
  • In 20 healthy people, back squat depth went with one measure only: ankle bend in a lunge test, with the knee bent.8
  • A 2026 meta-analysis of 14 studies (177 people) found that raising the heels increased ankle and knee range during the squat, but not hip or trunk range.9

The first two studies found links, not proof that a looser ankle deepens a squat. Still, try one change at a time:

  1. Raise your heels a little on small plates or a wedge.
  2. Practice the bottom with a goblet squat, pausing for a breath or two at your deepest controlled position.
  3. Try a slightly wider stance, with your toes turned out a little more.
  4. Train the ankles and hips: ankle mobility covers the knee-to-wall test and its drills, hip mobility the hip checks, and the mobility routine has the deep squat hold.

Some of the limit is structure, not tightness: a deep squat ends when the thigh meets the calf, however flexible you are.10 Squat as deep as you can control.

The pause squat

A pause squat is the same squat with a stop at the bottom: sit down to your depth, hold still for about two seconds without letting go of your brace, then stand up without bouncing. Taking the bounce away costs you in the moment; the trial below describes a consensus that the pause lowers performance compared with the usual rebound,11 so expect to use less weight than on your normal squat.

Over weeks, the pause holds up. In a ten-week trial, 26 men trained the squat either with a pause of about two seconds at the bottom of each rep or bouncing out of it. Both groups improved on most of the strength and bar-speed tests, and the pause group's gains were larger by effect size; on the jump test, the bounce group's gain was larger.11 The pause also makes reps easier to compare: in 17 resistance-trained athletes, a two-second pause made bar speed more consistent from one trial to the next across the whole range of loads, in the squat and the bench press (a coefficient of variation of 2.9% against 3.9% in the squat).12

To use it, run some of your squat sets as pause squats at a weight you can hold still at the bottom, heels down and back in the position you set. Count the two seconds; a rushed pause turns back into a bounce. Pausing seated on a box is the box squat, which isn't quite the same: the box takes some of your weight.

Barbell, dumbbell and machine versions

Each version below is the same movement with the weight in a different place, and the placement changes how upright you stay.

VersionWhere the weight sitsWhat changes
High-bar back squatHigh on the top of the shouldersA more upright torso, more knee bend, usually a deeper squat, more front-thigh activity
Low-bar back squatA few inches lower, across the back of the shouldersMore hip bend and forward lean, more glute, inner-thigh and back-muscle activity
Front squatAcross the front of the shouldersSimilar overall muscle activity to the back squat in one study
Zercher squatIn the crooks of the elbows, in front of youFront-loaded like the front squat, without its rack position; not yet studied by name
Overhead squatLocked out overhead, arms straightMuch lighter loads; slightly more front-of-trunk and less leg activity than the back squat in one study
Goblet squatOne dumbbell held against the chestThe beginner and home version; the load is limited by what you can hold
Smith machine squatA bar fixed on railsThe bar runs on rails, so you don't balance it; less muscle activity in one small study

The first two rows come from a 2017 review of high-bar and low-bar squats, which describes lifters moving differently to keep their center of mass over their feet: with the bar lower on the back, the torso leans further forward to balance it.13 In a study of 18 recreationally trained lifters (nine men, nine women), a low bar with a wide stance put more of the work on the hips, while a high bar with a narrow stance put more on the knees, with more front-thigh and less glute activity.14

For the front squat, a study of 15 healthy trained people found it as effective as the back squat in overall muscle recruitment.15 The rack position, the setup and the rest of that comparison are on the front squat. For the Smith machine, a study of six people found average muscle activity across seven leg and trunk muscles was 43% higher in the free-weight squat, each done at an eight-rep max.16 Six people is a very small sample, so take it as a hint. The goblet squat is the home version, and the three-day dumbbell plan opens Day A with it. With no weight at all, it's the bodyweight squat. Pick the version your gym has and you can do with clean positions, and keep it for a while.

Stance width, and squats for glutes

A wider stance and a lower bar shift the squat toward the hips, as the study above found. For the glutes specifically, depth has the stronger evidence. In the full-versus-half trial, the glutes grew about three times as much with full squats (6.7% vs 2.2%).5 That's growth measured over ten weeks; the stance study measured muscle activity during the lift itself, which is a weaker guide to what grows. So a squat for the glutes is a deep squat first and a wide one second. For the front of the thigh, a high bar and a narrower stance lean the other way.

Adding to your squat

There's no timeline here either. Adding to a squat comes down to a few habits the evidence supports:

  • Squat at least twice a week, as an early lift. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found strength was enhanced by two or more sessions a week and by doing an exercise at the beginning of a session rather than the end.3 How those two days fit together is your workout split's job.
  • Keep some sets heavy, with reps in hand. The same stand found strength was enhanced by heavier loads, at least 80% of a one-rep max, for two to three sets.3 Stop those sets with two or three reps in reserve, as the faults below suggest.
  • Rest three to five minutes between heavy sets of one to six reps, as the ACSM's 2009 stand recommends.17
  • Earn each jump. When you can do one or two reps more than you planned at a weight, the 2009 stand suggests adding 2 to 10%.17 Progressive overload covers how to pick the jump.
  • Hold the depth. A heavier squat that stops higher isn't a stronger squat: the 2021 meta-analysis above found full range built more strength than partial reps.1

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Knees caving in. On the way up, the knees drift toward each other. Push your knees out in the direction of your toes, and lighten the load until they stay there.
  • Heels lifting. The weight drifts onto your toes. Sit down between your heels rather than forward over your toes, and try a slightly wider stance.
  • The chest dropping on the way up. The hips rise first and the squat turns into a lean-over. Rebrace before each rep, drive your chest and hips up together, and drop the weight if it keeps happening.
  • Depth that shrinks as the weight climbs. Keep the depth and let the weight wait; the reviews above favor the full range.
  • Grinding every set to the last rep. A 2025 review of 22 studies in experienced lifters (293 people) found that heavier loads were consistently linked to a slower bar and more variable joint movement, and fatigue slowed the bar too.18 Keep two or three reps in reserve on heavy squats. (Here's how to judge reps left.)
  • Squatting through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
