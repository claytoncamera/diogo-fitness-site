Mind-muscle connection: what the trials show, and when to use it

Think about the muscle, not the weight. The mind-muscle connection is one of the oldest ideas in the gym, and it now has trials behind it. Here's what it means, what the trials found for muscle activity, growth and strength, when it helps and when it gets in the way, and how to practise it on a curl and a row.

Short answer: The mind-muscle connection means focusing on the muscle you're training rather than on moving the weight. At light and moderate loads it raises that muscle's activity, and in one eight-week trial in untrained men it grew the elbow flexors (the biceps and its neighbours) more.1,7 For a single all-out effort, focusing on the movement works better.8

The mind-muscle connection is a way of paying attention while you lift. Instead of thinking about moving the weight, you think about the muscle doing it: the biceps shortening on a curl, the back pulling on a row. Researchers call that an internal focus. Its opposite, an external focus, puts your attention on the result of the movement: the bar travelling up, the floor pushed away.

Both have been tested. This page covers what the trials found for muscle activity, muscle growth and strength, when an internal focus helps and when it doesn't, and how to practise it.

What the trials show: muscle activity

Most of the studies measure muscle activity with electrodes on the skin over a few reps. Telling people to focus on one muscle usually turns that muscle up, within limits:

  • On the bench press, up to moderate loads. In 18 resistance-trained men, focusing on the chest or the triceps raised that muscle's activity at 20% to 60% of their one-rep max, but not at 80%.1 In 11 male college football players lifting half their max, focusing on the chest raised chest activity by 22%, and focusing on the triceps raised triceps activity by 26%; at 80%, triceps activity didn't change with either instruction, though the chest instruction still raised chest activity.2
  • On the lat pulldown, in beginners. In eight women with little or no lifting background, instruction on using the lats raised lat activity, while biceps activity didn't change.3
  • On a machine shoulder press. In 31 healthy young adults with no training experience, lifting 40% of their max, focusing on the shoulders or on the triceps raised activity in that muscle, and activity in the upper trapezius didn't change.4
  • On push-ups, with experience. In 18 resistance-trained men with about eight years of training on average, focusing on the chest raised chest activity by 9 percentage points of its maximum. Focusing on the triceps didn't raise its activity significantly on average, but the more years a man had trained, the better he could do it.5
  • Less across a whole set to failure. In 20 people with little or no lifting experience doing a seated row, instructions to use the back muscles raised lat activity in the first reps, but had little effect across a whole set taken to failure.6

Turning one muscle up didn't mean switching the others off. In the 18-man bench press study, focusing on the triceps raised chest activity too at 50% and 60% of max,1 and the authors of the pulldown study say the increase probably doesn't represent isolation of the muscle.3 And activity is measured during a set; it isn't growth over weeks.

What the trials show: muscle growth

One trial has tested it over weeks. For eight weeks, 30 untrained college-aged men trained three times a week, four sets of 8 to 12 reps per exercise, with either an internal focus on contracting the target muscle or an external focus on the outcome of the lift. The internal group's elbow flexors, the biceps and its neighbours, thickened 12.4% against 6.9%; quadriceps thickness changed about the same in both groups.7

So the evidence for growth is one small trial in young men, with a difference in the arms and none in the thighs. It's a reason to try an internal focus on arm exercises, not proof that it grows every muscle.

What the trials show: strength

For strength, the outward focus comes out ahead in the moment. A 2021 meta-analysis of 10 studies found, in seven studies of single efforts, that an external focus raised strength on the spot. Over weeks of training, three long-term studies showed no significant difference between the two kinds of focus overall, with an edge for the external focus in lower-body strength.8 In the eight-week trial above, the strength differences between the groups weren't statistically significant either.7

When it helps and when it doesn't

  • Light and moderate sets. In the bench press studies, the effect held up to about 60% of a max. At 80% it disappeared in one study; in the other, the chest instruction still raised chest activity, but the triceps instruction did nothing.1,2
  • Single-joint exercises and muscles that are hard to feel. Curls, flies, raises and the lats on a pulldown are natural places to try it, and the one growth result was in the arms.
  • Not for a heavy single or a max set. When the goal is the most weight, think about the bar or the floor, not the muscle.
  • Less as a set gets hard. In the seated row study, the effect was clearest in the first reps.
  • The glutes. In 30 young women doing a bridge, verbal and touch cues to use the glutes raised glute activity, and hamstring activity rose by a similar amount, so the cue did not single out the glutes.9 A cue can turn the target up without turning the rest down.

For the chest, the bench press and push-up studies above are the evidence; for the lats, the pulldown and row studies. The cues on the back workout are the same idea for rows.

How to practise it on a curl and a row

On a curl, with a weight you could lift about 15 times:

  1. Before the set, flex the biceps hard with no weight for a few seconds, so you know what it feels like working.
  2. Curl slowly, thinking about the biceps shortening and pulling the forearm up, not about lifting the dumbbell.
  3. Keep the elbow still at your side. If it drifts forward, the shoulder is taking over.
  4. Squeeze for a second at the top, then lower over two or three seconds.

On a one-arm row, dumbbell or cable:

  1. Start each rep by drawing the shoulder blade back, then pull the elbow.
  2. Think of your hand as a hook and drive the elbow back past your ribs: the back pulls, the arm follows.
  3. Pause with the elbow back, then let the arm travel forward under control.

Practise on light warm-up sets, then keep the same attention on working sets of 8 to 15 reps. On the heavy compound lifts, switch back to the movement: drive the floor away on a squat, push the bar off your chest on a bench press. More curl and row choices are on the arm workout, the concentration curl and the dumbbell row; for the chest, dips and the presses in the chest workout; for the glutes, the glute workout.

What it isn't

A burn or a pump isn't the measure: none of the trials above measured either. The measure is the same as for any training, more weight or more reps on the same exercise over the weeks (progressive overload). An internal focus is a tool for some sets, not a substitute for hard ones.

And no cue fixes an exercise that hurts instead of working the muscle. If something hurts sharply or keeps hurting, stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
