Desk stretches: simple moves for your chair, and how often to get up

A day at a desk is hours in one position. These are moves you can do in your chair or right beside it, how long to hold each one, a five-minute routine, and what the studies of office workers actually measured.

Short answer: Desk stretches are simple moves you can do in or beside your chair: neck tilts, shoulder rolls, wrist stretches, an overhead reach, a seated twist and a forward curl. Hold most gently for 10 to 20 seconds, never into pain. An occupational safety guide also advises a 5 to 10 minute break every hour, ideally standing and walking.

Desk stretches are moves you can do in your chair, or standing right beside it, without changing clothes or getting on the floor. This page lists them as movements: what to do and how long to hold. It doesn't claim that any of them fixes or prevents a problem. If you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, stop and ask a doctor or a physical therapist.

The moves and hold times below come from the Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety's fact sheet on stretching at the workstation, unless a row says practical.1 A full stretching session for flexibility, done on the floor, is the stretching routine.

How long to hold, and how often to get up

  • Hold gently, 10 to 20 seconds. The fact sheet gives that as a reasonable hold for stretches in general (the rows above give the exceptions), with normal breathing, no bouncing and no locked joints. You should feel a stretch, not pain; if you feel pain or severe discomfort, stop.1
  • Get up every hour. The same fact sheet recommends a break of 5 to 10 minutes for every hour at a workstation, ideally standing and walking, and says some of the stretches can be done in that time.1
  • Standing up and stretching made the biggest difference in one lab study. The study ran in two parts, with office workers and university students who do the same kind of computer work. First, in 24 healthy adults aged 20 to 25, muscle activity readings (EMG) of the trapezius and latissimus dorsi, in the shoulders and back, showed signs of fatigue after about 40 minutes of computer work. Then, in 48 others aged 19 to 24, a 5-minute break spent standing and stretching was the most effective of the breaks tested, against sitting in an armchair or walking around, for 5 or 10 minutes, and held the muscles at a recovered level for about 30 to 45 minutes.2

A five-minute desk routine

Practical: one pass of this is about five minutes. Do it once an hour, or at least once in the morning and once in the afternoon, and stand up for it when you can.

What studies of stretching at work measured

Many studies of stretching at work measure pain or discomfort, and this page makes no claim about either. These measured something else:

  • Hamstring flexibility, in women at work. In a trial at a fruit and vegetable company, 58 women with an average age of about 44 were split into two groups. One group did three hamstring stretches of 20 seconds each, three times a week for 12 weeks, at work. Their scores on a toe-touch test and a straight-leg-raise test went up; the group that didn't stretch showed a non-significant drop.3
  • Range of motion, in office workers. In 161 office workers without major musculoskeletal disorders, supervised static stretching on a stretching machine, 10 minutes twice a week for three months, increased range of motion in every test except hip extension. The people who started least flexible gained the most.4

Neither study tested the chair stretches in the table above. What they show is that regular stretching at work changed range of motion in those workers; whether a few desk stretches a day do the same hasn't been tested.

Desk stretches or a stretching routine?

  • Desk stretches break up the hours in one position. Short, frequent, in your work clothes.
  • A stretching routine is for flexibility: longer holds, more muscle groups, a few times a week.
  • A mobility routine is for lifters who want range in the squat and overhead positions.
  • Training is the rest of the week: a desk job with three short sessions a week is the workout plan for remote workers.

Common questions

How often should I do desk stretches?

The occupational safety fact sheet recommends a 5 to 10 minute break every hour at a workstation, standing and walking if you can.1 Practical: put the five-minute routine into that break.

What are desk stretches for the neck and shoulders?

The neck tilt and turn, the head glide, shoulder shrugs, shoulder rolls, the seated chest stretch and the cross-body arm, the first six rows of the table. This page makes no claim that they relieve neck or shoulder pain; if you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, stop and ask a doctor or a physical therapist.

What are desk stretches for the lower back?

The seated twist, the seated back curl and the standing lean back. The same goes for pain here: if you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, stop and ask a doctor or a physical therapist.

Can I do them standing?

Most of them, yes, and standing up is part of the point: the fact sheet's hourly break is ideally on your feet.1 The seated twist and the back curl need the chair.

What if a stretch hurts?

Stop. You should feel a stretch, not pain.1 If you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, stop and ask a doctor or a physical therapist.
