The 12-3-30 workout: what it is, what it measured in the lab, and how to work up to it

Twelve percent incline, three miles an hour, thirty minutes. It went around the internet as a workout and arrived at a lab in 2025, which is where the numbers on this page come from. Here's what it is and who named it, what the study measured, what walking uphill asks of your legs, how it compares with jogging, why the rails change the answer, and how to work up to the full version.

Short answer: The 12-3-30 workout is 30 minutes of treadmill walking at a 12% incline and 3 miles an hour: a mile and a half, roughly 950 feet of climb. The 12-3-30 workout is one treadmill setting held for half an hour: a 12% incline, 3 miles an hour, 30 minutes. Walk, don't run, and don't change anything. At 3 miles an hour you cover a mile and a half, and at a 12% grade that's roughly 950 feet of vertical climb, which is why a workout that is "just walking" leaves your calves and glutes with an opinion.

This page covers where the name came from, what the one lab study to measure it found, what walking uphill trains, how it compares with jogging, why holding the rails changes the answer, and how to work up to the full version if 12% at 3 miles an hour is too much on day one. It's about what was measured, not a promise about the scale. Steps and walking in general are on walking for fat loss; the pace that keeps any session steady is on zone 2 cardio.

Where it came from

The workout was developed by Lauren Giraldo, a social-media creator, and spread through her YouTube and TikTok videos, where she described doing it about five days a week and gave her own account of losing weight with it. A 2025 study in the International Journal of Exercise Science, by its own account the first peer-reviewed test of the routine, notes those claims and that no scientific study had evaluated them before.1 One person's account is one person's account; what the study measured is below, and no trial has followed people doing 12-3-30 for weeks.

What the lab measured

The 2025 study put 16 adults, 7 women and 9 men, through two sessions in a lab: the 12-3-30 workout as written, and a self-paced treadmill run that stopped when it had used the same amount of energy. Fourteen completed both. Everyone was 18 or older and already active at least three times a week.1 The results, as means:

Measure12-3-30Self-paced run
Time to use the same energy30 minutesAbout 24 minutes
Energy usedAbout 308 kilocaloriesAbout 310 (matched by design)
Energy per minuteAbout 10.2 kilocaloriesAbout 13.1
Share of energy from fat during the sessionAbout 41%About 33%

The women in the study averaged about 254 kilocalories over the 30 minutes and the men about 337.1 Two things to take from the table. The 12-3-30 workout used energy more slowly than running, so it took longer to use the same amount. And it drew a larger share of that energy from fat during the session, which the authors are careful to say is not the same as losing more fat: in their words, increasing energy expenditure is the main determinant for weight loss, so a run that uses the same energy in less time is the more time-efficient option, and 12-3-30 may suit people who aren't in a hurry.1

Your own number will differ with your weight and your treadmill. The study was small and the participants were mostly college-aged and already active, which its authors list as a limitation.1

What walking uphill trains

  • The cost climbs with the grade. A 2023 paper on the energetics of walking on slopes describes the metabolic cost of transport rising linearly with gradient on uphill and downhill grades steeper than 15%, with a nonlinear relationship on shallower ones; and in its own experiment, spending time on a mix of steeper and shallower grades cost about 8% more energy than the same climb at one fixed grade.2 The paper's own fixed uphill grade was 10.5%; 12-3-30 is a little steeper than that.
  • The hips do the most extra work. In 10 young adults, 5 men and 5 women, walking up a 9-degree grade, a little steeper than 12%, raised the activity of the hip extensors most of all compared with level walking, with the knee and ankle extensors up as well, and the rise grew with walking speed.3 That's the glutes and hamstrings, then the front of the thighs and the calves.
  • Smaller peak accelerations than jogging, at the same energy. In 19 physically inactive adults aged 18 to 31, walking at a 20% grade and jogging on the level for ten minutes each, matched for energy, the jog produced higher peak accelerations at the foot and the pelvis and more activation of the shin and front-thigh muscles.4 That was a 20% grade, steeper than 12-3-30; the page makes no claim beyond what was measured.

12-3-30 vs jogging

The question under most searches is whether walking uphill replaces running. Two studies matched the two for energy.

  • No difference in oxygen or heart rate; less enjoyment. In 22 adults aged 19 to 31, twenty minutes of walking at a 20% grade and twenty minutes of level jogging, matched for energy, produced no significant difference in oxygen uptake or heart rate, about 170 kilocalories each, and a rise in rated effort over the session that didn't differ between the two; the jog scored higher on an enjoyment scale afterwards, and the authors' summary is that the participants preferred it.5
  • Slower per minute. In the 12-3-30 study, the run matched for energy took about 24 minutes against 30.1

So incline walking and jogging matched for energy asked about the same of the heart and lungs in the first study, the walk took longer in the second, and the people in the first study preferred the jog. If you'd rather walk, 12-3-30 is a fair substitute; if you'd rather run, nothing here says you shouldn't. If you'd rather climb real steps, the stair climber is the other version of the same idea.

Why the rails change the answer

Holding the treadmill's rails on a steep incline shifts some of your weight onto your arms, and the console still credits you with the full climb. In 10 healthy adults aged 38 to 60 walking at speeds and grades set to 75, 85 and 95% of their ventilatory threshold, gripping the handrails lowered heart rate and oxygen uptake at every stage compared with a free arm swing, and resting the hands on the rails lowered them too, by less.6 The study's point was that handrail support blunts what the session asks of you. If you can't do 12% at 3 miles an hour without holding on, the fix is the next section, not your hands.

How to work up to it

Three numbers, and you only need to be honest about which one is too big. Keep the 30 minutes and lower one of the other two until you can walk the whole half hour with your hands free and a sentence still possible.

If a week's step is still hard with your hands free, repeat it. Shorter legs, a heavier body or a treadmill that reads high all make 12% at 3 miles an hour a bigger ask; the setting is a target, not a rule. Three sessions a week of 30 minutes, with a brisk walk or two, reaches the World Health Organization's floor of 150 moderate minutes, or 75 vigorous;7 which of those a 12-3-30 session is for you depends on your fitness, and the talk test is the check.

12-3-30 beside lifting

It's leg work, so it belongs after lifting or a day away from a heavy lower-body session. One trial is the reason to be careful about placing it: in 30 untrained 18-to-23-year-olds, twice-weekly incline treadmill walking or cycling for nine weeks beside leg training left leg-press gains below lifting alone, and the men who cycled gained more than the men who walked uphill.8 That was two sessions a week on top of lifting, with strength as the measured result; cardio and lifting covers the order, the spacing and how big the interference effect is in general.

Common questions

How many calories does 12-3-30 burn?

About 308 kilocalories on average in the one study that measured it, in 14 recreationally active adults: about 254 for the women and 337 for the men.1 Your own number will differ.

Does 12-3-30 give results?

No trial has followed people doing 12-3-30 for weeks, so there are no results to report; the lab study measured one session, and the weight-loss claims around the workout are its creator's own account.1 What's known is what one session costs, above, and that it counts toward the weekly activity guideline.7

Is 12-3-30 better than running?

Matched for energy, incline walking and level jogging asked about the same of the heart and lungs in 22 young adults, and they preferred the jog;5 the walk took longer to use the same energy in the 12-3-30 study.1 Better is whichever one you'll do three times a week.
