Muscle-up: what it takes, the parts to train, and strict or kipping

A pull-up that keeps going: you pull high enough to get your chest over the bar, turn your wrists over it, and press up into a dip. It's an advanced skill. Here's what it's made of, what the research says about each part, how to build toward it, strict or kipping, bar or rings, the faults, and where it fits in a week.

Short answer: A muscle-up is a pull-up, a turn of the wrists over the bar and a dip, done in one move. It's an advanced skill, so train the parts first: strict pull-ups pulled high, straight-bar dips and slow negatives. A muscle-up takes you from hanging below a bar or a pair of rings to supporting yourself above them with straight arms, in one movement. It has three parts: a pull, high enough that your chest gets close to the bar; a transition, where your wrists roll over the bar and your chest moves over your hands; and a dip, pressing up to straight arms. It's an advanced bodyweight skill, and you don't need it to build a strong back and arms.

This page covers what each part asks of you, what the research says about those parts, how to build toward a first rep, strict or kipping, bar or rings, the faults, and where it fits in a week. The pull-up itself is on how to do a pull-up, and the dip on how to do dips.

What it takes

  • The pull. A pull-up that keeps accelerating past the chin, toward the lower chest. The muscles are the pull-up's: in 21 men and 4 women doing pull-ups and chin-ups, the lats, the big muscles down the sides of the back, were the most active muscles measured, followed by the biceps.1
  • The transition. Your wrists turn over the bar while your chest leans over your hands. It's the part that's mostly skill, and the part no study found for this page has measured.
  • The dip. A straight-bar dip from the bottom position. In 13 experienced men, bar dips produced higher peak muscle activity than dips off a bench, and ring dips had similar peaks to bar dips, with three muscles rising further.2

A PubMed search for this page found six records that mention the muscle-up, and none of them tests how to train or learn it. So the steps below put together what's known about the pull and the dip with the usual coaching cues, labelled as such. And there's no timeline here: when the first rep comes depends on where you start, and no study found for this page measured it.

Before you try one

A practical sign, not a research finding, is that you're ready to practice the full movement when you can do several strict pull-ups with your chest reaching the bar and several straight-bar dips with your shoulders going below your elbows, all without swinging. Until then, train the parts:

  1. High pull-ups. Strict pull-ups, pulling as high as you can on every rep, aiming the bar at your lower chest.
  2. Slow negatives. Start above the bar and lower through the dip and the pull-up over several seconds. A 2009 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials found that eccentric, or lowering, training done at higher intensities raised strength more than concentric, or lifting, training, with gains more specific to the speed and type of muscle action trained.3
  3. Straight-bar dips. Hands on a single bar in front of you, dipping until your upper arms are about level and pressing back up.
  4. Grip. On rings, the usual cue for the transition is a false grip, with the ring sitting further into your palm, and it asks more of your forearms. In 30 physically inactive young men doing an eight-week pull-up program, the group that added forearm and grip training gained the most pull-up reps.4
  5. The transition, low. On low rings or a low bar with your feet on the floor, practice rolling your chest over your hands while your legs take some of the weight.

How to do a muscle-up on a bar

  1. Hang with an overhand grip about shoulder-width, thumbs around the bar or beside it, arms straight.
  2. Pull hard and fast, driving your elbows down and back, and keep pulling past your chin toward your lower chest.
  3. Lean your chest over the bar as it reaches you, letting your wrists roll over so your hands end under your shoulders.
  4. Press up into the dip until your arms are straight.
  5. Lower with control back through the dip and the pull to a hang. That's one rep.

Strict or kipping

A kipping muscle-up uses a swing from the hips and legs to help the pull. No study found for this page measured it on the muscle-up, but one study measured the same trade on the pull-up. In 11 athletes doing sets of five strict and five kipping pull-ups, the kip raised activity in the abs, the obliques and the hip muscles and lowered biceps activity, and the authors suggest strict pull-ups for more upper-body muscle activity and kipping for more full-body activity, with the potential for more reps.5

The practical reading: learn the strict version first, since the swing hides a weak pull, and keep kipping as a separate skill for when the goal is reps.

Bar or rings

On a bar, your hands stay put, so the bar has to travel around your chest and the transition needs a higher pull. On rings, the rings move with you and can come in close to your body, which can make the transition easier but asks more control at the top, the way ring dips asked more of some muscles than bar dips.2 That's a practice reading of the two, not a trial. Pick the one you have access to and stay with it while you learn.

Muscle-up vs pull-up

A pull-up ends with your chin over the bar; a muscle-up keeps going, over the bar and up to straight arms, so it adds a higher pull, the transition and a dip. If your goal is a stronger back and arms, pull-ups and dips trained separately cover the same muscles with more control over the load. The muscle-up is a skill goal, and a fine one if it's yours.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Pulling only to the chin. The bar never gets low enough on your body to get over. Train high pull-ups until the bar reaches your lower chest.
  • Pulling straight up into the bar. Pull the bar toward your hips, not your face, so your chest can move over it.
  • One arm getting over first. It's called a chicken wing. Slow down, go back to negatives, and keep both elbows moving together.
  • Swinging to make up for a weak pull. If you need a big kip, the strict parts aren't ready yet.
  • Dropping from the top. Lower through the dip and the pull under control, or step down from a box.
  • Pushing through something that hurts. Sore muscles the next day are normal; a sharp or lasting hurt in the shoulders, elbows or wrists isn't. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • Practice early in a session, when you're fresh: three to five sets of one to three reps of the hardest step you can do cleanly, with full rests.
  • Keep the parts in your week. Pull-ups and dips stay the strength work; the American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, which covers healthy adults, advises at least two sets per exercise.6
  • Count it as pulling and pushing. A set of muscle-ups works the same muscles as your pull-ups and dips, so take it from those totals rather than adding it on top.
  • Move up one step at a time. When a step is clean for all its reps, try the next one. (Progressive overload covers adding difficulty.)
