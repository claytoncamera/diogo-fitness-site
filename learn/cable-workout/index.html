Cable workout: one full session on a single cable station

One adjustable pulley, a handle, a rope and a bar cover a whole session. Here's what a cable does that a dumbbell doesn't, an eight-exercise session for one station, and what the trials say when cables and free weights go head to head.

Short answer: On one adjustable cable station, do eight exercises: a pull-through, a one-arm chest press, a kneeling one-arm pulldown, a row, a lateral raise, an overhead triceps extension, a curl and a Pallof press. Two or three sets of 10 to 15, stopping one or two reps short. Cables build muscle about as well as free weights.4

A cable station is a weight stack with a pulley you can slide up and down a column, and a clip for a handle, a rope or a short bar. One station, set at three heights, covers every muscle group in a single session.

This page is that session, what a cable does differently from a dumbbell, and when that difference matters. The exercises for each region in depth live on their own pages, linked below, so this one stays a single session you can run at one station.

What a cable does differently

The pull comes from the pulley, not the floor. A dumbbell always pulls straight down. A cable pulls along its own line, toward wherever you set the pulley: from below, from the side, from above. That's how a cable can load a lateral raise from the very bottom, a curl with the arm drawn back, or a press while you stand.

"Constant tension" is half true. The weight on the stack doesn't change during the rep, but how hard the rep is on the muscle still does. Researchers call it the resistance profile: how torque demands vary across a joint's range of motion. A dumbbell lateral raise has less torque at the bottom, where the side delt is longest; a cable raise can be set up to load that same position more.1 Move the pulley and you move the hardest part of the rep.

Small, quick changes. A pin moves the load in one step and the next exercise is a clip away, which is why a full session fits one station.

When cables beat free weights, and when they don't

  • For growth, the trials are a draw. 24 trained lifters trained one arm with a dumbbell lateral raise and the other with a cable version, five sets to failure twice a week for eight weeks; side-delt thickness rose 3.3% to 4.6%, with no meaningful difference between the arms.1
  • The angle a cable allows can matter. In a 12-week trial, 21 adults did cable triceps extensions with one arm overhead and the other at their side. The overhead arm's whole triceps grew 19.9% against 13.9%, even though that arm lifted lighter weights.2 The cable made the overhead position easy to load.
  • Not every angle does. In 24 untrained men over ten weeks, cable curls with the upper arm drawn back or kept at the side grew the elbow flexors by about 7% to 9% either way.3
  • Machines against free weights overall. A 2023 meta-analysis of 13 studies with 1,016 participants found no difference between free-weight and machine training compared directly, on strength or on muscle size; people got stronger on whatever they trained on.4 In one of those comparisons, 46 young adults (26 of them women) trained with free weights or machines for eight weeks: biceps and thigh muscle thickness rose with no difference between the groups, and the machine group gained more on the machine bench press, 13.9% against 8.6%.5 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found strength wasn't affected by free weights versus machines, and too little data to say for muscle size.6

So cables don't build more muscle than free weights. They earn their place where a free weight can't put the load: the bottom of a raise, the arm overhead, a pull from the side for the core. For a heavy squat or deadlift, or a barbell max you want to test, free weights stay the tool.

The session: eight exercises, one station

Set the pulley three times: low, chest height, high. Group the exercises by height so you move it as little as possible. A session takes about forty-five minutes.

By body part

Each region has its own page with the studies and the weekly set counts. What the cable adds to each:

  • Chest: the standing press and a cable fly add chest work without more barbell pressing. The chest workout puts the fly after the presses.
  • Back: a row and a pulldown fill both pulling slots, and the pull-through covers the hinge. The back workout has the grip and elbow details.
  • Shoulders: the lateral raise from the bottom, and a face pull or reverse fly for the rear. See the shoulder workout.
  • Triceps and biceps: the overhead extension is the triceps exercise with the trial behind it; the pushdown is the second. Curls with a bar or a handle, either arm position, and a reverse curl for the forearms. The arm workout counts how much your week needs.
  • Core: the Pallof press and the kneeling cable crunch. The core workout explains what each one trains.

How to progress on a stack

Add reps to the top of the range first, then move one pin. If one pin is too big a jump, stay where you are and keep adding reps until the heavier pin fits inside the range. Note the pulley height and where you stood as well as the weight, because a step closer or farther changes the rep. And because a cable's load is easy to change, it's easy to drift heavier with worse reps: the weight only counts if the rep looks the same.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the stack pull you. If the weight drags you toward the pulley, step away or brace in a split stance before you add load.
  • Swinging the body. On raises, curls and pushdowns, your body stays still and only the working joint moves.
  • Banging the stack. The plates shouldn't touch between reps; control the return.
  • The wrong height. Set the pulley so the cable is already pulling at the start of the rep.
  • Pushing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
