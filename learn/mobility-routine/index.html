A mobility routine for lifters: ten minutes for the range your lifts use

For a lifter, mobility is a means, not a goal: enough range to reach the positions your lifts ask for. Here's what mobility and flexibility mean, what changes range of motion over weeks (your lifts are part of it), and a ten-minute routine for the joints the big lifts use.

Short answer: Flexibility is how far your soft tissues let a joint move, one trainable part of its range;1 mobility, as lifters use the word, is range you can reach and control in a lift. Lifting with weights improved range of motion about as much as stretching in a 2023 meta-analysis.3 For more range, add a ten-minute routine.

Search suggestions for a mobility routine lean toward runners, desk workers and older adults. This one is for people who lift, and it starts from a narrower question: does your range of motion stop any lift you do from reaching its position? If your squat, hinge, press and pull all get where they need to go, the lifts are already doing most of the work. If one doesn't, a short routine aimed at that joint is the tool. If what you want is general flexibility rather than range for your lifts, the full-body stretching routine fits better.

Below: mobility versus flexibility in plain words, what the research says changes range over weeks, and a ten-minute routine for the ankles, hips, upper back and shoulders. Getting ready for today's heavy sets is a different job, the warm-up, and so are the few minutes after a session, the cool-down.

Mobility vs flexibility, in plain words

The words get used loosely, even by researchers. A 2026 paper by five sports scientists draws the line that matters. Range of motion is how far a joint actually moves. Flexibility, the give in the muscles, tendons and other soft tissues around it, is only one trainable part of that range; bone and joint structure set limits that training doesn't change. Reaching a full range also takes control of the muscles moving the joint, which is what strength training trains. And "mobility" is used in so many different ways that the authors set the word aside.1

For lifting, these working definitions are enough:

WordWhat it meansTrainable?
Range of motionHow far a joint moves, measured in degreesPartly: the soft-tissue part and your control of it, not the bones
FlexibilityHow far your soft tissues let the joint goYes, with stretching or with lifting
Mobility (as lifters use it)The range you can reach and control inside a movement, often with a loadYes: flexibility plus strength at the end of the range

The same paper gives a lifter's example of a limit that isn't flexibility: a deep squat stops when the thigh meets the calf, however flexible you are.1 So the target isn't the most range possible. It's enough range, under control, for the lifts you do.

What changes range of motion over weeks

Two large meta-analyses answer it, and they agree more than most people expect:

  • Stretching works. A 2024 meta-analysis of 77 studies, with 3,870 participants averaging 27 years old, found regular stretching increased range of motion with a moderate effect. Static holds and PNF stretching, which pairs a hold with a muscle contraction, produced more range than dynamic or bouncing stretches, and women gained more than men. Neither the total time spent stretching nor the number of sessions a week was linked to how much range people gained, and the analysis showed signs of reporting bias.2
  • So does lifting. A 2023 meta-analysis of 55 studies, with 2,756 healthy participants averaging 24 years old, found resistance training increased range of motion, also with a moderate effect. Free weights, machines and bands all did; exercises using only body weight showed no significant change. Untrained and sedentary people changed more than trained or active ones.3
  • Neither beat the other. In that 2023 analysis, seven studies compared resistance training with stretching and found no difference in range gained, and four found that stretching plus lifting gained no more than stretching alone. The authors concluded that, since lifting with external loads can improve range of motion, extra stretching before or after lifting may not be necessary for flexibility.3

Two more findings shape the routine below:

  • One session doesn't last. A 2016 review of stretching's short-term effects found the gains in range typically lasted under 30 minutes.4 The lasting change comes from repeating it over weeks.
  • Stop, and some of it slips. A 2025 meta-analysis of 13 studies (556 people, aged about 6 to 66) followed stretching programs of 5 to 15 weeks with 2 to 6 weeks off. Range stayed above where people started, but dropped a little from where they finished.5

One limit to keep in mind: these studies measured range in joint tests, such as a sit-and-reach or the angle at a hip, knee or shoulder. Whether a gain on a test shows up as a deeper squat or a cleaner overhead press is not what they measured.

Your lifts already count

For a lifter, the 2023 finding is the useful one. Every squat to depth, every Romanian deadlift lowered until the hamstrings are fully stretched and every press lowered all the way is time spent near the end of a range, under load. A 2025 consensus statement from 20 international stretching experts points to full-range resistance training as an alternative to stretching for long-term range, especially with attention to the lowering phase.6

So the first mobility rule costs nothing: lift through the full range you can control. Squat form covers how deep, and the Romanian deadlift covers where the hinge stops. Add a routine when a lift can't reach its position yet, or when you want more range than your lifts give you.

The routine: ten minutes, four areas

Do all four areas if you're not sure which one holds you back; otherwise pick the ones your lifts need. Hold each stretch at a gentle-to-moderate pull: in the 2024 meta-analysis, harder stretches produced no more range than gentler ones.2

AreaExerciseHowLifts that use the range
AnklesKnee-to-wall rocksFoot a hand's width from a wall, heel down, push the knee toward the wall and back. 10 slow reps a sideSquats, lunges
AnklesWall calf stretchHands on the wall, back leg straight, heel down. 2 holds of 30 seconds a sideSquats, lunges
HipsHalf-kneeling hip flexor stretchOne knee down, squeeze that side's glute, shift your hips forward. 2 holds of 30 seconds a sideLunges, split squats
HipsDeep squat holdHold a rack or a doorframe, sit to the bottom with your heels down. 2 holds of 30 secondsSquats
Upper backOpen booksLie on your side, knees bent, arms out in front, and rotate the top arm open toward the floor behind you. 8 slow reps a sideOverhead presses
ShouldersLat stretch on a benchKneel, elbows on a bench, sit your hips back toward your heels. 2 holds of 30 secondsOverhead presses, pull-ups
ShouldersDoorway chest stretchForearms on both sides of a doorframe, step through until you feel it. 2 holds of 30 secondsBench press, push-ups

The five holds are static stretches, which, along with PNF, produced more range than dynamic stretching in the 2024 meta-analysis;2 the knee-to-wall rocks and open books move the joint through its range under control. All of it needs only a wall, a doorway and a bench or sturdy chair.

How often: the 2025 consensus panel recommends static or PNF stretching for long-term range, two or three sets a day of 30 to 120 seconds per muscle, to build up as much weekly time as possible.6 The 2024 meta-analysis, though, found no link between time or sessions a week and range gained.2 A workable middle: the full routine on three or more days a week, and the one area your lifts need most on the other days too.

Shoulder mobility for the overhead position

For a lifter, shoulder mobility mostly means one position: arms straight overhead, upper arms by your ears and ribs down, without leaning back to get there. It's the top of an overhead press and the hang of a pull-up.

  • It isn't only the shoulder joint. In 32 healthy women raising their arms, the upper arm, the shoulder blade and the upper back moved together in a consistent pattern.7 That's why the routine above pairs the lat and chest stretches with open books for the upper back.
  • Pressing and pulling count. In the 2023 meta-analysis, lifting improved range at the shoulder as it did at the hips and knees: 15 of its measures were at the shoulder, and the joint tested made no significant difference.3

Three drills for the overhead position, with a broomstick or a light band:

  1. Pass-throughs. Hold the stick or band with a wide grip and straight arms, lift it up and over your head until it's behind you, then bring it back. 10 slow reps.
  2. Wall slides. Back, head and forearms against a wall, slide your arms up as far as they stay in contact, then back down. 10 slow reps.
  3. Overhead hold. Press the stick straight up and hold it there, arms locked and ribs down, for 20 to 30 seconds: the top of an overhead press without the weight.

Pass-throughs and wall slides move rather than hold, so they fit before a pressing session as well as after it.

When to do it

  • After lifting, or on a day off. Long static holds right before heavy sets aren't the right warm-up; the warm-up guide explains why, and also why a short hold on one tight spot, followed by your warm-up sets, is fine.
  • On a rest day. At this effort, the routine is the "mobility work" an active recovery day can include.
  • In ten spare minutes. It fits in front of a screen in the evening as well as in a gym.

What a mobility routine won't do

  • Build muscle. The 2025 panel doesn't recommend stretching as a primary way to add muscle: the effect is small, and where lifting isn't an option, its suggestion is daily static stretching of more than 15 minutes per muscle for at least six weeks.6 Lifting does that job.
  • Change your bone structure. Some limits are the shape of your bones and joints, and the 2026 paper notes that a difference between your left and right side can come from that rather than from tightness.1 If one hip always stops sooner, don't force it.
  • Replace the warm-up. Range over weeks and readiness for today's heaviest set are different jobs, and they get different tools.

Sharp pain during a stretch is a signal to stop, not to push through: talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.
