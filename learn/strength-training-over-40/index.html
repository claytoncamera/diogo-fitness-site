Strength training over 40 (and 50): what changes, and what doesn't

The studies on lifting later in life mostly cover people in their 60s and 70s, and their strength and muscle still went up. What changes after 40 is less about the lifts and more about the pacing, a matter of practice rather than research: a warm-up you don't skip, smaller jumps, and recovery you plan instead of hope for. Here's what the research says, and a week built on it.

Short answer: Strength and muscle respond to lifting well past 40.1,2 In 47 studies of 1,079 adults aged 50 and over, strength rose on each of the four tests analysed.1 What changes is the pacing, as practice rather than research: a full warm-up, smaller jumps, planned recovery.

Most searches about lifting after 40 come down to two questions: does it still work, and what should change. This page answers both from the research, which mostly studied people two or three decades older than 40, and says which group each finding covers. How coaching handles the swaps and the weekly call is on coaching after 40; coming back after years away, start with getting back into training.

Does lifting still work after 40?

Yes, and the evidence runs well past 40. A meta-analysis pooled 47 studies of 1,079 adults aged 50 and over, with average ages mostly between 60 and 75, and found strength rose on each of the four tests it analysed: about 29% on the leg press, 24% on the chest press, 33% on the knee extension and 25% on the lat pulldown.1

Muscle came too. A companion review of 49 studies and 1,328 adults aged 50 and over found lean body mass, which includes muscle, rose by about 1.1 kg on average, with bigger gains from higher-volume programs.2 And it doesn't stop at 70: a review of 22 randomized trials in people aged 75 and over found resistance training raised both strength and whole-muscle size; strength also rose in the subgroup aged 80 and up.3

None of those reviews draws a line at 40; the people in them were mostly decades older, and they still got stronger. At 50 you're at the young edge of that evidence; at 40 you're just below it, so read it as the closest evidence there is, not a test at your age.

What changes with age, in the research

  • Older groups gained less lean mass. In the lean-mass review, among adults 50 and over, older groups gained less than younger ones, and the authors concluded that starting earlier in life may work better.2 That's a reason to start this year rather than next, not a reason to expect nothing.
  • The masters athletes in the studies were strong. A systematic review of 55 studies of master athletes, in cohorts averaging over 59 years old with 20 or more years of training, found master strength and power athletes (and young controls) were significantly stronger than the other groups studied.4 In 40 recreational masters athletes aged 40 to 81 who trained four or five times a week, mid-thigh muscle area, quadriceps size and quadriceps strength did not decline with age.5 A snapshot, not a trial, so it can't prove cause; it does suggest that, among people who keep training hard, muscle area and quadriceps strength were not lower in the older ones.
  • Power responds too, and lifting fast on purpose helps. In 12 randomized trials of adults aged 50 and over, resistance training improved leg power by a small-to-moderate amount, about 55 watts, and lifting the weight fast on purpose beat lifting it at a normal pace.6 A meta-analysis of 29 trials and 1,313 people, with average ages of 65 to 81 across the trials, found the same: power training was more effective for muscle power than standard progressive training.7
  • Recovery is the least settled. A review of aging and recovery found a perception among older athletes that recovery slows with age, but the studies behind it rarely account for how active the participants were, so aging and inactivity are hard to separate.8 Read your own log instead: if the same session leaves you flat two days later, that's your data.

How heavy to lift after 40

Heavy enough to matter. In the 47-study review, higher-intensity training went with bigger strength gains,1 and the 29-trial meta-analysis found high intensity beat moderate and low for maximal strength.7 The counterweight is a 2016 meta-analysis of 15 studies and 448 people, average age 68, comparing heavy loads (about 80% of a one-rep max) with light-to-moderate ones (about 45%): strength gains tended to be larger with the heavier loads, the gap shrank when total work was matched, muscle growth was marginally larger with heavy loads but small either way, and lighter loads were judged enough for strength when the reps were high enough.9

A dose-response review of 25 trials in healthy adults with a mean age of 65 and over found the largest strength effects at 70% to 79% of a one-rep max, the one load variable that predicted gains; its seven-to-nine-rep and two-to-three-set figures were single-variable tallies, and sets and reps had little or no effect in its meta-regression.10 The authors caution that the trials were of poor methodological quality overall, so treat these as a starting point.10 In plain terms, a weight you could lift about ten or eleven times, done for eight. For most sets after 40, work in the 6-to-12 rep range with one to three reps in reserve; the heaviest sets, fives and under, come after weeks of building up. How to pick the starting weight covers the first session.

Warm-up and exercise choices

The warm-up is where the setup gets more careful. The pattern: a few minutes of easy movement, then two to four warm-up sets of your first lift, climbing from about half your working weight to about 80% of it; how to warm up before lifting has the sets and the weights. Its studies weren't split by age, so the over-40 version is a rule of practice, not a finding: keep the full ramp on the first lift, and when time is short, shorten the easy movement first.

Swapping a movement that doesn't sit right for one that works the same muscles is covered on coaching after 40. One addition from the older-adult research: the trials in the 25-study review were all machine-based,10 so the strength gains above came from machine training.

Recovery, and how fast to add weight

Progression is the part to pace. The ACSM stand notes that progression isn't necessary for a benefit, and that increasing the stimulus is likely a requirement only for continued long-term progress; it adds that in some populations, due to inexperience, loads start low and progression is needed for a meaningful benefit.11 So the climb can be slow, but for someone a few months in it still has to happen. When it does, climb by reps first: pick a range such as 8 to 12, add a rep or two a week, and add weight only when every set hits the top. That's double progression; after 40 the only change is the size of the jump: the smallest step the equipment allows, and an extra week at a new weight when the last rep felt heavy.

Between sets, the general rule on rest between sets applies; the older-adult evidence is too thin to change it.10 Between sessions, leave at least a day before training the same muscles hard again, and take a lighter week when reps stop climbing for two or three weeks in a row. Rest days and the deload week cover both; a workout log makes it visible.

How many days a week

Two or three. A meta-regression of 14 studies in adults over 60 found maximal strength rose slightly with each extra training day, muscle growth didn't change with frequency, and the authors concluded that more than two weekly sessions was unlikely to add anything for older adults.12 The 25-trial review found large strength effects at both two and three sessions a week, with two ahead, but its authors note the two-session trials were short and that eight of its nine muscle-size trials used three sessions, so it can't separate the two.10

So pick the number you can keep. How often to train lays out the options, and the once-a-week question is answered on the coaching page.

A sample week

Three full-body sessions with a day between them, each about 45 minutes, on the older-adult review's load figure: two to three sets of about eight at a weight you could lift ten or eleven times.10 The sets of 8 use that load; take a lighter weight for the sets of 10 and 15 so each still stops one to three reps short of failure. Every session opens with warm-up sets of the first lift.

New to lifting, or back after years? Spend the first one or two weeks at two sets of each with three or four reps left, then move to the sets above: the slower ramp from the dumbbell plan.

Two days? Keep Monday and Wednesday. If a movement bothers you, swap it for the alternative in its row. If something hurts rather than feels hard, see a doctor or a physical therapist before you keep going.

Getting in shape after 40, starting from zero

Most people who type "getting in shape after 40" aren't asking how heavy to go. They haven't trained in years, or ever, and want to know where to start. The target is the same one the World Health Organization sets for every adult from 18 to 64: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week (or half that much vigorous activity) plus muscle-strengthening work for all the major muscle groups on two or more days, and the guidelines add that doing some activity is better than none and that people not meeting them should start with small amounts and gradually increase.13 Nothing in that changes at 40.

How I'd order it, as practice rather than research:

  1. Weeks one and two: walk, and do two short sessions. A daily brisk walk works toward the aerobic half while your joints get used to the idea. For the lifting, take Monday and Wednesday from the sample week above at two sets of each with three or four reps left, the slower ramp from the dumbbell plan, or the bodyweight workout if you'd rather not set foot in a gym yet.
  2. Weeks three to six: add the third day and the full sets. Climb by reps first and keep the warm-up on every session, as above. If a movement doesn't sit right, swap it for the alternative in its row.
  3. After that: the sample week, and a log. The pacing rules on this page (smaller jumps, a planned lighter week) are what keep the restart from becoming another stop.

Coming back after a long break rather than starting cold? Getting back into training covers what you kept and how fast it returns. If something hurts rather than feels hard, or you have a medical question, see a doctor or a physical therapist first; the WHO guidelines say adults with chronic conditions may wish to consult a physical activity specialist or healthcare professional about what's appropriate.13

Strength training over 50, for women and men

The 47-study review above included adults aged 50 and over,1 though most of its participants were older, so people in their 50s are thinly covered. Two common questions are whether the answer differs for women and for men.

Strength training for women over 50

The same plan works. In a 2020 review and meta-analysis of studies in young and middle-aged adults, men and women showed no significant difference in muscle growth or lower-body strength gains, and women gained more upper-body strength relative to where they started.14 That review didn't include older adults. In one small 18-week study of 23 people over 65, both women and men got stronger, though the men's percentage gains in knee-extension strength were larger on average.15 Neither study covers women in their 50s directly, but both found women's strength responding to lifting. Strength training for women covers the plan in detail.

Workouts for men over 50

The same principles, the same week. The sample week above is the starting point: full-body sessions, most sets with one to three reps in reserve, and small jumps in weight. If you've lifted before and stopped, getting back into training covers the first month back.

Whatever your age, if you have a heart condition, joint replacement, high blood pressure or another health condition, or you haven't been active for a long time, talk to your doctor before you start. Past 65, workouts for seniors covers the guideline for older adults and a gentler first week.

Common questions

How do I get in shape after 40 if I haven't exercised in years?

Walk most days and lift twice a week, at two sets of each movement with reps to spare, for the first two weeks; add the third day and the full sets over the next month; then run the sample week with a log. The guideline target is the same as for any adult under 65,13 and the evidence that lifting still works runs well past 40.1 The steps are in the section above.

Can you still build muscle after 40?

The reviews above say yes: lean mass rose in adults aged 50 and over,2 and whole-muscle size rose even in trials of people aged 75 and up.3 The gains were smaller in older groups and modest overall, so the research points to steady, modest gains rather than a dramatic change.

Is it too late to start working out at 30?

No, and the research draws no line at 30. In pooled data from training studies of untrained men and women aged 19 to 78, age and sex didn't change how much muscle size and strength rose.16 The strength review above pooled studies of adults aged 50 and over and found gains on every test it analysed.1 At 30 you're younger than anyone in it, so start where any beginner does: how to start lifting weights.

How many reps should you do after 40?

Most sets sit in the 6-to-12 range, with one to three reps in reserve. The older-adult dose-response review's most effective single-variable tally was seven to nine reps a set, but its meta-regression found little effect of reps,10 and lighter loads also worked when the reps were high enough.9

Do you need more rest days after 40?

The research is thin: the review on recovery and aging rarely separates age from how active people were.8 Two or three sessions a week with a day between them covered the studies above; start there and let your log tell you if a session needs more room.
