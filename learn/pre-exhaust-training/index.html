Pre-exhaust training: an isolation set first, then the compound lift, and what the studies measured

An isolation exercise first, the compound lift straight after, almost no rest between. Here's what the muscle-activity studies and the training trials measured, in whom, where they disagree, and when it's worth trying.

Short answer: Pre-exhaustion means doing a single-joint exercise for a muscle group right before a multi-joint exercise for the same muscle group.2 In the three muscle-activity studies cited here, activity in the muscle the set was aimed at rose in one (the triceps), was unchanged in one (the lats) and fell in one (the quadriceps, with fewer reps).3,5,4 In an 8-week and a 12-week training trial, it did not beat doing the exercises in the usual order.1,2

The idea behind pre-exhaustion is that an isolation exercise done right before a compound exercise targets the muscle, tiring it, to get greater gains in strength and size.1 A 2026 trial describes the method as doing a single-joint exercise for a muscle group before a multi-joint exercise for the same muscle group, with minimal rest time between.2 A leg extension followed by a leg press is the classic pair.

This page covers what the method is, what the muscle-activity studies and the training trials measured, where they disagree, and when it's worth trying. Pairing two exercises in general is on the supersets page; which exercise to do first is on compound exercises.

What pre-exhaust training is

Pre-exhaust is a set order, not a different exercise. In the most recent trial, the lifter did a set of the single-joint exercise and then, within about 10 seconds, a set of the multi-joint exercise for the same muscle, followed by about two minutes of rest.2 Researchers compare it with the traditional order, where all the sets of one exercise are done before moving to the next. It sits next to a same-muscle superset on the supersets page, with one difference: the order is fixed, isolation first.

These are the pairs the studies used:

Gołaś 2017Incline dumbbell fly, front deltoid raise or lying triceps extensionBench press4

What the muscle-activity studies measured

Three studies recorded electromyography (EMG), the electrical activity of a muscle, during the compound lift. EMG shows how active a muscle was in one session. It is not muscle growth.

  • Leg press, 17 healthy men. One set of knee extensions at a 10-rep max before one set of leg presses at a 10-rep max. Activity in the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis during the leg press was significantly lower when pre-exhausted, the gluteus maximus did not change, and the men did significantly fewer leg press reps. The authors concluded their findings do not support the belief that pre-exhaustion enhances muscle activity.3
  • Bench press, eight healthy athletes experienced in resistance training (sex not stated in the abstract). A bench press at 95% of a one-rep max after a pre-exhaust set of incline dumbbell flys, front deltoid raises or lying triceps extensions. Peak triceps activity was significantly greater after triceps pre-exhaustion than before it, but pectoralis major and front deltoid activity showed no significant differences.4
  • Seated row, 20 adults with little or no strength-training experience. A row to failure with or without a previous pullover set. There was no effect on the latissimus dorsi, the target muscle, but the posterior deltoid's median frequency fell with pre-exhaustion, which the authors read as more fatigue there.5

So across these three studies, activity in the muscle the set was aimed at rose in one (the triceps, after a triceps exercise), didn't change in one (the lats), and dropped in one (the quadriceps in the leg press). In the bench press study the chest and front deltoid, also pre-exhausted in their own sessions, didn't change.

What the training trials found

Activity in one session is one thing; what happens over weeks of training is another. Three trials compared pre-exhaustion with the usual order:

  • 12 weeks, 39 trained participants (9 men and 30 women). Fourteen did pre-exhaustion, 17 did the same order with a rest between the two exercises, and 8 did compound exercises first. There were no significant differences between groups in strength on the chest press, leg press and pulldown, or in body composition. The authors concluded pre-exhaustion offers no greater benefit than the same exercises with rest between them.1
  • Eight weeks, 41 resistance-trained adults finished (32 men, 9 women). Lower-body training twice a week, four sets of each exercise at an 8- to 12-rep max with about two minutes' rest, pre-exhaust against the traditional order. Muscle size and body composition improved slightly more with the traditional order, with statistical uncertainty about a true difference; squat one-rep max, local muscular endurance and jump height were similar.2 The pre-exhaust sessions were reported as taking 35.9% less time (26:22 against 41:09), and the estimated training volume was 29.8% greater with the traditional order, with a wide interval (-4.9% to +73.0%).2
  • Nine weeks, 31 men described as untrained. The men had at least a year of earlier lifting experience, but none in the previous six months. Twice a week, twelve did one set of leg extensions to failure at 20% of a one-rep max before three leg press sets at 75%; twelve did the three leg press sets alone; seven did not train. Leg press strength rose 16% with pre-exhaustion and 15% without, and quadriceps thickness rose in both. The pre-exhaust group did this with a lower training load.6

Where they disagree

The two lower-body trials point in slightly different directions. The 2026 trial found a small edge in muscle size for the traditional order, while the 2019 trial in men described as untrained found similar leg press strength and quadriceps growth with less total load for pre-exhaustion. The groups differed (trained against described-as-untrained, 8 against 9 weeks), and the 2019 groups did not do equal work, so I won't call a winner. What they share is that the pre-exhaust group didn't clearly do better.

What these studies didn't test

The 2026 trial covered the lower body only, in adults aged 18 to 40.2 The muscle-activity studies measured one session each. The 2019 trial was nine weeks in men described as untrained, with a lower training load in the pre-exhaust group.6 None of the studies cited here followed lifters for years, so they can't say what the method does over a long career.

When to try it, and when to skip it (practical)

As a practical guide, not a finding:

  • Skip it when the compound lift is the goal. A pre-exhaust set left lifters with fewer leg press reps in one study,3 and the ACSM's 2009 stand puts multi-joint exercises before single-joint ones in strength programs.7 The ACSM's 2026 stand found strength was enhanced for exercises done at the start of a session.8 If you want a heavier squat or press, do it first and fresh.
  • Try it when time is short. The pre-exhaust sessions were about a third shorter in the 2026 trial, with a similar squat one-rep max and local muscular endurance, and slightly smaller gains in muscle size.2 That is the case for it: the same exercises in less time.
  • Try it if you like the format. Don't count on it to make a muscle more active in the compound lift: in the activity studies above it mostly didn't. The training trials found little or no difference in strength between the orders. Expect to use a lighter weight on the compound lift, because the men in one study did fewer reps.3
  • Pick the pair by muscle: leg extension then leg press for the quads, chest fly then bench press for the chest, a pullover then a seated row for the back. These are the pairs the studies used, not a recommended list.
  • How to run it: one set of the single-joint exercise, straight into one set of the compound, then rest about two minutes and repeat for three or four rounds. Stop both sets a few reps short of failure; reps in reserve explains how to judge that. The studies took their sets to failure or a 10-rep max, so this version is gentler than what they tested.

Common questions

What is pre-exhaust training?

Doing a single-joint exercise for a muscle group before a multi-joint exercise for the same muscle group, with minimal rest between them.2

Does pre-exhaustion build more muscle?

Not in the trials cited here. In an eight-week trial the traditional order gave slightly larger improvements in muscle size, with uncertainty,2 and a 12-week trial found no significant difference in body composition or strength.1 A nine-week trial in men described as untrained found similar leg press strength and quadriceps growth with a lower training load.6

Does pre-exhaust work for chest, legs or back?

The studies tested the chest (bench press), legs (leg press, squat) and back (seated row). Chest and front deltoid activity didn't change in the bench press study, though triceps activity rose after a triceps set; lat activity didn't change in the row study; quadriceps activity fell in the leg press study.4,5,3 Those are single sessions of EMG, not growth.

Is pre-exhaust the same as a superset?

It is a kind of one. A superset pairs two exercises back to back; a pre-exhaust pair is the same-muscle version with the single-joint exercise first. Supersets covers the other kinds.

Is pre-exhaustion good for beginners?

The training trials cited here were in trained participants and in men described as untrained who had earlier lifting experience;1,6 none was in true beginners, and none found a clear gain from the method over the usual order. For a beginner, learning the compound lifts comes first (how to start lifting weights).
