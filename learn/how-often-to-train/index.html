How often should you train? Less than you think.

The best training schedule is the one you'll still be doing in six months. Here's how to find yours, from once a week to five.

By Diogo·Updated October 11, 2026·3 min read

In this article

  1. The only schedule that works is the one you keep
  2. What each schedule looks like
  3. What the research says, in plain English
  4. How to choose your number
  5. Make the sessions count
  6. If you want it built around your week

Fitness social media makes it look like you need to train six days a week, two-hour sessions, split into body parts. If you have a job, classes, a family or a social life, that plan is a trap. You'll do it for two weeks and then do nothing.

Here's a more honest answer: once a week at the gym is better than none, and most people make real progress on far less than they expect.

The only schedule that works is the one you keep

Training frequency is a tool, not a badge. The right number depends on your goal, how much time you really have and how well you recover. If a plan is so ambitious that you miss sessions, it's a worse plan than a smaller one you finish every week.

What each schedule looks like

Days / weekHow it's usually set upGood for
1One full-body session built around the big movementsVery busy weeks. It's the floor, and it still works.
2Two full-body sessions, or a simple upper/lower splitMost people who are balancing a lot.
3Three full-body sessions, or a simple rotationA common starting point. Enough frequency to keep progressing.
4Upper/lower, twice eachMore weekly volume if your schedule and recovery allow it.
5A split focused on specific goalsSpecial cases like performance. It's rarely required for good progress.

What the research says, in plain English

Studies generally find that training a muscle group about twice a week tends to beat once a week when everything else is equal. But what matters most is total weekly work: the number of hard sets you do for each muscle. Once that's matched, how you slice it across the days matters less than people think.

The takeaway: don't chase an ideal split. Choose the schedule you can repeat, then make the sessions you do count.

How to choose your number

  1. Look at your real week. Which days are genuinely free? Be honest, and don't count days you hope to be free.
  2. Start lower than you think. It's far easier to add a day than to recover from burning out.
  3. Pick days with space between them. Muscles grow during recovery, not while you're lifting.
  4. Match it to the goal. General fitness and fat loss are happy on two or three days. More specialized goals may need more.
  5. Re-check every month. If you hit every session easily, add one. If you keep missing, drop one.

Make the sessions count

  • Focus on the big movements: squats or leg press, a hinge like deadlifts or RDLs, presses, rows and pull-downs.
  • Progress over time. Add a little weight or a rep when you can. A written log makes this easy.
  • Keep sessions efficient. Effective beats long. Most good sessions run 45 to 60 minutes.
  • Leave a few reps in the tank on most sets. (Here's how.)

Don't forget the other 23 hours

What you do outside the gym matters as much as what you do in it: daily steps, sleep and food. Walking is one of the easiest additions you can make.

If you want it built around your week

The right plan fits your schedule, your goal and your experience. That's the whole idea behind coaching: you shouldn't have to rearrange your life to fit a program.
