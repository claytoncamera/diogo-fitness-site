Can you lose fat and build muscle at the same time?

Yes, for more people than you'd think. It's called body recomposition, and it depends more on protein, lifting and patience than on finding the perfect diet.

By Diogo·Updated October 18, 2026·3 min read

In this article

  2. Who it works best for
  3. The four things it takes
  4. How to know it's working
  5. When to pick one goal instead

The old rule was simple: you bulk, then you cut. You can't build muscle in a calorie deficit and you can't lose fat in a surplus, so pick one. Like a lot of old rules, it's partly true and mostly unhelpful.

Losing fat and building muscle at the same time has a name, body recomposition, and the research says it happens more often than the old rule allows. Here's who it works for and what it takes.

What body recomposition means

Recomposition is when your body fat goes down while your muscle goes up over the same stretch of time. The scale might not move much, which is why people who are doing it right often think it isn't working. Their jeans fit differently, their lifts are up, their photos look different, and the number they're staring at is the same.

That's the first thing to understand: if you're chasing recomposition, the scale is the worst tool you have.

Who it works best for

Recomposition is easiest when your body has the most room to change:

  • Beginners. New lifters can build muscle even in a deficit.1
  • People coming back. If you used to train and stopped, in my experience lost muscle comes back faster than it took to build.
  • People with more fat to lose. They tend to find it easier, in my experience, and it's the group where recomposition is best documented.1

What about trained lifters? A 2020 review in the Strength and Conditioning Journal went through the studies and found that recomposition shows up in resistance-trained people too, not just beginners, when progressive training and a solid nutrition plan are in place.1 It's slower and the margin for error is smaller, but it isn't a myth.

The four things it takes

1. Lift, and keep making it harder. Muscle needs a reason to grow. That means two to four strength sessions a week with weights that go up over time. (Here's how progression works.) Walking and cardio are great for fat loss and health, but on their own they build little muscle.

2. Eat enough protein. This is the lever most people under-pull. A meta-analysis of 49 studies found that protein supports muscle gain during training up to around 1.6 grams per kilogram of bodyweight a day, with no clear benefit beyond that.2 In a deficit, more may help: in a four-week trial, men eating 2.4 g/kg while cutting hard and training gained more lean mass and lost more fat than men eating 1.2 g/kg.3 My rule of thumb is 0.7 to 1 gram per pound of bodyweight, which lands in that range. (More on setting the number.)

3. A small deficit, not a crash. Big deficits lose fat faster, but they also make it hard to hold on to muscle. A common evidence-based recommendation for keeping muscle while dieting is to lose about 0.5 to 1 percent of bodyweight a week.4 For recomposition I'd aim at the low end of that, or even maintenance calories, and let the training and protein do the work.

4. Sleep and time. Recovery is when muscle actually gets built, and short sleep seems to work against both goals. Give it twelve weeks before you judge it. Recomposition is a slow signal.

How to know it's working

Since the scale lies here, use the other tools:

  • Strength. If your main lifts are going up week to week, you're probably building, or at least keeping, muscle.
  • Waist measurement. Same spot, same time of day, once a week. If it's dropping, fat is leaving.
  • Photos. Same lighting, same poses, every two to four weeks. You won't see it day to day. You will see it in a month.
  • Weekly weight average. Flat or slightly down is a good sign when the lifts are climbing. (Here's why single weigh-ins mislead.)

When to pick one goal instead

Recomposition is slower than a focused phase. If you have a lot of fat to lose, a proper fat-loss phase with protein kept high will get you there faster, and you can still hold, or even gain, some muscle along the way. If you're already lean and want to get noticeably bigger, a small surplus and a program built to grow is faster than trying to do it at maintenance.

Where recomposition shines is the middle: you're not very lean, not very heavy, and you want to look and feel different in three months without picking a side. That describes a lot of the people I coach.
