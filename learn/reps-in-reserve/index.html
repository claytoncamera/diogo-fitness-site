There's always 3 reps in reserve

Next time you think a set is over, ask whether that was really your last rep. Here's how to use reps in reserve to train harder, safely.

Short answer: Reps in reserve (RIR) is how many more good reps you could have done before failure. For most working sets, aim for about 1 to 3 reps in reserve.4,5

You're mid-set, the weight feels heavy, and a voice in your head says that's enough. So you rack it.

Here's a question worth asking: was that really your last rep? More often than not, there were a few more in there.1,2 Learning to tell the difference is one of the most useful skills in training, and it has a name: reps in reserve, or RIR.

What reps in reserve means

RIR is how many more good reps you could have done before you hit failure, meaning the point where you can't complete another rep with decent form.

  • 0 RIR: that was your last possible rep.
  • 1 RIR: you could have done one more.
  • 3 RIR: you could have done three more. It felt hard, but not brutal.

Why it matters

Muscles respond to sets that are challenging, meaning hard enough that the last few reps slow down and take real effort. Sets that stop long before that are less effective, no matter how many you do.

The catch is that people are usually bad at judging it, especially beginners. Research on this keeps finding that lifters tend to underestimate how many reps they have left, particularly when a set is still far from failure.2,3 So the rep that feels like your last often isn't.

How to use it

  1. Most working sets: aim for about 1 to 3 reps in reserve.4,5 That's hard enough to count and still leaves your form and your joints in good shape.
  2. On safer movements, push a little closer. Machines and isolation work (like curls or lateral raises) are good places to go to 0 or 1 RIR.6
  3. On heavy compound lifts, stay more conservative. Squats, deadlifts and presses are higher stakes. Keeping 2 or 3 reps in reserve there is smart.6
  4. Take a breath before you quit. When you feel done, pause, breathe and ask whether you could get one or two more clean reps. Often you can.
  5. Log it. Write the weight, the reps and how many you had left. Over a few weeks, your judgment gets much sharper.

RIR and RPE: two ways to say the same thing

RPE stands for rating of perceived exertion. For lifting, there's a 1-to-10 version built on reps in reserve: RPE 10 means you had nothing left, RPE 9 means one rep left, RPE 8 means two, and so on.7 So RPE is 10 minus your reps in reserve, and the 1 to 3 RIR above is RPE 7 to 9.

Training to failure: should you?

You don't have to. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found that taking sets to failure didn't add to gains in strength, muscle size or power.4

For muscle growth, a 2022 review of 15 studies in young adults, 7 of them measuring muscle size, found no significant difference overall; its edge for failure in trained lifters came from two studies.9 A 2023 review of 15 studies found a trivial edge at most.6 A 2021 review of 13 studies in adults found more growth with failure, but not once total work was matched.10 A 2024 analysis did find growth rising as sets ended closer to failure, though its authors call it exploratory.11

For strength, failure made no difference overall, and stopping short came out ahead when total work wasn't matched.9,10 For recovery, it costs more: in a 2017 study of ten trained men, sets to failure slowed recovery for up to a day or two.12

Every set to failure?

No. It doesn't add strength, the edge for size is small and uncertain, and it costs the most recovery. Progress comes from adding a rep or a little weight over the weeks: that's progressive overload.

Close to failure vs failure

Close is usually enough if it's truly close.4 In a 2024 eight-week trial, 18 trained men and women grew their quads about as much stopping leg sets one or two reps short as going to failure.5 So the advice above holds: 0 or 1 in reserve on machines and isolation work, 2 or 3 on heavy compound lifts. The exception is very light weights. In a 2022 trial of 25 untrained men, knee-extension sets at 30 percent of their max built no significant muscle unless they went to failure; heavier sets grew either way.13

Common mistakes

  • Stopping way too early. If a set never gets hard, it isn't doing much, deload weeks aside.
  • Grinding every set to failure. It's exhausting, hard to recover from and not needed for most sets.12,6,4
  • Letting form fall apart. A rep with a heaved, jerky motion doesn't count. Failure means failing with good technique. Pull-ups included.
  • Comparing yourself to other people. Your reserve is your own. Judge it by how the set feels to you, not by the number on the bar.

A simple way to practice

On your next set of 10, stop at 8 and write down how many more you think you had. Then try a set where you go until the bar clearly slows down. Compare the two. That gap is what you're learning to read.

The bigger idea

Training isn't about suffering. It's about giving your body a clear reason to adapt and then recovering well. RIR helps you do exactly that, hard enough to matter and controlled enough to keep going next week. If you're brand new, how to start lifting weights turns this into a first month.
