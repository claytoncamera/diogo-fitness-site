How many reps per set? A wide range builds muscle

Eight to twelve for size, one to five for strength, fifteen and up for endurance: the classic rule is only partly right. Here's what the research says about rep ranges, and how to choose one for each lift.

Short answer: For muscle, a wide range of reps works when the sets are hard: about 6 to 15 is the efficient middle, and light sets of 15 to 40 work too when taken to failure.4 For strength, heavier sets of about 1 to 6 reps do more.2,5 There is no single hypertrophy range.

The classic rule goes like this: one to five reps for strength, eight to twelve for muscle, fifteen and up for endurance. It's called the repetition continuum, and it's widely accepted. A 2021 review that re-examined it found the research supports it for strength, but not for muscle size, and only weakly for endurance.1

This page covers how many reps per set to use for muscle, for strength and for endurance, why the rep number only means something next to effort, and how to choose a range for each exercise. How many of those sets to do each week is how many sets per week.

How many reps to build muscle

The exact number matters less than you'd think. A 2017 meta-analysis of 21 studies compared light loads (60% of your one-rep max or less) with heavy loads, every set taken to failure. Muscle growth was similar between the two; one-rep-max strength favored the heavy loads.2 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found the same across its overview of the research: loads anywhere from 30% to 100% of a one-rep max didn't change how much muscle people built.3

The 2021 review is blunt about what that means: "there is no ideal 'hypertrophy zone'". It found similar whole-muscle growth across loads from about 30% of a one-rep max upward, in younger and older people and in beginners and trained lifters alike. There does seem to be a floor: in one study it describes, sets at 20% of a one-rep max built about half the muscle of heavier ones, so the authors place the lower limit somewhere around 30%.1

So why is 6 to 12 still the usual advice?

Because it's efficient, not because it's magic. The same review makes the case: light loads mean many more reps per set, which takes more time and brings more discomfort, and heavy loads need more sets to build the same muscle, with high volume adding stress on the joints.1 The middle avoids both. A 2021 review of time-efficient training recommends loads you can lift 6 to 15 times, and says lighter loads for 15 to 40 reps work too when the sets are taken to failure, which is useful at home with light dumbbells.4

How many reps for strength

Here the old rule holds up. Heavy loads built more one-rep-max strength in the 2017 meta-analysis,2 and the ACSM's 2026 stand found strength was enhanced by heavier loads, at 80% of a one-rep max or more, in a dose-response.3 For intermediate and advanced lifters, the ACSM's 2009 stand recommends eventually emphasizing heavy sets of 1 to 6 reps for strength, with three to five minutes of rest between them.5 Five sets of five, the 5x5 workout, is one classic way to train in that range.

Two caveats. First, part of the heavy-load advantage is practice: strength is usually tested on the lift people trained, and the 2021 review notes that on an isometric test, pushing against a fixed sensor, load made little difference to strength gains.1 Second, lighter training still builds plenty of strength: the 2021 review's summary of a 2017 meta-analysis puts the rise in one-rep maxes at about 35% with heavy loads and 28% with light ones (using that meta-analysis's own cut-offs for heavy and light).4 If you don't need to lift the heaviest single possible, moderate reps get you most of the way.

How many reps for muscular endurance

The ACSM's 2009 stand recommends light to moderate loads, 40% to 60% of a one-rep max, for more than 15 reps per set, with rests under 90 seconds.5 The evidence behind a special endurance range is thinner than the advice: the 2021 review calls a load-specific effect on muscular endurance "equivocal", with what support there is mostly for the lower body.1 Higher reps are a reasonable way to train endurance; they aren't the only way.

The rep number means nothing without effort

Ten reps with a weight you could have lifted twenty times is not a set of ten in any way that matters. The muscle-growth findings above come from sets that ended at or close to failure. The ACSM's 2026 stand says enough effort can come from sets that stop near failure, with a target of two to three reps in reserve, and that going all the way to failure isn't necessary for gains.3 Reps in reserve explains how to judge that from inside a set.

So choose the range first, then a weight that makes the last few reps in that range hard. If you can blow past the top of the range, the weight is too light; if you can't reach the bottom, it's too heavy.

How to choose reps for each exercise

  • New to lifting? Eight to twelve reps on everything. The ACSM's 2009 stand recommends that range for novices,5 and it's what the first four weeks uses.
  • Big compound lifts (squats, deadlifts, presses, rows) suit the lower end, about 5 to 10. (Compound exercises, explained.)
  • Machines, cables and single-joint lifts (curls, raises, leg extensions) suit the higher end, about 10 to 20.
  • Training at home with light weights? Use the higher reps and take sets closer to failure; that's how light loads keep working.
  • Give each exercise a range and keep it for at least a few weeks, so you can tell whether you're progressing. Adding reps inside the range, then weight, is double progression.

Common mistakes

  • Treating 8 to 12 as the only range that builds muscle. It's a sensible default, not a rule.
  • Stopping at the number. A set of twelve that could have been twenty did little. The number is a target for effort, not a finish line.
  • Grinding heavy singles too early. Very heavy sets are a skill. In your first months, the novice range of 8 to 12 is the place to start.
  • Changing the rep range every week. A range only tells you something if you stay in it long enough to beat it.
  • Training through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. If you have an injury, a medical condition or a medical question, talk to a doctor before you change how you train.

Common questions

How many reps on the bench press, leg press, deadlift or curls?

Sort the lift first. The bench press and the deadlift are big compound lifts, so about 5 to 10; curls are single-joint, so about 10 to 20 (the arm workout). The leg press is a compound lift on a machine, so it sits between the two; its page gives a range for the first lift and for after a squat.
