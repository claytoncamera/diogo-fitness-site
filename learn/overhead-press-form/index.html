Overhead press form: brace, press, then get your head through

The overhead press is the vertical push: a weight from your shoulders to straight arms overhead. Here's the standing barbell press step by step, the bar path, the dumbbell, seated and machine versions, the two faults that show up first, and where it goes in your week.

Short answer: Grip the bar just outside your shoulders, forearms upright, bar on the front of your shoulders. Brace your midsection and squeeze your glutes. Press straight up, moving your head back just enough for the bar to pass your face, then bring your head through so the bar finishes over it. Lower to your shoulders. Three sets of 6 to 12.

The overhead press is pressing a weight from your shoulders to straight arms above your head, standing or seated, with a barbell, dumbbells or a machine. It's the vertical push, the lift a week of bench pressing doesn't cover, and the base of the shoulder workout. Holding the bar overhead while you squat is a different lift: the overhead squat.

This page is the form: the standing barbell press step by step, the bar path, how wide to grip, the dumbbell, seated and machine versions, the common faults, and how many sets. The shoulder day itself, with the raises and reverse flies around the press, lives on the shoulder workout page.

What the overhead press trains

Mostly the front and side of the shoulder, with the triceps straightening your elbows and the upper chest and upper trapezius helping. In 13 men experienced in strength training, the front head of the shoulder was most active in the shoulder press of the four exercises tested, and the side head was roughly six to nine times as active in the shoulder press and the lateral raise as in the bench press or the fly.1 That's why the press anchors a shoulder day: it reaches two of the three heads.

How to do a standing barbell overhead press

  1. Set the bar in a rack at about the height of your collarbone.
  2. Grip it just outside your shoulders, the bar low in your palms over your forearms, wrists straight rather than bent back.
  3. Unrack it onto the front of your shoulders. Step back, feet about hip-width apart. Your forearms are roughly upright and your elbows a little in front of the bar.
  4. Brace. Take a breath, tighten your midsection, and squeeze your glutes. Ribs down, not flared up.
  5. Press straight up. Move your head back just enough for the bar to pass your chin and nose.
  6. Get your head through. Once the bar passes your forehead, bring your head forward under it. Finish with your arms straight and the bar over the middle of your head, roughly in line with your shoulders and hips.
  7. Lower it under control to the front of your shoulders, moving your head back out of the way again. That's one rep.

The bar path

Because your face is in the way, the bar can't travel in a perfectly straight line. It moves slightly back around your face and finishes over your head. In a study of eight competitive bodybuilders pressing seated against a backrest, the front press ran from the upper chest to straight arms above and behind the head, with the forearms kept upright, and the authors describe the result as a curved, C-shaped path rather than a straight line.2 If the bar finishes in front of your head, it's pulling you forward; if your torso tips back to get under it, it's too heavy.

That study and a second one also compared the press with the bar lowered behind the neck. The front press allowed heavier loads: in 14 resistance-trained men, the front press used greater loads than the behind-the-neck version,3 and in the bodybuilders, the front press max was about 82 kilograms against about 76 behind the neck.2 Pressing behind the neck shifted activity toward the rear of the shoulder in both studies, and toward the side in one; pressing in front worked the chest more in both.2,3 This page teaches the front press, the standard version.

How wide to grip

Just outside shoulder width, with your forearms vertical at the bottom, is the starting point. In 11 resistance-trained men pressing a barbell seated to failure with three grips, narrower grips allowed more weight and moved the shoulders and elbows through more range, while wider grips pushed the bar out sideways harder and put less demand on the elbows, and narrower grips less on the shoulders.4 Grip width changes how the work is shared rather than making one width right. Pick the width where your forearms stay upright, and keep it the same each session.

Barbell, dumbbell, seated and machine versions

In 15 healthy men tested at 80% of their max in four versions, the standing dumbbell press produced the most shoulder activity and the lowest max: about 7% less than the standing barbell press and about 10% less than the seated dumbbell press.5 More balance asked more of the shoulders and allowed less weight. In short:

  • Standing barbell. The steps above. The heaviest standing version, and the one that asks most of your brace. Dip your knees and drive with your legs to start the bar, and it's the push press.
  • Dumbbell, standing or seated. Dumbbells start just outside your shoulders, palms forward or turned slightly in, and finish over your shoulders. Each arm works on its own, so a weaker side can't hide (the dumbbell shoulder press has both versions step by step). Sitting on a bench with an upright back lets you press more. Start with your palms facing you and turn them forward as you press, and it's the Arnold press.
  • Seated barbell. A bench with the back set nearly upright, as in the studies above, takes the legs and most of the balance out.
  • Landmine. One end of a barbell anchored on the floor, pressed up and forward from your shoulder, one arm or two: the landmine press, an angled press.
  • Machine or Smith machine. The path is fixed. In the bodybuilder study, the front machine press allowed a heavier max than the front barbell press, about 95 against 82 kilograms, while the barbell produced more muscle activity, which the authors put down to steadying the load.2 Over eight weeks, 38 resistance-trained men made similar strength gains, and similar growth in the muscles measured, on a free-weight program or a machine program that included the shoulder press.6

So pick the version your gym and your body make easy to repeat, and keep it long enough to add weight. With dumbbells at home, a seated press on a chair works; with nothing at all, the shoulder workout's home options include pike push-ups.

Common faults, and a fix for each

  • Leaning back. The lower back arches and the press turns into a steep incline press. Squeeze your glutes, keep your ribs down, and lighten the bar until you can press without the lean. Brace the way you would for a plank.
  • Elbows flaring out to the sides. The elbows drift out and behind the bar at the bottom and the bar drifts forward. Keep your forearms upright and your elbows slightly in front of the bar as you start the press.
  • Pressing around your face. Not moving your head pushes the bar forward. Head back, then through.
  • Wrists bent back. The bar sits in your fingers. Move it down into the heel of your palm.
  • Half reps. Arms straight at the top, bar back to your shoulders at the bottom. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found strength was enhanced by training through a complete range of motion.7
  • Pressing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and how it fits the shoulder day

  • Three sets of about 6 to 12, as on the shoulder workout, with a rep or two in reserve. (How to judge reps left.) The ACSM's 2026 stand advises at least two sets per exercise.7
  • Press first. The same stand found strength was enhanced for exercises done at the beginning of a session.7 Lateral raises and reverse flies come after.
  • Count it with your bench. If the press comes straight after a chest day full of bench pressing, two sets is usually plenty.
  • Add weight in the smallest steps you have. The press moves less weight than the bench, so a jump that's small on a bench is a big one here. (How to pick the weight.)
