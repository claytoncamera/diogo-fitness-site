Are YouTube workouts effective? Free videos vs. a coach

Free videos can get you moving: a trial of weekly workout videos found exactly that. What they can't do is write the plan around your week, progress it, or notice when you stop. Here's where each option fits.

In a 12-week randomized trial, young adults sent weekly workout videos did more moderate-to-vigorous activity and more muscle-strengthening work than a group sent health-education videos. What a free video doesn't do is write the plan around your week, progress it when you get stronger, or notice when you stop. If you follow a video and keep following it, you don't need a coach. If you've saved twenty videos and done four, the videos aren't the problem.

I'm an online coach, so I have a stake in this. I'll try to be fair, including about when free videos are all you need, and about how little the research can settle.

What free videos can do

More than people give them credit for. In a 2022 randomized trial, 64 young adults (48 of them women, average age about 23) were split in two for 12 weeks. One group was sent a weekly aerobic and muscle-strengthening workout video, delivered through YouTube; the other was sent a weekly general health-education video. Measured with accelerometers, the workout-video group did more moderate-to-vigorous activity and slept more efficiently; by questionnaire, they reported more frequent muscle-strengthening work, more intrinsic motivation and fewer perceived barriers. Sedentary time, light activity and sleep duration didn't differ.1 It was a small trial, run during pandemic restrictions, in people in their early twenties, and the videos arrived on a schedule from researchers. It still says the obvious thing plainly: a free video you actually do is exercise.

The basics are free too. The World Health Organization recommends that adults do muscle-strengthening work for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week, plus aerobic activity.2 You don't need to pay anyone to learn that, and a follow-along video can cover it.

Where free videos and free programs stop

The plan isn't yours. A video is one session for everyone watching. It doesn't know your equipment, your schedule or what you did on Tuesday, and it doesn't get harder because you did. Progressing is on you: a heavier weight, a harder video, more sets. (What progression looks like.) A free written program is better here, because a good one has a rule for progressing built in. (The free plans on this site all do.)

Quality varies, and views don't tell you which is which. I couldn't find a study that grades general workout videos on YouTube for accuracy; the ones I found grade videos on medical topics or on one narrow exercise category, which is a different question. The nearest thing is a 2021 study of popular Brazilian Instagram accounts (over 100,000 followers, at least half their posts about exercise or health). The researchers rated the posts' quality as low, about 39% on their scale, and only about 2.7% of the posts cited a reference. The influencers with more followers tended to have less formal education, and a creator's qualifications didn't predict the quality of their posts.3 That's one platform in one country, so don't stretch it. The practical reading is: a big audience isn't evidence the advice is good. Check the plan against the basics above, whoever made it.

Nobody sees you, and nobody notices when you stop. In a 12-week study in which 20 moderately trained men started the same program, the group supervised by a personal trainer increased their loads faster and gained more maximal strength than the group training alone.4 And on sticking with it: in a 2025 randomized trial, 112 trained adults started the same 10-week, three-days-a-week strength program with a coach beside them, guided by an app, or from a PDF on their own. Of those who started, 88% of the group with a coach beside them, 81% of the app group and 52% of the PDF group completed at least 26 of the 30 sessions. Every group that finished got stronger, and the coached group gained more on the squat than the other two.5 The PDF group is the closest thing in that trial to a free program you follow on your own, and about half of them didn't make it to the end of ten weeks.

That gap, between meaning to and doing, is the whole story. In a meta-analysis of 10 studies and 3,899 people, nearly half of the people who intended to meet physical-activity guidelines didn't follow through.6 A free video can't close it, because it doesn't know you stopped.

What an online coach adds (how I work)

With me, your workouts live in the Trainerize app. You weigh in twice a week and send progress photos every two weeks. We talk on a weekly call scheduled around your time zone, and you can message me with questions between calls. On the call we look at the week you actually had and change the plan for the one that's coming.

So what you'd be paying for isn't the workout. The internet is full of workouts, and a lot of them are good. You'd be paying for a plan written for you, progression decided from your numbers, and someone noticing when you go quiet. There's a reason that last part might matter: a meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies (19,951 people) found that getting people to monitor their progress toward a goal helped them reach it, and the effect was larger when progress was reported or made public, and when it was physically recorded.7 Those studies aren't about coaching. A weekly check-in is a version of both.

Where it stops: I'm not in the room, so I can't correct you mid-set, and I don't lead the session the way a follow-along video does. It costs you more time each week than pressing play: the weigh-ins, the photos and the call. It costs money, and I don't publish a price list. And if you already train consistently on your own and know how to adjust your plan, a coach may not be worth it for you yet. (Here's how I'd decide.)

What the research can't tell you

I couldn't find a study that compares people following free workout videos with people working with an online coach, over months, and measures what happened. The studies above each test one piece: scheduled videos against no videos, supervision against none, a coach beside you against an app or a PDF, how good the free advice is on one platform. None of them can tell you which one will work for you.

So here's my read, and it's an opinion, not a finding: free videos are the best starting point there has ever been. If you press play three times a week and keep doing it, you're doing the hard part, and you may never need a coach. The question is what happens in week five, when the video gets easy or the week gets busy. A video can't get harder for you, and it can't ask what happened. A coach does both.

How to choose: four questions

  1. Do you keep pressing play? If you've followed videos for two months and you're still going, keep going. Add a log so you can see yourself progress.
  2. Does your training need to progress on purpose? If your goal is strength or muscle, you need a plan with a rule for getting harder. A free written program can do that; a random video each day can't. (How to start lifting, if you haven't.)
  3. Do you keep starting over? If you know what to do and still stop, a better video isn't the missing piece. Someone reading your numbers is. That's the case for a coach. (Why people keep starting over.)
  4. Is anything injured or medical? Doctor or physical therapist first, whichever you pick. A video can't see you move. I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor.

It isn't either/or. You can start with free videos, move to a free written program when you want to progress on purpose, and look for a coach if the plan keeps dying in the first bad week. Common questions

Are YouTube workouts good for beginners?

They can be a good way in: someone leads the session, you can pause, and it costs nothing to try. Two things to check. Pick videos that show the movements slowly and cue the positions, and start with the easier version of anything that involves weight. Nobody is watching your form, so if something hurts, stop. (A bodyweight workout to start with, written down.)

Are YouTube workouts effective for building strength?

Getting stronger takes a plan that gets harder as you do, with heavier loads over weeks. A follow-along video is the same session every time you play it, so it can't do that for you. Use a written program with a progression rule, or a coach, for strength. Videos still work well for conditioning and for learning the moves.

Can I get fit with free videos alone?

Yes, if you keep doing them. In the one trial I found, young adults sent weekly videos moved more and did more strength work than the comparison group over 12 weeks. The limit isn't the videos. It's whether you're still pressing play in month three.

YouTube workouts or the gym?

They aren't opposites. Many follow-along videos use little or no equipment, so they suit home; a gym gives you weights to progress with. If you have a membership you don't use, that's its own question.

Why pay a coach when videos are free?

Don't, if videos are working. If they're not, what you'd be paying for is a plan written for your week, progression decided from your numbers, and someone who notices when you stop. Here's how I'd decide whether that's worth it at all.
