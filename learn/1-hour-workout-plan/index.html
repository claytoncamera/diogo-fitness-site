A 1-hour workout plan: what the full hour buys

Thirty minutes is enough for a lot of weeks. An hour is a different session, not the same one done slower. Here's what the extra half hour buys, according to the research, a sample hour laid out minute by minute, and how to spend it so the hour goes to sets instead of to the phone.

Short answer: An hour gives you what a thirty-minute session has to cut: a proper warm-up, full rest between sets, and more sets per muscle. In 21 trained men, three-minute rests built more strength and thigh muscle than one-minute rests. Spend the extra minutes there, not on more exercises.2

A thirty-minute session works by cutting: a short warm-up, paired exercises, rests kept tight. The 30-minute workout is that page, and for a lot of weeks it's the right one. This page is the other case: you have a full hour, and you want to know what to do with it.

The short version is that an hour isn't a thirty-minute session with more exercises bolted on. It buys three specific things the short session gives up, and the sample hour below spends the minutes on those. A review of time-efficient training notes that, with a warm-up and stretching, traditional strength programs often exceed an hour,1 so an hour is a normal length for a full session, not a long one.

What a full hour buys over thirty minutes

1. Full rest between sets

This is the biggest one. In an eight-week study, 21 young resistance-trained men did the same three full-body workouts a week with either one-minute or three-minute rests; the three-minute group gained more squat and bench press strength and more thigh muscle thickness.2 A 2024 meta-analysis of nine studies found a small benefit for muscle growth from resting more than sixty seconds, and no appreciable difference once rests went past ninety seconds.3 A 2021 review put it this way: untrained people can do well on one to two minutes, trained people probably need two minutes or more, and the longer rests belong on the heavy multi-joint lifts.1 In thirty minutes, rest is the first thing that gets squeezed. In an hour, it doesn't have to be. (How long to rest, by lift.)

2. More sets

A 2017 meta-analysis of 15 studies found a graded dose-response: more weekly sets, more muscle growth.4 A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 participants found the same direction for muscle and strength, with diminishing returns, more pronounced for strength.5 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found muscle growth was enhanced by ten or more sets per muscle group a week.6 Three hours like the one below come to about eight to eleven sets a week for each big muscle group (counting a press's sets as half for the muscles it only helps), around that line; two hours, about six or seven. That count is practical arithmetic; how many sets suit you is on how many sets per week.

3. A warm-up that does its job

The time-efficiency review advises keeping the warm-up specific to the exercise, and prioritizing stretching only when flexibility is a goal.1 In a study of 40 resistance-trained men, a warm-up of a few reps with light loads alone was not enough to optimize squat and bench press performance.7 So the warm-up in the hour is a few minutes of anything easy, then lighter sets of the first lift building up to the working weight. (How to warm up before lifting.)

The two main lifts are done as straight sets, with full rest. Only the second pair and the small muscles are alternated, because the time-efficiency review advises against pairing methods for heavy compound free-weight lifts such as the squat and bench press.1 (How to pair exercises.) Add weight by the rule on progressive overload: when the top of the rep range comes with reps to spare, go up the smallest step.

How many hours a week

Two or three. The ACSM's primary recommendation is lifting at least twice a week with all major muscle groups trained.6 Beyond that, a meta-analysis of 25 studies found that when the total sets were matched, how often a muscle was trained made no meaningful difference to growth, so you can pick the frequency you prefer.8 Three hours of A, B, A one week and B, A, B the next is a complete plan. Four days a week? Split the same hour into upper and lower days: the upper/lower plan. Which split suits your week is on workout splits.

Cardio in the hour

If the hour has to hold cardio too, lift first. A 2018 meta-analysis of studies that put both in one session found lower-body strength was higher when lifting came before endurance training, and the order made no difference to aerobic capacity.9 So: the lifting blocks as written, cut the small-muscle block, and finish with ten to fifteen minutes on the bike or the incline treadmill (practical). Cardio and lifting together has the rest.

Common questions

Is an hour too long for a workout?

No. Including a warm-up, a traditional strength session often runs past an hour.1 An hour is too long only if it's spent between sets on a phone. If the hour keeps running to ninety minutes, cut the small-muscle block first.

Is one hour better than thirty minutes?

It's more, not automatically better. More sets meant more growth in the meta-analyses, with diminishing returns,5 and longer rests helped trained men.2 On a short day, the 30-minute workout is the plan.

Can I do an hour every day?

You can, but you don't need to. Two or three hours a week covers the recommendation;6 more days only help if the weekly sets fit what you recover from. Two sessions in one day is its own question: working out twice a day. How often to train walks through the choice.

What if I only have 45 minutes?

That's the 30-minute workout's territory: its list of what to cut first applies to forty-five minutes too.
