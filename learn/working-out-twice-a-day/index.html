Working out twice a day: what the two-a-day trials found

Two sessions in one day sounds like an athlete's schedule or a mistake. The research is small but clear enough to use: when the same training was split into two sessions, it did at least as well as one. Here's what the trials found, the gap to leave between lifting and cardio, and how to set a two-a-day up.

Short answer: In the three trials, two sessions a day did at least as well as one. In 23 trained men, twice-daily sessions raised squat strength more (16.1 versus 7.8 percent) with similar muscle growth. Split the day's sets; don't add more.3

A two-a-day is two training sessions in one day. People end up there for three reasons: an athlete's schedule, a day with two short gaps instead of one long one, or wanting to keep lifting and cardio apart. All three ask the same question: does splitting the day help, hurt, or make no difference?

This page is what the trials measured, which is strength and muscle, not health or recovery in general. How many days a week to train is a different question, and how often to train is the page for it. This one is about the sessions inside a single day.

What the two-a-day trials found

Three trials compared one session a day with two. All three are small and short, and all three studied trained people or athletes.

TrialWhoDesignWhat happened
Häkkinen and Kallinen, 199410 female athletesThree weeks of one daily session, then three weeks with the same total volume split into two daily sessionsNo systematic change in strength or quadriceps size over the one-session period; significant gains in both over the two-session period1
Hartman and colleagues, 200710 nationally competitive male weightlifters, about 20 years oldThree weeks, similar volume, once or twice a dayNo significant differences between the groups on any measure2
Corrêa and colleagues, 202223 resistance-trained menEight weeks, each muscle group trained once or twice a daySquat one-rep max rose 16.1 percent with two daily sessions and 7.8 percent with one; bench press, muscular endurance and muscle thickness rose in both, with no difference between them3

Read together: two sessions a day never did worse than one in these trials, and twice it did better on a strength measure. Hartman's group concluded there were no additional benefits from training twice a day in national-level weightlifters.2 Corrêa's group concluded the extra daily session helped lower-body strength but did not add to upper-body strength, muscular endurance or muscle growth.3 A design note on the 1994 trial: its two periods ran one after the other in the same athletes, so the two-session period also came second.

Why the split might help strength but not size

Two larger bodies of research line up with those results, and the line is a hypothesis, not a finding of the two-a-day trials themselves. For muscle growth, a meta-analysis of 25 studies found that when total volume was matched, how often a muscle was trained made no meaningful difference.4 For strength, a 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies found strength gains increased with training frequency, with diminishing returns, while frequency's effect on muscle growth was compatible with a negligible one.5 That's the same pattern as the 2022 trial: more strength, similar size. Häkkinen's own reading was that smaller units of training may give the nervous system a more effective stimulus.1

Lifting and cardio on the same day

This is the two-a-day most people actually do. In a seven-week trial of 58 amateur rugby players, three of the groups did strength and aerobic training with 0, 6 or 24 hours between them; strength gains in the bench press and half squat were lower with no gap than with 6 or 24 hours, and the authors advised against scheduling the two with less than six hours of recovery between them.6 They also found twice a day with a six-hour gap was, to a lesser extent, not optimal compared with a full day apart.6 The larger picture points a different way: a 2022 meta-analysis of 43 studies found adding aerobic training did not compromise muscle growth or maximal strength, and found explosive strength took a bigger hit when the two shared a session than when they were at least three hours apart.7 So the six-hour gap is one trial's advice for athletes, and a few hours apart is a reasonable practical floor.

If they have to share one session, lift first: a 2018 meta-analysis found lower-body strength was higher when strength training came before endurance training in the same session, and the order made no difference to aerobic capacity.8 Cardio and lifting together has the rest of that research.

How to set up a two-a-day (practical)

Two of the trials split the same training rather than adding to it. So the rule is: take one day's work and divide it, keeping the weekly sets the same. The set-ups below are practical, not tested as written.

Why two sessionsMorningEvening
Two short gaps in the dayThe first half of the session: the main lower and upper liftsThe second half: the second pair and the small muscles
Lifting and cardio apartThe lifting sessionThe cardio, six hours later or more where the day allows
A split by body partLower bodyUpper body

Keep the weekly sets inside the ranges on how many sets per week, and keep the days off: two-a-days don't replace rest days. Write the weights down; if the evening session's weights keep dropping below the morning's plan, the day is too full. A full single session with time to spare is on the 1-hour workout plan.

Common questions

Is it bad to work out twice a day?

For strength and muscle, the trials found two sessions a day did no worse than one over three to eight weeks.3 They measured performance, not health. If you have a medical condition or a medical question, ask a doctor.

Can I do cardio in the morning and weights at night?

Yes, and the gap is the point: in the rugby trial, a six-hour or full-day gap protected strength gains better than none.6 Lifting first was the order tested there.

Should beginners train twice a day?

None of the trials studied beginners. The ACSM's 2026 primary recommendation is lifting at least twice a week with every major muscle group trained,9 which one session a day covers. How to start lifting weights is the beginner plan.

Are two short sessions as good as one long one?

In these trials, at least as good when the training was split rather than added.2 In practice, two thirty-minute halves of one session is a fair way to use a split day.
