Pyramid sets: heavier, lighter, or both, and what the studies measured

A pyramid changes the weight from set to set instead of repeating it. Here are the three shapes, how to load each one, how it compares with straight sets in the studies, and when it's worth the bother.

Short answer: A pyramid changes the weight from set to set: heavier each set (ascending), lighter each set (descending), or up and back down. A 2023 review of 15 studies found pyramid training was not superior to traditional sets for strength gains or muscle growth,1 so use one for variety or to find the day's weight, not as a shortcut.

In straight sets you pick one weight and repeat it. In a pyramid the weight changes from set to set, and the reps change with it: heavier weights get fewer reps, lighter weights get more. Plotted on a page, the weights climb or fall like the side of a pyramid, which is where the name comes from.

This page covers the three shapes, how to load each one, and what the studies measured. Drop sets, where you cut the weight and keep going inside one set, are a different method and live on the supersets page. How many reps suit a set is how many reps.

The three shapes

ShapeAlso calledThe sets, in order
AscendingCrescent, or a plain pyramid12 reps with the lightest weight, 10 reps heavier, 8 reps heavier still
DescendingDecrescent, or a reverse pyramid8 reps with the heaviest weight, 10 reps lighter, 12 reps lighter still
TriangleUp and down12, 10, 8, 10, 12 reps, so the weight climbs and then falls

The rep numbers are examples, not rules. The ascending 12/10/8 is a protocol the studies below used. None of the sources on this page tested the triangle, so everything measured here is about the ascending and descending shapes.

What the studies measured

A 2023 review of 15 studies, six on single sessions and nine on training over weeks, concluded that the pyramid protocol was not superior to the traditional one on acute responses, strength gains or muscle growth. Its practical reading was that the choice can rest on periodization, motivation or personal preference, with the caveat that the studies used rep zones of 8 to 12 or loads of 67% to 85% of a one-rep max.1 Three of the longitudinal trials show what that looked like:

  • In men, on legs. 32 well-trained volunteers trained each leg for 12 weeks. One leg did traditional sets (3 to 5 sets of 6 to 12 reps at 75% of a one-rep max), the other a crescent pyramid (3 to 5 sets of 6 to 15 reps at 65% to 85%) or drop sets, with total volume equalised. Muscle cross-sectional area rose 7.6% with traditional sets and 7.5% with the pyramid, and leg-press strength rose 25.9% with both.2
  • In older women, on the whole body. 33 resistance-trained women, average age about 70, trained three days a week for eight weeks, either 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps at one weight or an ascending 12/10/8 pyramid with heavier loads each set. Chest-press strength and muscle mass improved in both groups with no difference between them.3
  • A wider pyramid. 39 older women trained eight weeks on ascending pyramids of either 12/10/8 or 15/10/5 reps. Both groups gained strength and muscle mass, with no statistical difference between the zones.4

In the first two trials the pyramid did no better than straight sets, and no worse; the third compared two pyramid zones with no straight-set arm. All three covered the ascending shape, over eight to twelve weeks, in well-trained men and in older women; the descending shape appears in the review and in the effort study below.

What a pyramid feels like, and how long to rest

A descending pyramid felt harder than straight sets even when the work added up the same. In 24 resistance-trained men, total training volume didn't differ between drop sets, a descending pyramid and traditional sets on the squat, leg press and knee extension, but the descending pyramid got a higher session effort rating than the traditional sets (6.6 against 5.9, in the study's units), and drop sets rated higher than both.5

Rest between sets matters because the weight changes. In 20 young women who did a crescent pyramid of five leg-press sets to failure, from 60% to 80% of a one-rep max, three minutes' rest allowed more total work than one minute (12,820 kg against 10,367 kg), and two minutes did about as well as three.6 Two to three minutes is a sensible rest when the weights are heavy; rest between sets has the longer version.

How to load a pyramid

This part is practical advice, not a finding from the studies above.

  1. Pick three rep targets, such as 12, 10 and 8, and write the weights next to them in your log.
  2. Start the first set so you'd have two or three reps left. On an ascending pyramid the first set is the easy one; it only counts as a hard set if it ends close to the limit. (What counts as a set.)
  3. Make the smallest jump that lowers your reps to the next target. The last set of an ascending pyramid is the heaviest and ends with two or three reps in reserve. (How to judge reps left.)
  4. On a descending pyramid, warm up first. Your heaviest set comes first, so do the lighter ramp sets from the warm-up page before it, and drop the weight after it.
  5. Progress the same way each week. When you hit every target with reps to spare, add weight to every set by the smallest step, or add a rep to the top set. (Progressive overload.)

Three sets is the usual size. The trials above used three, three to five, and five.

When a pyramid fits

  • When you like it. The 2023 review's own conclusion is that the choice can rest on motivation and preference.1 If a rising weight keeps you in the session, that's a reason.
  • As a way to find the day's weight. An ascending pyramid builds up to a top set without a separate guess.
  • On machines and dumbbells, where changing the weight takes seconds. On a barbell it means plates between sets.
  • If strength is the goal. The ACSM's 2026 stand found strength was enhanced by lifting heavier loads (at least 80% of a one-rep max), for two to three sets, at the beginning of training sessions.7 The stand's line is about which exercises come first; no source here shows a descending pyramid helps strength.

Skip it when you're new and still learning the lift. Straight sets make progress easy to read: same weight, add a rep. And you don't need it for growth: the same stand found that loads anywhere from 30% to 100% of a one-rep max, and training to failure, didn't change muscle growth.7

Pyramid sets vs drop sets

A pyramid changes the weight between sets, with a rest in between. A drop set changes it inside one set, with no rest, and every drop goes to failure. The drop-set studies and the case for using them sparingly are on the supersets page.

Common mistakes

  • Counting the light set. A set that ends with six or seven reps left barely counts toward your week.
  • Going to failure every set. The ACSM's 2026 stand found training to failure didn't change muscle growth,7 and with the weight rising, the last set is the one to leave a rep or two short.
  • Changing the shape every week. The log only shows progress if the plan holds still.
  • Working through a sharp pain. Effort and soreness are part of it; a sharp or lasting pain isn't. Stop that exercise, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Common questions

Pyramid sets or straight sets for muscle?

Either. The review and the first two trials above found no advantage for pyramids over traditional sets on strength or muscle growth.1,2,3

Ascending or descending?

No source on this page compares them head to head. The review searched for crescent and decrescent pyramids against traditional sets.1 In one 2023 study of 24 trained men, the descending pyramid got a higher session effort rating than traditional sets at similar volume.5 Pick the one whose first set you want to be the heavy one.

How many sets in a pyramid?

Three is usual. The trials above used three to five.
