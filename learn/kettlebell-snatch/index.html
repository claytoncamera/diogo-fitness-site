Kettlebell snatch: one bell from between your legs to overhead, in one swing

The kettlebell snatch swings one bell from between your legs all the way overhead in a single move, finishing with a straight arm. Here's how to do it, how it compares with the swing, what the studies measured in force, muscles, oxygen and heart rate, the faults, and the sets.

Short answer: Hike one kettlebell back between your legs, drive your hips forward, pull it up close to your body and punch your hand through as it turns over, finishing with a straight arm overhead. A kettlebell snatch takes one bell from between your legs to overhead, arm straight, in one movement. One force-plate study describes it as an extension of the kettlebell swing: the bell is swung upward from between the legs until it reaches the overhead position.1 The power comes from the hips, as in the kettlebell swing; the difference is that the bell keeps travelling up past your chest and turns over onto the back of your forearm at the top.

It builds on the swing and the kettlebell clean; as a practical order, learn those two first. This page covers the form, how the snatch compares with the swing, what the studies measured, the faults and the sets.

How to do a kettlebell snatch

  1. Set the bell a little in front of you, between your feet, feet about shoulder width. Hinge at the hips with a flat back and take the handle in one hand.
  2. Hike it back high between your legs, as in a swing.
  3. Drive your hips forward and stand up hard. The hips move the bell, not the arm.
  4. Pull it close. As the bell floats up, let your elbow bend and stay near your body so the bell rises along your body instead of arcing out in front.
  5. Punch through. As the bell passes your face, slide your hand through the handle and straighten your arm, so the bell rolls over and settles against the back of your forearm instead of flipping and landing hard.
  6. Lock out overhead: arm straight, bicep near your ear, wrist straight, standing tall. Pause.
  7. Drop it. Let the bell fall in front of you, shift your hips back as it comes down, and let it swing back between your legs for the next rep.

These steps are practical coaching cues, not study findings. In kettlebell sport, a rep counts only when the bell is locked out motionless overhead, and one study describes the sport snatch in six phases, starting from that lockout: the drop, the re-grip, the back swing, the forward swing, the acceleration pull and the hand insertion.1

Snatch vs swing

  • Mechanics. Twenty-two men did 3 sets of 8 snatches and 8 two-handed swings with a 24 kg kettlebell on a force platform. The snatch moved the combined centre of mass of lifter and bell higher (22 vs 18 cm). In both exercises far more work went into moving it up than forward, but the two-handed swing did more forward, horizontal work than the snatch. Apart from that horizontal emphasis, the authors found the mechanical output of the two exercises similar, and suggest coaches could use them interchangeably for the explosive part of a program.2
  • Muscles. Fourteen resistance-trained people did five reps each of a one-arm swing, a one-arm snatch and a one-arm clean with a weight they could lift 8 to 10 times. The snatch and the clean drew more activity than the swing from the obliques on the side opposite the bell. The swing drew more than the snatch from the muscles along the spine, and more than the clean from the outer thigh muscle (part of the quadriceps). The authors conclude the three aren't redundant exercises.3

So the two studies point in slightly different directions: one found the snatch and the two-handed swing mechanically similar, the other found the one-arm snatch and one-arm swing worked some muscles differently. Both measured single sessions, not what weeks of either build.

What else the studies measured

  • Force over a long set. Twelve kettlebell-trained men (about 35 years old on average), all amateur kettlebell sport lifters, did a 6-minute snatch set with a 24 kg bell, changing hands once, at about 14 reps a minute. The force applied to the bell at its point of greatest acceleration averaged about 814 N on the way down and 885 N on the way up, and the peak force through both feet about 1,746 N on the way down and 1,768 N on the way up. Force through the feet was similar in the first and last 14 reps, but the peak force applied to the bell fell over the 6 minutes, and each leg was loaded differently.1
  • Oxygen and heart rate. Ten men, average age about 28, did a graded test of continuous 16 kg snatches, set up like a kettlebell sport event, and a maximal rowing test. On the snatches, their peak oxygen uptake was 37.6 ml/kg/min, about 83% of their rowing maximum, and their peak heart rate 174 beats a minute, about 98% of their rowing maximum heart rate.4
  • A four-week trial. Seventeen female Division I college soccer players were assigned to a snatch group (9) or a circuit-weight-training group (8), which did 20 minutes of free-weight and bodyweight circuits instead. Both trained three days a week for four weeks on top of their off-season strength program. The snatch group did 20 minutes of snatches, 15 seconds of work and 15 seconds of rest. Their maximal oxygen uptake rose by 2.3 ml/kg/min on average, about 6%; the circuit group's didn't change significantly.5 The abstract reports each group's change, not a direct comparison between the groups. That's one small, short trial in college athletes, not a rule for everyone.

For what oxygen-uptake numbers mean, see how to improve VO2 max.

What weight to use

In kettlebell sport competition, the force-plate study notes, amateur lifters use 24 kg, professionals 32 kg and novices 16 kg.1 Those are competition sizes for trained lifters. As a practical start, not a finding: use a bell lighter than the one you swing, one you can press overhead with one arm, and move up only when every rep locks out cleanly.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • The bell banging your forearm. It's flipping over your hand at the top. Keep it closer on the way up and punch your hand through earlier.
  • Pressing it the last few inches. The hips didn't send it high enough. Drive harder from the hinge rather than finishing with the shoulder.
  • A soft elbow overhead. Lock the arm straight and pause, as a sport rep requires.
  • Leaning back at the top. Stand tall with your ribs down; the bell should be over your shoulder, not behind it.
  • Letting it drop without the hinge. Shift your hips back as the bell falls, so it swings between your legs rather than pulling your back round.
  • Lifting through something that hurts. If you have pain, an injury or a medical condition, stop and ask a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where they go

The studies used 3 sets of 8 with a 24 kg bell in 22 men,2 five reps with a weight people could lift 8 to 10 times,3 a 6-minute set in kettlebell sport lifters,1 and 20 minutes of 15 seconds on, 15 seconds off in college athletes.5 Those are study protocols, not a starting plan. As a practical start, not a finding:

  • To learn it: three to five sets of five on each side, resting about a minute, after a few sets of swings.
  • For conditioning: once the reps are clean, work in short intervals, such as 15 to 30 seconds of snatches with as much rest, switching hands each round.
  • Once or twice a week, in the same session as your swings. It shares their hinge. The kettlebell workout shows how a week fits together.

Common questions

Kettlebell snatch or swing?

Both are driven by the hips. In one study, the snatch moved lifter and bell higher and the two-handed swing did more forward work, with otherwise similar output;2 in another, the one-arm snatch drew more from the opposite obliques and less from the muscles along the spine than the one-arm swing.3 In practice, learn the swing first.

What muscles does the kettlebell snatch work?

The muscle study recorded eight: the biceps, the front and back of the shoulder, the muscles along the spine, the outer thigh, one of the hamstrings, the obliques on the opposite side, and the glutes.3 It compared the snatch with the swing and the clean only.

Is the kettlebell snatch cardio?

In ten men doing continuous 16 kg snatches, heart rate reached about 98% of the maximum on a rowing test, and oxygen uptake about 83%.4 That was a continuous graded test; no study found for this page measured shorter sets.

Kettlebell snatch vs barbell snatch?

The force-plate study contrasts them: the kettlebell follows a C-shaped path and can travel between the legs, while a barbell follows an S-shape in front of the knees.1 The barbell snatch is a separate Olympic lift; the Olympic lifts with their own pages here are the power clean and the clean and jerk.
