A beginner 5K plan: eight weeks of run-walk, three days a week, no speed work

A first 5K doesn't need speed work, a watch or a fast start. It needs three easy sessions a week, run-walk intervals that slowly turn into running, and the patience to repeat a week when it felt like too much. Here's an eight-week plan, labelled practical, and the research behind its shape.

Short answer: Run-walk three times a week for eight weeks, at a pace you could talk through, lengthening the jogs each week until week 8 is 30 minutes of jogging. A Dutch 6-week program for adult novice runners used the same three-sessions-a-week, gradually-lengthening shape, aimed at 3 km without stopping.1 The weekly plan below is practical, not a protocol from a trial.

A 5K is five kilometres, a little over three miles, and it's the first distance most people aim at. Some people look for a plan under the name "Couch to 5K", which belongs to a specific programme; this plan is our own and has no connection to that programme. Its shape is run-walk intervals that slowly become running.

It's a plan, labelled practical, not a promise about when you'll finish a 5K; the studies cited here measured groups, and your own pace and start depend on things a page can't see. If you haven't run at all, running for beginners comes first, and how to run a mile without stopping is a shorter first goal.

What the research says about the shape

  • Three sessions a week, lengthening. A Dutch 6-week program for adult novice runners, aimed at learning to run 3 km without stopping, had its participants train three times a week, with the running time and distance gradually increased.1
  • Walk breaks are often recommended. Alternating run and walk periods is often recommended to novice runners, the authors of a 2016 trial write; in 42 non-elite runners doing a marathon, a run/walk strategy and running only finished in similar times.2
  • The pace is a talking one. In 16 physically active volunteers, 30-minute runs held at the last stage where they still passed the talk test produced intensities the authors judged compatible with training in healthy adults.3 A lab study of active people, so a rule of thumb for a beginner.
  • Add the weeks gradually. The World Health Organization advises starting with small amounts of physical activity and gradually increasing frequency, intensity and duration; its adult target is 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, or 75 to 150 vigorous.4

That's all the evidence the plan leans on. The weeks, the interval lengths and the eight-week length are mine: a practical layout.

The plan: eight weeks, three sessions

A practical layout, not a protocol from a trial. Run three days a week with at least a day between sessions (for example Monday, Wednesday and Saturday). Every session starts with 5 minutes of brisk walking and ends with 5 minutes of easy walking. "Jog" means a pace at which you could say a sentence. If a week felt too hard, repeat it before moving on; if a session is missed, pick the week back up, don't skip ahead.

1Jog 1 minute, walk 2 minutes, eight times8 minutes
2Jog 2 minutes, walk 2 minutes, six times12 minutes
3Jog 3 minutes, walk 2 minutes, five times15 minutes
4Jog 5 minutes, walk 2 minutes, four times20 minutes
5Jog 8 minutes, walk 2 minutes, three times24 minutes
6Jog 12 minutes, walk 2 minutes, jog 12 minutes24 minutes
7Jog 20 minutes, no walks20 minutes
8Jog 30 minutes, no walks30 minutes

Measuring the 5K. The plan is built on time, not distance. After week 8, measure what you cover in 30 minutes of jogging, on a treadmill, a watch or a route on a map. If it's short of 5 km, repeat week 8 and add a few minutes at a time; the distance comes from the minutes, and nobody is racing you. Treadmill vs outdoor running covers where to do it.

How the week fits around it

  • The other days. Rest, or walk. Rest days has what a day off is for.
  • Lifting. The WHO also asks adults for muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days a week.4 Two short lower-body sessions on non-run days fit: strength training for runners and cardio and lifting have the details, and the first four weeks of lifting if you've never started.
  • Track it. One line a session: week, how it felt, whether you could talk (the workout log).

One number to know

In the Dutch 6-week program, 1,696 adult novice runners aged 18 to 65 kept a weekly log, and 10.9% of them had a running-related injury during the program, defined as a lower-body or back musculoskeletal complaint, blamed on running, that hampered running for three consecutive sessions.5 It's a count in one program, not a prediction. The plan repeats a week rather than skipping ahead (practical). I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor: if something hurts and won't settle, or you have a medical condition, stop and ask one.

Common questions

Can a complete beginner do a 5K in eight weeks?

No page can promise it. The Dutch program was 6 weeks and aimed at 3 km without stopping, and it measured physical activity, not 5K finishes.1 The plan above is eight weeks because 30 minutes of jogging is the target; repeat any week and the plan simply takes longer.

What if I can't finish a session?

Walk until you can talk, then jog again, and finish the session on time. If a whole week felt too hard, repeat it. Slowing down is the answer to most hard sessions: the talk test is the pace gauge.3

Should I run more than three days a week?

Not in this plan. Three sessions is what the Dutch program used,1 and the days between are part of the plan. More weekly activity adds to the WHO's minutes,4 but walking and lifting can fill them without more running.

Do I have to run the whole 5K?

No. Run-walk is often recommended to novice runners, and in a trial of 42 non-elite runners doing a marathon, those using a run/walk strategy finished in a similar time to those running only.2 The plan above just happens to end with jogging only.

Do I need a watch, an app or a race?

None of them. A clock and a way to measure the distance afterwards is enough. This page doesn't recommend any app or product, and none of the studies cited tested one.

Three runs, two lifting days and a job is a scheduling problem as much as a training one.
