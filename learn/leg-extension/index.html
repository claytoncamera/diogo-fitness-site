Leg extension: the setup, the range, and what to do without a machine

Sit with your knees at the machine's pivot, the pad on your lower shins, and straighten your legs against it. It's the one common exercise that trains the front of the thigh on its own, and the research on it is more specific than for most machines. Here's the setup, the range, the seat angle, what to do at home, and the faults.

Short answer: Line your knees up with the machine's pivot and set the pad on your lower shins, just above the ankles. Straighten your legs until nearly locked, pause, then lower slowly to a deep knee bend. Don't skip the bottom half: in 45 untrained women, reps in the bent-knee half grew the thigh as well as or better than full reps.4

The leg extension is a machine where you sit, hook your shins under a pad and straighten your knees against it. It trains the quadriceps, the four muscles on the front of the thigh, with nothing else doing the work: no hips, no balance, no back. That's why it usually comes last on a leg day, and it reaches one muscle that squats and presses mostly don't.

This page covers what it trains, the setup, the rep, how much range to use, the seat angle, what to do without a machine, and the faults. The full session around it is on the leg day workout.

What the leg extension trains

All four quadriceps muscles straighten the knee, but one of them, the rectus femoris, also crosses the hip. That's the one the leg extension reaches best:

  • More than the leg press. In 15 men, rectus femoris activity was higher in the leg extension than in the leg press, while the other quadriceps muscles were about the same in both.1 In 15 resistance-trained men, the knee extension machine again produced more rectus femoris activity, while the leg press produced more in the outer quadriceps muscle, the vastus lateralis.2
  • What squats leave out. In 17 men who did ten weeks of full or half squats, the quadriceps grew by about 5% either way, but the rectus femoris didn't grow in either group.3

So squats and leg presses build most of the front of the thigh, and the leg extension fills the gap they leave.

How to set up the leg extension machine

  1. Move the backrest until, sitting all the way back, the bend of your knee lines up with the machine's pivot, the round joint at the side of the seat.
  2. Set the shin pad on the front of your lower shins, just above the ankles.
  3. Set the start angle, if the machine has one, so your knees start bent to about a right angle or a little deeper.
  4. Hold the handles and keep your hips down in the seat.

The rep

  1. Straighten your legs against the pad until your knees are nearly locked.
  2. Pause for a second at the top.
  3. Lower in about two or three seconds all the way back to the start, without letting the stack touch down between reps.

How much range to use

The bottom of the rep, with the knee deeply bent, is where the quadriceps are longest, and it's the part most worth keeping:

  • The bent-knee half did well. In 45 untrained women, partial reps in the bent-knee half of the movement grew parts of the thigh more than full reps or reps in the straight-leg half. Reps in the straight-leg half alone grew some regions no more than not training at all.4
  • In trained lifters, a draw. In 16 resistance-trained men who trained one leg with partials in the stretched part of each movement and the other with full reps, on the leg press, the leg extension and the lying leg curl for eight weeks, the outer quadriceps grew similarly in both legs.5 In a pre-registered trial of 297 people at 15 sites, thigh muscle growth with stretched-position partials and with full reps was practically the same.6
  • The overall verdict. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found too little data to say whether range of motion changes muscle growth.7

So the rule is simple: never cut the bottom off. Full reps, from a deep bend to nearly straight, are the default; when they run out at the end of a set, a few partials in the bent-knee half keep the set going. Reps that only move through the last few inches near a straight leg are the version to drop. The same question comes up on lateral raises, where it hasn't been tested yet.

The seat angle

Because the rectus femoris crosses the hip, how upright you sit changes its length. In 22 untrained men who trained one leg with the hip bent to 90 degrees, sitting upright, and the other at 40 degrees, leaning back, for ten weeks, the rectus femoris grew more with the reclined seat; the vastus lateralis grew about the same either way.8 If your machine's backrest tilts back, use it. If it doesn't, the upright version still trains the whole quadriceps.

Leg extensions at home, without a machine

  • Band leg extension. Sit on a high chair or bench, loop a band around one ankle, anchor it behind and below you, and straighten the knee. A review of eight studies found bands and tubing produced similar strength gains to machines and dumbbells for the lower body.9
  • Reverse Nordic. Kneel upright with your feet held or tucked under something heavy, and lean your whole body back from the knees as far as you can control, then come back up. In 23 healthy volunteers, 15 of them men, it produced rectus femoris activity during the lowering similar to the single-leg squat, the Bulgarian split squat and the lunge, with less knee bend than any of them.10
  • Sissy squat. Holding a doorframe, rise onto your toes and let your knees travel forward as you lean your body back. No study of it was found; it's the same knee-forward pattern as the reverse Nordic, standing.
  • Split squats and lunges. They train the front of the thigh with your hips involved, so they're closer to a squat than to a leg extension, but at home they're where most of the work comes from.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Knees out of line with the pivot. The pad slides up and down your shins during the rep. Move the backrest until it doesn't.
  • Kicking the weight up. If the stack flies up and drops, it's too heavy. Lift for a second, lower for two or three.
  • Hips lifting off the seat. Hold the handles and sit back; the hips stay down through the set.
  • Cutting the bottom off. Short reps near a straight leg skip the part with the best evidence. Return all the way to the start.
  • Extending through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • Two or three sets of 10 to 15, after the squat or leg press, as on the leg day workout. Single-joint machines suit the higher end of the rep range. (How many reps.)
  • Close to failure. A machine you can stop at any point is a good place for zero or one rep left. (How to judge reps left.)
  • Pair it with the leg curl, the front and back of the thigh on two machines that often sit side by side.
  • After a machine squat? On a day built around the hack squat, the leg extension is the natural finisher.
