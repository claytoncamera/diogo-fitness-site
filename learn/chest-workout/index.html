Chest workout: three exercises, counted by the week

Three kinds of exercise cover the chest: a flat press, a low incline and a fly. What decides how much they do is your weekly hard sets. Here's the plan, gym or home.

Short answer: Build a chest workout on three exercises: a flat press, an incline press at about 30 degrees4 and a fly. Then count hard sets across the week: the American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 overview ties ten or more sets per muscle group a week to more muscle growth.2 At home, push-ups and dumbbells fill the same slots.

Most chest workouts you'll find are a list of exercises. The list matters less than two questions: which kinds of exercise reach the whole chest, and how many hard sets they add up to across a week. This page answers both, then puts them into a two-day plan with a gym version and a home version.

It doesn't cover how to set up and press a barbell; that's bench press form. Where the chest days sit in your week is your workout split.

Count the week, not the workout

For muscle, the number that tracks growth is hard sets per muscle per week. Three large analyses point the same way:

  • More sets, more growth. A 2017 meta-analysis of 15 studies found a graded dose-response: each extra weekly set was linked to a little more growth, with a trend toward ten or more sets per muscle doing better than fewer.1
  • Ten as a marker. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, an overview of the research, found that ten or more sets per muscle group a week enhanced muscle growth.2
  • With diminishing returns. A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 participants found that muscle size keeps rising with weekly sets, with diminishing returns. The counting method with the strongest evidence scored a set that trains a muscle only indirectly as half a set.3

For the chest, that makes counting simple. Every hard set of a press, a fly or a push-up is one chest set. (Presses count for the triceps too, as half sets: the arm workout.) A hard set ends with only a few reps left in you (here's how to judge that); warm-up sets don't count. In your first months, fewer is enough: progressive overload puts a beginner on two or three full-body days at around 6 to 10 hard sets per muscle a week. Ten is a number to build toward, not a floor for week one.

The flat press: the base

A flat press trains the whole chest at once. In 30 trained adults, each with at least a year of lifting three or more times a week, the flat bench produced similar activity across the upper, middle and lower parts of the chest.4

The tool matters less than people argue about. In a 2011 study of 12 trained men who tested a barbell, a Smith machine and dumbbells, chest activity didn't differ between the three; the loads did.5 So a barbell bench, a dumbbell bench or a chest-press machine can all fill this slot. Pick the one you can load and add to, and keep it for a few months.

The incline: keep it low

An incline press moves the work up the chest, until the bench gets steep enough that the front of the shoulder does more of the work.

  • Where the upper chest works hardest. In the 2020 study of 30 trained adults, each lifter pressed at 0, 15, 30, 45 and 60 degrees, at 60% of their max for each angle. The upper chest was most active at 30 degrees. Above 30, the front of the shoulder did more and all three parts of the chest did less.4
  • What it did over eight weeks. 47 untrained young men trained once a week for eight weeks on the flat bench, the incline bench or both, with the same number of sets in each group. Strength rose by about the same in all three. Muscle thickness changed differently at only one of three sites, high on the chest, where the incline group gained the most.6

Read together: a low incline, around 30 degrees, adds the most upper-chest work, and a steeper one gives the front of the shoulder more of the work, which is the job of the shoulder workout. One study measured a single session and the other was eight weeks in beginners, so the incline is a useful addition, not a requirement.

The fly: an extra, not the base

A fly moves only at the shoulder, with the arms nearly straight. In 17 resistance-trained men, about five years into lifting, the bench press produced more chest, front-shoulder and triceps activity than a dumbbell fly across most of the rep, and the fly was done with lighter weights. The authors' advice: both can go in a program, with the emphasis on the bench press.7 On whether single-joint or multi-joint exercises build more muscle, the ACSM's 2026 overview found too little data to say.2

So the fly is a way to add chest sets without adding more pressing: a cable fly, a fly machine or dumbbells. Keep it after the presses: the ACSM's 2009 position stand advises putting multiple-joint exercises before single-joint ones in strength programs,8 and that is the order used here, though the ACSM's 2026 stand found that exercise order did not affect muscle growth.2 Keep the weight light enough to control the bottom of each rep. A light fly is also a good place to try the mind-muscle connection.

A week of chest: the plan

Two chest days a week, at least a day apart, about ten hard sets in total. On an upper/lower split they're the two upper days; on push/pull/legs, the two push days.

The week10

Reps: for muscle, the ACSM's 2009 stand puts the emphasis on sets of 6 to 12 reps.8 That suits the presses; a fly is easier to control at the top of that range or a little above it. Every set stops with two or three reps still in you.

Your first months: two sets of each exercise, eight a week, inside that 6-to-10 range. Add from there one set at a time, and only when your lifts stop climbing. Already past ten? More sets can still add growth, with the diminishing returns the 2026 meta-regression found, and every set has to be recovered from.

At home

The home column works because, for muscle, the weight matters less than you might think. The ACSM's 2026 overview found muscle growth wasn't affected by loads anywhere from 30% to 100% of a one-rep max.2 What changes at home is how you make a set hard enough to count:

  • Push-ups count as presses. When regular push-ups pass about twenty reps, move to a harder version; how to do push-ups runs from incline to full to harder ones.
  • A dumbbell floor press is the flat press without a bench. The three-day dumbbell plan uses it.
  • Dumbbell flies on the floor end each rep when your upper arms touch the floor.
  • Slow the lowering to three or four seconds when the reps climb too high for the weights you have.

Common mistakes

  • Bench press and nothing else. It's the base, not the whole week. An incline and a fly add work the flat bench doesn't.
  • A steep incline. Above about 30 degrees the front of the shoulder does more and the chest less. Keep the chest version near 30.
  • Counting warm-ups and easy sets. Only sets that end near failure go in the weekly count.
  • Pressing without pulling. A week of presses needs rows and pulldowns too: the back workout.
  • Pressing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.

Common questions

How many chest sets a week?

Build toward ten or more hard sets.2 Early on, fewer is enough.

What incline angle should I use?

Around 30 degrees, where the upper chest was most active; steeper, the front of the shoulder did more.4
