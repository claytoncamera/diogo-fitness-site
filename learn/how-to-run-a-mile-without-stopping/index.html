How to run a mile without stopping: slow down, take walk breaks, and lengthen the jogs

A mile you can't finish may be as much a pace problem as a fitness one. Here's how to run one without stopping: slow down, use walk breaks, make the jogs longer each week, and what the studies of beginners and mile-length runs measured.

Short answer: Run slower than you think you should, at a pace where you could say a sentence, and use walk breaks, lengthening the jogs each week until the walks go. If you can't run a mile without stopping, a likely reason is the first minute: you start at the pace of a person who's about to run 400 m, and soon you're breathing too hard to talk. The fix is boring: slow down, walk on purpose, and lengthen the jogs week by week.

The lab studies on this site run 1,600 m and call it about a mile.1 This page covers the pace, the walk breaks, a six-week build to your first mile, and what the studies measured. It's a page about how a first mile gets built, not a promise about when you'll run yours; the studies cover groups, and your own timeline depends on where you start. If you're starting from scratch, running for beginners is the broader page.

1. Slow down until you could talk

A likely reason a first mile fails is the pace, not the distance. In 16 physically active volunteers, 30-minute runs held at the last stage where they still passed the talk test produced intensities the authors judged compatible with training in healthy adults.2 That was a lab study of active people, not of first-time runners, so use it as a rule of thumb: if you can't say a short sentence out loud, you're running faster than you can hold, so slow down, even if it feels like a shuffle. It's not a race pace, and nothing here asks for one: for scale, the 1,600 m run in a 2012 lab study was at 160 m a minute, a mile in about 10 minutes, with a walk at 86 m a minute taking about 18.6.1 Those were the study's speeds, not targets, and your first mile will take as long as it takes.

2. Walk on purpose, then lengthen the jogs

Walk breaks aren't failing. They're how the beginner program in the research was built: the Dutch 6-week program for adult novice runners, aimed at learning to run 3 km continuously, had them train three times a week with run-walk sessions in which the running time and distance were gradually increased.3 In a marathon trial of 42 non-elite runners, a run/walk strategy and running only gave similar finish times; the paper notes run/walk periods are often recommended to novice runners.4 The marathon runners aren't beginners on a first mile, so take it as evidence that walking breaks don't cost a runner their finish, not as a plan. The plan is below.

The six-week build

A practical layout, not a protocol from a trial. Three sessions a week with a day between them. Each starts with 5 minutes of brisk walking and ends with 5 minutes of easy walking. "Jog" means a pace at which you could talk. If a week feels too hard, repeat it before moving on.

WeekEach of the three sessions
1Jog 1 minute, walk 2 minutes, eight times
2Jog 2 minutes, walk 2 minutes, six times
3Jog 3 minutes, walk 2 minutes, five times
4Jog 5 minutes, walk 2 minutes, four times
5Jog 8 minutes, walk 2 minutes, three times
6Your mile: jog it with no walks, at the same pace as week 5's jogs. Measure it on a treadmill, a map or a watch. If you need a walk, take it, and try again at the next session

The mile itself is the third session of week 6. If the first two feel easy, there's nothing to prove by speeding up: the aim is the distance without stopping. Once you have it, the same shape stretches to half an hour in a beginner 5K plan.

On the day

  • Warm up by walking. Five minutes of brisk walking, then start the jog slower than feels natural.
  • Hold the pace you started at. If the second half feels easier than the first, that was the right pace; a faster second half is a better problem to have than a stop.
  • Treadmill or outside. In a lab study of 24 people, running 1,600 m at 2.82 m/s (about 6.3 mph) took 481 kJ on a treadmill and 480 on a track, a subgroup,5 so pick the one you'll actually turn up for. More on that: treadmill vs outdoor running.
  • Count what you did. A mile with a walk in the middle still counts as the first one on the way to a mile without. Write the session down (the workout log).

Common questions

Why can't I run a mile without stopping?

Often it's the pace. The research can't diagnose you, but the talk test is the practical check: if you couldn't say a sentence, the pace was more than you could hold.2 Slowing down and adding walk breaks is how the beginner program in the studies was built.3 I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor: if breathlessness feels out of proportion or comes with anything else that worries you, stop and ask one.

How long should it take to run a mile?

As long as it takes at a pace you could talk at. There's no standard time for a beginner on this page: the only speeds here are the ones from the lab, a mile in about 10 minutes at the study's run and about 18.6 at its walk,1 and they were set by the researchers.

How many times a week should I practise?

Three is the number the Dutch beginner program used.3 Leaving a day between sessions is practical, not a finding. Rest days covers the other days.

Can I run a mile without getting out of breath?

The talk test is the practical check: at a pace where you can say a sentence, you're running slower than a pace that leaves you gasping.2 Whether you'll stay comfortable for a whole mile depends on you; slow down until you can, and see a doctor if breathlessness worries you.

Is walking part of it okay?

For a first mile, yes: the plan above uses it on purpose, and the beginner program above is built on run-walk,3 while marathon runners who used it finished in a similar time to those who didn't.4

How much exercise is enough in a week?

The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines ask adults for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, or 75 to 150 vigorous, and advise starting with small amounts and gradually increasing how often, how hard and how long.6 The plan's three sessions are a start toward those minutes, not all of them.
