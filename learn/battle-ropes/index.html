Battle ropes: how to do the waves, what the studies measured and a session to run

Hold an end of a heavy rope in each hand and make waves. Battle ropes are arm-driven interval work: short, hard and over quickly. Here's how to do them, what the studies measured for effort, rest and muscle activity, what weeks of rope training changed, a session to run, and what to do without a rope.

Short answer: Anchor a heavy rope, hold an end in each hand, and from a half-squat make waves with your arms. As a practical start, do six rounds of 20 to 30 seconds with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. Battle ropes are long, heavy ropes that you anchor at the middle and hold by the two ends. You make waves by moving your arms up and down while your legs and trunk hold the stance. A round lasts seconds, the effort is high, and the rest between rounds changes how hard the session is.

This page covers the waves, what the studies measured for effort, rest and muscle activity, what weeks of rope training changed, a session to run, and what to do without a rope. A rope session is one way to run a HIIT workout, and it uses the same effort scale.

How to do battle ropes

  1. Anchor the rope around a post or heavy object at its middle, so you hold two equal ends. Step back until the rope has a slight sag.
  2. Take a half-squat, feet a little wider than your hips, back flat, chest up, an end in each hand.
  3. Make the wave. Move your arms up and down; the wave should travel to the anchor. Your hips and knees help drive it, so it isn't an arm-only exercise.
  4. Keep your stance for the whole round, and stop it when the waves shrink.
  • Alternating waves: one arm up as the other goes down.
  • Double waves: both arms together, up and down. The studies below used both, often in the same round.
  • Seated: the same waves without the leg drive. In 40 healthy people, 10 rounds of 15 seconds of waves and 45 of rest peaked at about 72% of the oxygen uptake each person reached in a cycling test when seated, and about 68% of a treadmill-test value when standing; different people did each.1

How hard they are, and what rest does

  • Against lifting. In 10 resistance-trained men aged about 21, a session of three 30-second rope bouts with two minutes of rest averaged 24.6 mL per kilogram per minute of oxygen use, work and rest together, the highest of 13 protocols alongside a burpee session at 22.9. The free-weight sessions (three sets of up to 10 repetitions at 75% of a one-repetition maximum) averaged 19.6 for the squat, 18.9 for the deadlift and 17.3 for the lunge, and the plank 7.9.2
  • The rest interval. In 12 men and 10 women, about 21 years old, eight 30-second intervals (15 seconds of single-arm waves, then 15 of double-arm waves) used about 50 to 53% of maximum oxygen uptake on average with a one-minute rest and about 38 to 41% with a two-minute rest. Heart rate and blood lactate were also higher with the shorter rest.3 Shorter rest makes the same waves a harder session.
  • The round length. In 14 active men, three all-out rope protocols with the same total work and a 1:3 work-to-rest ratio (12 × 10 seconds, 8 × 15 seconds, 4 × 30 seconds) didn't differ in enjoyment, intention or self-efficacy.4 Pick the format you'll repeat.

For how hard "hard" is, the HIIT page's scale applies: a few words at most during a round, 7 or 8 out of 10.

What the waves ask of your muscles

In 21 volunteers, muscle activity (not growth) ranged from 51 to 73% of a maximal contraction in the front of the shoulder, the oblique muscle at the side of the trunk and the lower-back muscles during alternating and double waves, and only 14 to 18% in the side glute. Alternating waves gave the obliques 20 percentage points more activity than double waves; double waves gave the lower back 16 more.5 Of the four muscles measured, shoulder, oblique and lower back had the most muscle activity and the side glute the least (muscle activity, not growth).

What weeks of rope training changed

  • Six weeks, active adults. In 18 men and 15 women, average age 23, who did 10 all-out 30-second bouts of double and alternating waves with 60 seconds of rest, three times a week, the ropes weighed 20 lb for the women and 25 lb for the men, then 10 lb more after three weeks. They worked at 85% or more of predicted maximum heart rate. After three and six weeks, upper-body maximal oxygen uptake, shoulder strength and power, and push-up and sit-up endurance were higher than at the start; the abstract describes no comparison group.6
  • Eight weeks against shuttle runs. In 30 male college basketball players, three sessions a week of rope intervals and shuttle-run intervals with the same sets, time and rest both improved aerobic capacity (17.6% for the rope group against 12.0%). The rope group also improved upper-body anaerobic power, jump height and core endurance, which the shuttle-run group didn't.7
  • Across 21 studies. In a 2026 meta-analysis of 590 participants aged 11 to 25, rope training improved upper-limb explosive power, grip strength, upper-limb muscular endurance and core endurance compared with control conditions, while its effect on lower-limb explosive power was uncertain. The authors conclude it "may" improve these, note large differences between the studies, and say the findings need confirming.8

All three involve young people and short trials, so read them as what rope intervals did in those groups, not as a promise for anyone else.

A rope session to run

This is a practical session built on the designs above, not a protocol from a trial.

  1. Warm up for five easy minutes: a brisk walk or easy steps, then a few slow waves.
  2. Six rounds: 20 to 30 seconds of waves, alternating then double, at a pace where you could manage only a few words, and 60 to 90 seconds of rest, standing or walking.
  3. Cool down for three minutes of easy walking.

That is about 16 to 20 minutes, with 2 to 3 minutes of hard work. Add one thing at a time: a round, then a shorter rest, then a heavier rope. The six-week trial used 10 rounds of 30 seconds with 60 seconds of rest.6

No rope? Swaps

None of these is the same exercise. Burpees came closest in the oxygen-use comparison above.2 Practical swaps for the same interval structure are kettlebell swings, mountain climbers and bear crawls.

Where it fits in your week

The HIIT page's practical start applies: one session a week for two weeks, then two, and not on a day when you're training your legs or shoulders hard. The trials above ran three sessions a week.6,7 Cardio and lifting covers how far apart to put it from your pressing days.

Common questions

Are battle ropes cardio or strength?

Both were measured. The rope session in the first study had a higher average rate of oxygen use than the lifting sessions did,2 and the six-week trial, whose abstract describes no comparison group, found higher upper-body oxygen uptake and shoulder strength than at the start.6 They don't replace heavy lifting.

Can a beginner use them?

The groups above were active or trained, so none shows what happens to a beginner. Start with fewer rounds, longer rest and a lighter rope, and rate the effort 7 rather than 10.

What if something hurts?

If you have pain, an injury or a condition, a doctor or a physical therapist should set the limits first.
