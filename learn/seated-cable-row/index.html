Seated cable row: the setup, the pull, and which grip

Sit tall, pull the handle to your stomach, and let your arms go long again without rocking. Most of the questions are about the grip, and the research gives a fairly clear answer. Here's the setup, the pull, neutral against wide, the faults, the versions and what to swap in.

Short answer: Feet on the platform, knees slightly bent, sit tall. Pull the handle to your stomach with your elbows close, then let your arms go long without rounding forward. A 2026 review linked a narrower grip and elbows kept closer to the body with more lat activity.3 Keep your torso nearly still: lean back a little, no more.

The seated cable row is a horizontal pull: you sit at a low cable with your feet braced on a platform and pull a handle toward your stomach. The cable sets the weight and the platform holds you in place, so the effort goes into the pull, not into holding yourself bent over.

This page covers the setup, the pull, neutral against wide grips, the faults (mostly rocking and rounding), the single-arm and machine versions, and what to swap in. Where it fits in a session is on the back workout; its vertical partner is the lat pulldown. Looking for the rowing machine instead? That's cardio, and zone 2 cardio covers it.

The setup

  1. Sit facing the stack with your feet flat on the platform and your knees slightly bent.
  2. Take the handle, a V-handle to start, and slide back until your arms are straight and the weight lifts off the stack.
  3. Sit tall. Chest up, shoulders down, your back in a neutral position from head to hips.
  4. Torso upright, or leaning back a few degrees. That's where it stays for the whole set.

The pull, step by step

  1. Start long: arms straight, shoulder blades allowed to drift forward a little.
  2. Drive your elbows back past your ribs, close to your sides, until the handle touches your stomach.
  3. Keep your torso still. The arms and shoulder blades move; your hips and trunk don't.
  4. Let the handle return under control until your arms are straight again. That's one rep.

What it trains. The lats down the sides of your back, the muscles between your shoulder blades, the back of the shoulder and the biceps. In 12 healthy men holding each pull still, the seated row produced the most activity between the shoulder blades of the four pulls tested, and shared the highest ratio of lat to biceps activity with the wide-grip pulldown.1

Squeeze the shoulder blades or let them move? Either. In that study, actively pulling the shoulder blades back didn't change activity between them or in the lats.1 In 14 resistance-trained men, holding the shoulder blades back for the whole set and letting them move gave comparable overall muscle activity; holding them back raised rear-shoulder activity on the pull.2

Neutral, narrow or wide grip

A 2026 review of the activity research found five seated-row studies, about 81 people in all,3 and two findings that point the same way:

  • Narrow favored the lats. In 14 resistance-trained men, a narrow grip produced more lat activity on both the pull and the return, while a wide grip produced more activity in the trapezius and the side of the shoulder.3 The same grip question comes up on the upright row.
  • Elbows low favored the lats. In 11 recreationally trained men and 10 women, rowing with the elbows raised out to 60 or 90 degrees raised activity in the upper and middle trapezius and the back of the shoulder, while lower elbow angles favored the lats.3

The review is careful with this: the two findings came from separate studies, the samples were small (12 to 21 people each) and mostly young, and they measured muscle activity during a few reps, not growth over months.3 It also notes the pattern is specific to the seated row: on the pulldown, grip width showed no significant effect.3

In practice, a V-handle puts your hands close with the palms facing and keeps the elbows by your sides. A wide overhand bar spreads the hands and lets the elbows flare. So pick the V-handle, or a narrow grip with elbows close, when the lats are the goal, and a wide bar with elbows out when you want more upper back and rear shoulder. Using both across a week is fine. The same handle hooks under the bar for the T-bar row.

The common faults, and a fix for each

  • Rocking. The torso swings back to start each rep and forward to finish it, so the swing moves the weight. A few degrees of lean is fine. Go lighter until your torso stays still.
  • Rounding to chase the handle. Let the shoulders reach forward at the end of each rep, but stop before your back rounds.
  • Shrugging. Shoulders up at your ears as the handle comes in. Keep them down and pull the elbows back, not up.
  • Half reps. Arms straight at the start, handle to your stomach at the finish.
  • Feeling it in your arms, not your back. In 20 people with little or no lifting experience doing a seated row, instructions to use the back muscles raised lat activity in the first reps, but had little effect across a whole set taken to failure.4 Think of your hands as hooks and lead with the elbows; the arms still work, and that's fine.
  • Rowing through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.

Versions and alternatives

VersionHowWhat changes
V-handle rowPalms facing, hands a few inches apartElbows stay close: the lat-leaning setup above
Wide-grip rowA long bar, overhand, hands wider than your shouldersElbows out: more upper back and rear shoulder
Single-arm cable rowOne handle, one arm at a timeEach side works alone, and your trunk resists the twist
Machine rowChest against a pad, handles in frontThe pad holds your torso, so rocking is off the table
Dumbbell rowOne hand and knee braced on a benchOne dumbbell is enough: the dumbbell row
Barbell rowHinged forward, a bar in both handsYou hold the hinge yourself: barbell row form

The trunk works differently across these. In 15 resistance-trained men, cable and machine rows done one arm at a time worked the obliques more than the two-arm versions, and a free-weight bent-over row worked the muscles along the spine more than a machine row did.5 For the back itself, one arm or two doesn't seem to matter much: a 2025 meta-analysis found no difference in muscle growth between one-limb and two-limb training, while strength rose most in whichever version was trained.6 At home, a band anchored in a door at waist height gives you the same pull (the band workout shows the setup).

Sets, reps and where it goes

  • Two or three sets of 8 to 12, with a rep or two in reserve. (How to judge reps left.) The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand advises at least two sets per exercise.7
  • Pair it with a vertical pull, such as the lat pulldown or a pull-up. The back workout has a slot for each direction.
  • Cable or dumbbell, your call. The same 2026 stand found strength gains didn't depend on free weights versus machines,7 so use the row your gym and your week make easy.
  • On a cable station day, pair it with a push such as the chest fly: one set of each, then rest.
  • Add weight when every set reaches 12 with reps to spare, one plate on the stack at a time. (Progressive overload covers how much.)

Common questions

Which muscles does the seated cable row work?

The lats down the sides of your back, the muscles between your shoulder blades, the back of the shoulder and the biceps. In a 2004 study of 12 healthy men, it produced the most activity between the shoulder blades of the four pulls tested.1

Wide or narrow grip?

A V-handle, or a narrow grip with elbows close, when the lats are the goal; a wide bar with elbows out for more upper back and rear shoulder.
