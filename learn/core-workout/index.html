Core workout: what your squats already cover, and what to add

Squats and deadlifts work the muscles along your spine hard. The front and sides of your trunk are another story. Here's what the studies measured, a four-exercise core block for the end of a session, and where it fits in your week.

Short answer: Heavy squats and deadlifts work the muscles along your spine harder than planks, side planks or ball exercises, but in the same studies they worked the abs and obliques no harder.1,2,4 So end your sessions with a short core block: a plank, a Pallof press, a carry and a crunch, two or three sets each.

"Core" usually means the muscles that wrap your trunk: the abs down the front, the obliques at the sides and the muscles along your spine at the back. In lifting, their main job is to hold your trunk still while your arms and legs move a weight. Under a bar, that job is the brace, tightening your midsection before each rep; squat form and deadlift form show how to set it for each lift.

This page is for someone who already squats, hinges, pushes and pulls. (If that isn't you yet, the first four weeks comes first.) It answers whether the big lifts already train your core, then gives a four-exercise block and where it goes in your week.

Do squats and deadlifts already train your core?

The back of it, yes. The front, much less. Studies that measured trunk muscle activity during heavy lifts and core exercises agree on the first part:

  • Heavy lifts beat floor exercises for the back muscles. In 16 people, squats and deadlifts at 80% of a one-rep max produced more activity in the muscles along the spine than the same lifts with body weight, a superman or a side plank. The abs and obliques showed no differences.1
  • And ball exercises. In nine resistance-trained men, squats and deadlifts at 90 and 100% of a one-rep max worked the back muscles harder than three stability-ball exercises, with no differences in the abs or obliques across any of them.2
  • The review. A 2013 systematic review of 17 studies and 252 healthy adults found a deep back muscle, the multifidus, more active in free-weight exercises than in ball and device exercises, and its authors advised building core training on multi-joint free-weight lifts. It covered only three deep muscles, though: not the rectus abdominis (the front abs) or the obliques.3

The front of the trunk is where the lifts fall short. In 12 resistance-trained men, seven of them competitive powerlifters, heavy back squats (a six-rep max) worked the abs and obliques about as hard as a plank with a weight on the back, and the spine muscles harder.4 In seven recreationally trained men who front-squatted and overhead-squatted at up to 95% of a three-rep max, a plank worked the abs and obliques harder than either squat.5

Read together: heavy lifts already give the muscles along your spine plenty of work, but the abs and obliques get about a plank's worth at most. All of these studies measured muscle activity during the exercises, not growth or strength over weeks, and they were small and mostly in men. Take them as a direction, not a verdict.

What adding core work changes

Training studies give a modest answer. A 2016 meta-analysis of trained people aged 16 to 44 found that core strength training made the trunk muscles much stronger, with only small-to-medium gains in other measures of fitness and athletic performance, compared with no extra training or regular training alone. The studies were of low quality, and trunk strength itself was only weakly linked to performance.6

So the case for a core block is plain: it trains the front and sides of your trunk more directly than your main lifts do, and a stronger trunk is a fair goal on its own. Don't count on it to move your other numbers much. It takes about ten minutes.

The core block: four exercises, four jobs

Each one trains a single job: refuse to arch, refuse to twist, refuse to lean, or curl forward.

The plank: stop the arch

Forearms and toes on the floor, a straight line from your head to your heels, and your midsection tight so your hips don't sag. Hold it only as long as that line holds. Can't keep it for twenty seconds? Start with the dead bug: on your back, arms and knees up, slowly lowering the opposite arm and leg while your back stays down on the floor.

Once forty-five seconds feels easy, make the plank harder rather than longer. In 19 people, a long-lever plank raised activity in the abs over a standard plank, a plank with the pelvis tucked under raised it in the obliques, and the two together raised both; the long lever did more of the work.7 Face up, the same two ideas make the hollow body hold.

The Pallof press: stop the twist

Stand side-on to a cable or a band anchored at chest height, hold the handle at your chest with both hands, and press it straight out without letting it turn you. The cable pulls you sideways; the job is to stay square. In a 2025 study of 12 physically active adults, six men and six women, who held the press out at arm's length, kneeling was the easiest version to keep still, and standing with one foot directly in front of the other was harder. It measured how much the pelvis moved, not muscle activity.8 The Russian twist trains the opposite job: turning.

The carry: stop the lean

Pick up a heavy dumbbell or kettlebell in one hand and walk tall, without leaning toward the weight or away from it. That's a suitcase carry; a weight in each hand makes it a farmer's carry. In 18 college-aged adults (12 women and 6 men), with about 25 kilograms in each loaded hand on average, both carries worked the muscles along the spine more than holding the same weights still, and the plank worked them very little. The plank worked the front abs hardest of anything tested. The suitcase carry, weight in the right hand, worked the obliques on the left side about as hard as the plank did.9

So the plank and the carry do different jobs; the one-hand carry is the one that loads your sides. Walk 20 to 30 meters, then switch hands. With no weight at home, a side plank on one forearm works the same refusal to lean.

The crunch: curl forward

The other three resist movement; this one bends your trunk forward, the abs' other job. Start with a floor crunch: curl your shoulders up toward your hips, then lower slowly. In a gym, a cable crunch (kneeling, pulling a rope down as you curl) or an ab-crunch machine lets you add weight like any other exercise. In 21 healthy men and women aged 23 to 43, a hanging knee raise with arm straps and a reverse crunch on a bench inclined 30 degrees were among the exercises that worked the abs and obliques hardest,10 so those are the next step up.

Farmer's carry and suitcase carry

The carry in the block is the suitcase carry, a weight in one hand. Its partner is the farmer's carry, a weight in each hand. Both are bracing on the move: the weight tries to pull you out of position, and your trunk doesn't let it.

  • Walking adds the work. In the 18-person study above, the farmer's carry raised activity in all four trunk muscles measured, on both sides, over holding the same dumbbells still, and the suitcase carry raised the muscles along the spine over a one-hand hold.9
  • One hand also works the far hip. In 26 healthy adults (14 women, 12 men) walking on a treadmill with 15 to 20% of their body weight in one hand, the hip muscles on the other side, the ones that keep the pelvis level, were more active than when walking without it.11

Both studies measured muscle activity during the walk, not changes over weeks. With a sled, the sled push pairs with a carry as a finisher.

How to brace under load: pick the weight up with a hinge, as in deadlift form. Stand tall and tighten your midsection, then walk with short, even steps, shoulders level and arms long. End the walk when your position slips: a lean, a twist, a shoulder dropping.

Which one: the farmer's carry holds more total weight, so your grip works hard too; the suitcase carry loads your sides. Rotate them through the block's carry slot. If your hands give out first, the forearm workout covers grip.

Leg raises: hanging, and lying on the floor

The hanging leg raise is the hardest step on the block's curl-forward row. Hang from a pull-up bar with your arms straight, raise your legs in front of you by curling your hips up, then lower them slowly without swinging. With the knees bent it's the hanging knee raise, the easier version, because bent legs make a shorter lever.

In 14 men comparing three exercises (the body saw, the hanging straight-leg raise and a walkout from a push-up), the hanging straight-leg raise was the biggest challenge to the abdominal wall, with front-ab (rectus abdominis) activity above 130% of a maximal contraction and outer-oblique activity at 88%.12 In the 21-person study above, the knee-raise version with arm straps was among the exercises that worked the abs and obliques hardest.10 Both studies measured muscle activity during the exercise, not changes over weeks.

Start with the knee raise, in arm straps if your grip gives out before your abs do, and move to straight legs once ten to fifteen controlled reps come with a rep or two left. If hanging itself is the limit, the dead hang in how to do a pull-up builds it, and the forearm workout covers grip.

Lying leg raise

The floor version needs no bar and no grip. Lie on your back with your legs straight and your lower back pressed down, raise both legs until they point up, then lower them slowly, stopping before your back lifts off the floor. Bend your knees to make it easier. Those cues are practical. Two studies of similar leg lifts measured the work: in 18 healthy, physically active women, a two-leg raise held still produced significantly more front-ab activity than a plank,13 and a 2024 review of nine studies, covering 109 healthy adults aged 20 to 40, reported the iliopsoas, a deep hip flexor, above 60% of a maximal contraction in supine hip flexion and leg lifts.14 Both measured muscle activity, not changes over weeks. Flutter kicks hold the legs low and alternate them.

How many sets, and how to progress

  • Two sets of each for the first two weeks, three from week three, the same build as the full-body plans on this site.
  • Holds and carries end when your position slips, not when you collapse: your hips sag in the plank, the cable turns you in the Pallof press, you lean in the carry.
  • Crunches run 10 to 15 reps, stopping with a rep or two left, like any other set. (Here's how to judge reps left.)
  • Progress one thing at a time: a few more seconds or meters, a heavier dumbbell, then a harder version. Progressive overload works here exactly as it does on a squat.

Where it goes in your week

At the end of the session. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 position stand advises ordering a strength session with large muscle groups before small ones, multiple-joint exercises before single-joint ones, and higher-intensity exercises before lower-intensity ones.15 A core block is lower-intensity work, so it goes after your squats, hinges, presses and rows, where the arm workout goes too.

Two or three times a week, on days you already lift:

  • A full-body plan with one core slot, like the first four weeks, the bodyweight workout or the college routine: keep the one slot and rotate the four exercises through it.
  • The 30-minute workout pairs its core move with the hinge. Any of the four fits.
  • Upper/lower or push/pull/legs: the whole block at the end of two sessions. Which two is your workout split's call.

What to skip

  • Trading lifts for ball work. In the studies above, heavy lifts worked the trunk as hard as or harder than ball and balance exercises did.1,2
  • Five-minute planks and endless crunches. When a version gets easy, move to a harder one, such as the ab wheel rollout.
  • Sharp or lasting pain. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.

Common questions

Is a core workout the same as an abs workout?

An abs workout is part of it. The abs are the front of the core, and this block trains them in two of its four slots, the plank and the crunch, with the leg raises as the hardest step. In the studies above, the plank worked the front abs harder than either carry,9 and the hanging straight-leg raise was the biggest challenge to the abdominal wall of the three exercises compared.12 The other two slots train the sides and the refusal to twist, which an abs workout of crunches alone leaves out.
