Group fitness classes vs. personal training: which one gets you fit?

A class gives you a set time, a room and one workout for everyone. One-to-one coaching gives you a plan written for you and someone reading your numbers. Here's when each fits, and what the research can and can't say.

Short answer: A group fitness class gives you a set time, an instructor at the front and other people in the room, with one workout for everyone. One-to-one coaching gives you a plan written for you and a person who reads your numbers and changes it. Pick a class if showing up is the hard part and you like training with others. Pick a coach if the plan has to be written for you, or you stop when nobody is watching your week.

A group fitness class gives you a set time, an instructor at the front and other people in the room, with one workout for everyone. One-to-one coaching gives you a plan written for you and a person who reads your numbers and changes the plan with you. The first is strongest on the day you'd rather not go. The second is strongest on the weeks the plan has to change.

I'm an online coach, so I have a stake in this. I'll try to be fair, including about when a class is the better call, and about how little the research can settle.

What a group class gives you, and where it stops

A class gives you three things a solo workout doesn't: a time you committed to, someone leading the session, and other people doing it with you. That last part is real. In a 12-week study of 69 first- and second-year medical students at one US school, the 25 who took a 30-minute group fitness class each week reported lower perceived stress and higher physical, mental and emotional quality of life than at the start. The 29 who exercised regularly on their own, or with one or two partners, improved only on mental quality of life, and the 15 who didn't exercise regularly didn't change. The students weren't randomly assigned to a group, so it isn't a randomized trial.1

Pull the whole literature together and the group advantage gets smaller. A 2026 meta-analysis of 71 studies compared individual and group-based physical activity programs. Group-based programs showed a small advantage for physical activity (22,042 people), psychosocial outcomes and health outcomes, but none of those differences was statistically significant. For functional outcomes such as strength and flexibility, a significant but small advantage for groups appeared after the authors removed outliers (14,429 people). Their conclusion: both individual and group-based approaches, in a room or online, may be effective.2 Those were structured programs in research settings, not drop-in classes at a gym, so read it as "groups help some people" rather than "groups win".

Where it stops: the workout is written for the room, not for you. If your goal is to get stronger on the main lifts, a class that changes every day isn't built to progress one lift the way a plan is. And attention is divided. In an 11-week trial, 124 untrained young men did the same program with one coach for every five lifters or one for every twenty-five. The closely supervised group gained more strength on the bench press and the knee extension, and more of them trained at full effort, with no difference in attendance.3 That wasn't a group fitness class, but it is the clearest evidence I know of that how many people share the coach changes what you get out of a session.

What one-to-one coaching gives you, and where it stops

With me, your workouts live in the Trainerize app. You weigh in twice a week and send progress photos every two weeks. We talk on a weekly call scheduled around your time zone, and you can message me with questions between calls. On the call we look at the week you actually had and change the plan for the one that's coming.

So the difference isn't the workout. A class has a workout too. The difference is that the plan is written for you and someone reads your numbers. There's a reason that might matter: a meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies (19,951 people) found that getting people to monitor their progress toward a goal helped them reach it, and the effect was larger when progress was reported or made public, and when it was physically recorded.4 Those studies aren't about coaching. A weekly check-in is a version of both.

On follow-through, one trial puts a coach beside you next to training alone. In a 2025 randomized trial, 112 trained adults started the same 10-week, three-days-a-week strength program with a coach beside them, guided by an app, or from a PDF. Of those who started, 88% of the group with a coach beside them, 81% of the app group and 52% of the PDF group completed at least 26 of the 30 sessions. Every group that finished got stronger, and the coached group gained more on the squat than the other two.5 There was no group class in that trial and no remote coach with a weekly call, so it can't rank a class against coaching like mine.

Where it stops: I'm not in the room. I can't correct you mid-set, and there's no crowd to carry you through a session you didn't feel like doing. It costs you more time each week than a class does: the weigh-ins, the photos and the call. It costs money, and I don't publish a price list. And if you already train consistently on your own and know how to adjust your plan, a coach may not be worth it for you yet. (Here's how I'd decide.)

What the research can't tell you

I couldn't find a study that compares a gym's group fitness class with an online coach as products, over months, with people following each one. The studies above each test one piece: a class against exercising alone in one school, group programs against individual programs in general, more supervision against less, a coach beside you against an app or a PDF. None of them can tell you which one will work for you.

So here's my read, and it's an opinion, not a finding: a class solves the showing-up problem for people who like a room. Coaching solves the what-do-I-do-this-week problem for people whose weeks don't hold still. If showing up is your problem and a class fixes it, take the class. If you show up and still don't progress, or the plan dies the first week the class times don't fit, that's the case for a coach.

How to choose: four questions

  1. Does a room get you moving? If a booked class with other people in it is what gets you out the door, that's worth a lot. Keep it.
  2. Does the plan have to be written for you? If your goal is a specific lift, a body change you want to track, or a schedule that moves, one workout for the room will keep running into your week. The World Health Organization recommends muscle-strengthening work for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week for adults;6 a class can cover that, but check whether yours does.
  3. Do you keep skipping the class you booked? If you book and don't go, the appointment isn't the missing piece. Someone reading your numbers is. (Why people keep starting over.)
  4. Is anything injured or medical? Doctor or physical therapist first, whichever you pick. I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor.

It isn't either/or. You can keep a class you love and run a plan around it. If you're weighing a trainer in the room rather than a class, that comparison is here. If the question is whether a membership on its own is enough, that's here.

Common questions

Are group fitness classes effective?

They can be. In one 12-week study of 69 medical students, the ones who took a weekly group class reported lower stress and higher quality of life, while the ones exercising on their own mostly didn't change. Across 71 studies, group programs had a small edge over individual ones that was significant only for functional outcomes like strength and flexibility, and only after the authors removed outliers. So a class works for a lot of people. Whether it progresses your training toward your goal depends on the class.

Is personal training better than group classes?

Not in general, and no study I found settles it. Closer supervision tends to mean more effort and more strength per session, and a plan written for you can progress a lift the way a changing class can't. A class gives you a time, a leader and a room. Which one is better depends on which of those you're missing.

What about small group training?

It sits between the two: a few people, a coach who knows each of them, usually one plan with scaling. If you want attention without the price of one-to-one time, it's worth asking a gym what the ratio is. In the supervision trial above, one coach for five lifters beat one for twenty-five.

Can I do a class and have a coach?

Yes. Tell any coach which classes you do and when, and the plan gets built around them. With me, the plan is built around your goals, your schedule and your lifestyle, so tell me about any class you already go to in the first conversation.

Is a class cheaper than a coach?

I can't say, and I won't guess. Classes are commonly sold as a membership, a pack or a drop-in; I don't publish a price list, and that conversation happens in the DMs. Whichever you pick, ask what you get each week and who looks at your numbers, then compare on that.
