Back workout: pick a vertical pull, a row and a hinge

Pull down from overhead, pull toward you, and hinge: one exercise for each, with the equipment you have, makes a back workout. Here's the menu.

Short answer: Pick one exercise from each of three slots: a vertical pull (a pull-up or lat pulldown), a horizontal pull (a row) and a hinge (a Romanian deadlift or back extension). The 2026 ACSM position stand's authors suggest considering vertical and horizontal directions for the upper body,1 so do both. Three hard sets of each makes a session.

"Back" is several muscles: the lats down the sides of your back, the trapezius and rhomboids between your shoulder blades, and the erector spinae along your spine. No single exercise works all of them hardest, so a back workout is built from slots rather than one favorite lift.

This page is a menu: three slots, options for the gym, machines and home, and the questions that come up when you choose. Technique lives on how to do a pull-up and deadlift form.

The three slots

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand didn't compare body regions, but its authors' view is that pushing and pulling exercises for the upper and lower body reach the major muscle groups, and that the upper body can also be split into horizontal and vertical directions.1 For a back workout, that means two kinds of pull, plus a hinge:

  1. A vertical pull: pulling down from overhead, like a pull-up or a lat pulldown.
  2. A horizontal pull: pulling toward your chest or stomach, like a row.
  3. A hinge: bending at the hips with your back straight, like a Romanian deadlift.

Why both pulls? They shift the work a little. In a 2004 study of 12 men with more than six months of lifting, who held each pull still for ten seconds, activity in the muscles between the shoulder blades was higher in the seated row than in the reverse-grip pulldown, and the wide-grip pulldown and the seated row worked the lats more relative to the biceps than the reverse-grip pulldown did.2 And the hinge: a 2020 review of 19 studies, in lifters aged 18 to 34 with at least six months of training, found the back muscles along the spine more active than the glutes and hamstrings in the deadlift.3

The vertical pull: three grip questions

Pull-ups if you can do them, the pulldown if you can't yet (how to do a pull-up covers getting from one to the other). On the pulldown, three questions come up.

Wide grip or close grip?

Grip width matters little here. In a study of 15 men, lat activity was similar with grips one, one and a half and two times shoulder width, and the narrow and medium grips allowed slightly heavier six-rep maxes than the wide one. The authors saw minor advantages for the medium grip.4 A 2026 review of 23 studies of lat activity likewise found the effects of grip width inconsistent.5 Pick a width between shoulder width and about twice that, wherever you feel strongest.

Palms away or palms toward you?

In a 2010 study of 12 men, a palms-away grip produced more lat activity than a palms-toward-you grip, at both a narrow and a wide width.6 The 2026 review points out that this finding remains unreplicated,5 and in the 2004 held-pull study of 12 men, palm direction made no difference to lat activity on the pulldown.2 Use either, or rotate them.

In front of your head or behind it?

In front, to the top of your chest. The review reports a study of 24 trained men in which pulling the bar down in front produced more lat activity during the pull than pulling it behind the neck, and calls the front version the most consistently supported setup.5

The horizontal pull: which row

Rows mostly differ in how much of you has to hold still while you pull.

  • Seated cable row. The 2026 review found two setups each linked with more lat activity, in separate studies: a narrower grip, and elbows kept lower and closer to your sides.5 Aim to pull your elbows back past your ribs.
  • Chest-supported row, on a machine or face down on an incline bench with dumbbells. The bench holds your torso, so the effort goes into the pull rather than into holding yourself bent over.
  • One-arm dumbbell row. One hand and one knee on a bench; the home row.
  • Barbell row. You hold a bent-over position for the whole set, so learn the hinge first.

Should you squeeze your shoulder blades together before each row? In the 2004 held-pull study of 12 men, deliberately pulling the shoulder blades back didn't change activity in the muscles between them.2 It's one small study, but no reason to force the squeeze.

The hinge: which one on a back day

The deadlift and the Romanian deadlift lean different ways. In the 2020 review, the back muscles were more active than the glutes and hamstrings in the deadlift, while the Romanian deadlift worked the biceps femoris, one of the hamstrings, harder than the back muscles.3 Either one counts as your hinge. Two things decide which:

  • Where your split puts it. If your leg day already has a heavy deadlift or Romanian deadlift, a back day can take a lighter hinge, like a back extension, or leave it out. Two heavy hinges on back-to-back days is a lot. (The leg day has its own hinge.)
  • What you can do well. Deadlift form covers the conventional, trap bar and Romanian versions step by step.

Feeling rows in your arms, not your back?

That's common, and a cue can shift some of it. In a study of eight women with little or no lifting background, verbal instruction on using the lats raised lat activity during the lat pulldown, while biceps activity didn't change. The authors' reading: you can turn one muscle up, but that isn't the same as switching the others off.7 (The mind-muscle connection covers when that kind of focus helps.) These are common cues, not the study's own words:

  • Think of pulling with your elbows, with your hands as hooks.
  • On the pulldown, drive your elbows down toward your back pockets.
  • On rows, pull your elbows back past your ribs, then let them travel forward under control.

Some arm work is normal, and the arm workout counts it toward your biceps. And pullovers, often sold as a lat exercise, appeared to work the chest more than the lats in the 2026 review.5 The same review suggests the straight-arm pulldown may target the back muscles more, depending on the grip.5

Three back workouts from the menu

Three sets of each pick, 8 to 12 reps on the pulls, and 6 to 10 on a heavy hinge or 8 to 12 on a lighter one. End each set with a couple of reps still in you. (Here's how to judge reps left.)

  • Gym: pull-up or assisted pull-up, chest-supported row, Romanian deadlift.
  • Machines and cables: lat pulldown, seated cable row, 45-degree back extension.
  • Home: doorway pull-ups or slow negatives, one-arm dumbbell row, dumbbell Romanian deadlift.

Lead with the pull you most want to get stronger on: the ACSM's 2026 stand found strength was enhanced for exercises done at the beginning of a session. It also found muscle growth was enhanced by ten or more sets per muscle group a week, and that how many days those sets were spread over didn't change it when volume was matched.1 Each session above has six sets of pulling, so two a week, on an upper/lower or push/pull/legs split, gives your back twelve. Which days those are is your split's job, and adding to each pick over the weeks is progressive overload.

What the research can and can't say

Nearly everything above comes from measuring muscle activity during a set, not growth over months. The 2026 review is plain about it: none of the 23 studies it included was a training study, and none measured growth or strength.5 The single studies named on this page are small, 8 to 24 people each, and all but one were in men. So read the grip and row choices as starting points. The logbook settles the rest: the exercise you keep adding weight or reps to is doing its job.

Common mistakes

  • Only pulldowns, or only rows. One of each.
  • Swinging the weight. Leaning far back on a pulldown or heaving a row hands the work to momentum. Go lighter.
  • Cutting the range short. Arms straight at the start of each rep, elbows all the way back at the end.
  • Pulling behind the neck. One study found more lat activity pulling to the front, so pull to the front of your chest.
  • Pulling through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and see a doctor or a physical therapist.
