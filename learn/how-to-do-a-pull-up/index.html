How to do a pull-up: from zero to one, then more

A pull-up is your whole body weight on two hands. Can't do one yet? Lower yourself slowly, take some help, and build the pull on a machine. Here's how, with the studies.

Short answer: Hang with straight arms, pull your shoulder blades down, and pull until your chin clears the bar, then lower all the way. If you can't do one yet, train the parts: slow negatives, band- or machine-assisted pull-ups and lat pulldowns.9,8 As each gets easier, take a little less help.

A pull-up lifts your whole body weight with your back and arms. That's what makes the first one hard, and why getting there is usually a matter of building up to it rather than trying harder on the bar.

This page covers the pull-up and the chin-up, how to do one well, four ways to train toward your first rep, a simple routine, and how to add reps once you have one. If you're new to lifting in general, the first four weeks come first.

Pull-up vs chin-up

A pull-up is done with your palms facing away from you, a chin-up with your palms facing you. They train the same main muscles:

  • The lats do the most work in both. In a study of 21 men and 4 women, the latissimus dorsi, the big muscles down the sides of the back, were the most active muscles measured, followed by the biceps. The chin-up used more chest and biceps; the pull-up used more of the lower trapezius, the lower part of the muscle between and below the shoulder blades.1
  • Grip changes the details, not the picture. In 19 strength-trained men, pull-ups with the palms facing away, facing you, facing each other or holding a rope produced similar muscle activity over a full rep. One difference: the palms-away grip worked the middle trapezius harder than the palms-facing-each-other grip.2

Chin-up or pull-up?

Pick by what you want a little more of. The lats lead either way: in that 2010 study of 25 people, their activity averaged 117 to 130% of a maximal test contraction across the three bar exercises tested, against 78 to 96% for the biceps, and the biceps and chest ran significantly higher on the chin-up.1 So the chin-up is the version with more arm in it, and the arm workout's count credits either one toward your biceps.

How wide to grip changes less than you might expect. In 14 trained men, pull-ups with a shoulder-width grip and with their own chosen grip, about 20% wider, gave similar one-rep maxes, reps to failure and power.3 A 2026 review of 23 studies found broadly similar lat activity across pull-up grips.4 Hands a little wider than your shoulders, as in the steps below, is a fine default.

So use whichever grip feels strongest while you build toward your first rep, and add the other later. Everything below works for both.

How to do a pull-up

  1. Grip the bar with your hands a little wider than your shoulders, thumbs around the bar.
  2. Start from a dead hang. Arms straight, feet off the floor, legs still.
  3. Pull your shoulder blades down first. It's a small movement before your elbows bend. The 2010 study of 25 people found a pattern suggesting that pull-ups and chin-ups start with the lower trapezius and the chest muscles and finish with the biceps and lats.1
  4. Drive your elbows down toward your ribs until your chin clears the bar.
  5. Lower yourself all the way to straight arms, under control. That's one rep.

No swinging or kicking. A kipping pull-up is a different skill; when the goal is your first strict rep, it skips the part you're trying to build.

Can't do one yet? Four ways in

Every route to a first pull-up does the same thing: it lets you practice the pull with less than your full body weight, or practice the part you can already do.

1. Negatives

Step up on a box or jump so your chin is over the bar, then lower yourself as slowly as you can, three to five seconds, to straight arms. Step back up and repeat.

Why it works: the lowering half is the easier half. In the 2017 study of 19 strength-trained men, the muscles of the arm and chest were more active in the pulling phase of every grip than in the lowering phase.2 A 2009 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials found that eccentric, or lowering, training done at higher intensities raised strength more than concentric, or lifting, training, but that the gains were more specific to the kind of movement trained, and it called for more research on how they carry over to whole movements.5 The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand found muscle growth was enhanced by eccentric overload, meaning more load or more time on the lowering phase.6 A negative is one way to do both: lower with extra time under load. The catch is that negatives build the lowering more than the pull, so pair them with one of the options below.

Expect some soreness at first. A 2003 review of the research on muscle soreness advises bringing eccentric, lowering-type exercises in gradually, over a week or two.7 (What next-day soreness is, and whether to train through it.)

2. Band-assisted pull-ups

Loop a resistance band over the bar and put a knee or a foot in it. The band takes part of your weight, so you can do full reps. It helps most at the bottom, where it's stretched the furthest, and least at the top. Move to a thinner band when the reps get easy.

3. The assisted pull-up machine

You kneel or stand on a pad, and a counterweight lifts part of your body weight. The more weight on the stack, the more help. It's the easiest option to measure: take a little weight off the stack as the reps get easier, and write the number down.

4. The lat pulldown

The pulldown trains the same pulling motion seated, with a weight you choose. In an eight-week trial, 34 recreationally active male college students did lat pulldowns three times a week, four sets of 12, either on a normal machine or with an unstable setup. Pull-up reps rose in both groups, and no group skipped the pulldowns. Everyone in the 2025 trial could already do at least one pull-up at the start, so it says nothing about getting a first rep.8

What one trial did, and a routine to start from

In an eight-week trial, 30 physically inactive young men trained twice a week with assisted pull-ups, inverted rows, chin-ups and negatives, three hard sets of each, lowering from the top of the negatives over several seconds. On average they could already do about three or four pull-ups. All three groups added reps, and the group that also trained its grip and forearms improved the most; the authors describe grip fatigue as a common limiting factor in the pull-up.9 It's one small trial, in men, but it's a useful template.

A starting point, two or three times a week, early in an upper-body or pull session. The three-set structure comes from the assisted-training trial above; the rep ranges and the dead hangs are a suggestion, not something a trial tested:

ExerciseSets and repsHow to progress
Negatives3 sets of 3 to 5, three to five seconds downSlower, then more reps
Band or machine-assisted pull-ups3 sets of 5 to 8Less help
Lat pulldown or inverted row3 sets of 8 to 12More weight, or a lower angle on the row
Dead hang2 or 3 holds, as long as you canLonger holds

Make each set hard, with only a few reps left in you (here's how to judge that), and add help-free reps or weight the way progressive overload describes. Every week or two, try one strict pull-up at the start of a session, fresh. How long the first rep takes varies a lot with where you start; most of these studies were in young men, so they say little about how fast anyone else will get there.

From one pull-up to more

Once you have one, the work changes from getting help to adding reps:

  • Do several sets of one or two with plenty of rest, instead of one all-out set. More good reps in a session is the goal.
  • Keep the pulldowns and the negatives. They let you train the pull harder than a set of one or two can. In the 2025 trial of 34 male college students, pull-up reps rose in both groups, all of whom could already do one.8
  • Add a rep before anything else. When you can do several sets of several reps, weighted pull-ups or a harder grip are the next step.

Pull-ups fit any split as the vertical pull: on a full-body or upper day, put them early, while you're fresh, and pair them with a press such as the bench press.

Common mistakes

  • Half reps. Straight arms at the bottom and chin over the bar at the top. A rep that stops short at either end isn't the rep you're building.
  • Swinging to get there. Momentum gets the chin over the bar once and teaches you nothing about the next one.
  • Only negatives, every day. They train the lowering more than the pull, and they need bringing in gradually. Mix them with assisted reps or pulldowns.
  • Ignoring your grip. If your hands give out before your back does, add dead hangs or farmer carries.
  • Testing every session. Trying for a max every time leaves less for the training that gets you there.
  • Pulling through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. Stop, and talk to a doctor or a physical therapist.

Common questions

Can you train for a pull-up at home?

Yes, if you have a bar to hang from, such as a doorway pull-up bar. Negatives need nothing else, a band stands in for the assisted machine, and an inverted row under a low, sturdy bar is the swap the routine above lists for the pulldown in the routine above. In 13 men and 13 women doing inverted rows on a commercially available portable pull-up device, the lats, biceps, lower trapezius and rear shoulder all reached very high activity, above 61% of a maximal contraction.10 That is muscle activity in one session, not a training trial, so it says nothing about how fast a first pull-up comes.
