Which workout split? Count your days first

Full body, upper/lower and push/pull/legs all work; pick the one that fits your days. Here's how they compare and how to switch without starting over.

Short answer: Count the days you can train, then pick the split that fits them: full body for two or three days, upper/lower for four, push/pull/legs for five or six. When weekly volume is matched, studies find split and full-body routines build similar strength and muscle,1 so choose the one you'll repeat and count your sets.

Ask which workout split to run and you'll get an argument: full body, upper/lower, push/pull/legs, a body part a day. Most of that argument skips the question that actually decides it. How many days a week can you train, every week, for months?

Start there, because the split is only how those days get divided. The research is on your side: when the weekly amount of training is matched, split and full-body routines build similar strength and muscle.1 This page puts the three common splits side by side, says which fits two to six days, and walks through what the studies say about training each muscle once or twice a week. Never lifted at all? The first four weeks comes first; you don't need to choose a split to start.

What a workout split is

A split is the plan for which muscles or movements each session trains across your week. Three layouts are the common ones:

  • Full body: every session trains the whole body, usually a squat, a hinge, a push and a pull, plus an extra or two.
  • Upper/lower: sessions alternate between upper body (chest, back, shoulders, arms) and lower body (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves).
  • Push/pull/legs: three kinds of session. Push is chest, shoulders and triceps; pull is back and biceps; legs is the lower body. (A back workout and a leg day, written out.)

You'll also see the body-part split: chest one day, back the next, and so on. It trains each muscle once a week and usually runs five days or more, which is why this page sticks to the three above.

What a week looks like on each:

  • Full body, three days: Monday, Wednesday and Friday, alternating two sessions (A, B, A, then B, A, B). The three-day dumbbell plan and the college workout routine are both written out this way; the full-body workout plan is the gym version with three sessions.
  • Upper/lower, four days: upper Monday, lower Tuesday, a day off, upper Thursday, lower Friday. (The four-day plan, written out.)
  • Push/pull/legs, six days: push, pull, legs, push, pull, legs, one day off. (The routine, written out.)

The trade is between days and focus. Full body asks for the fewest days, and a missed session costs the least, because the next one trains everything again. Upper/lower gives each half of the body a whole session, so there's room for more exercises per area. Push/pull/legs gives each session the narrowest job and needs the most days. Run it on three days and every muscle is trained once a week, with all of its sets in that one session.

Full body or upper/lower?

Count your days. Full body fits two or three, upper/lower fits four, and on those days both train each muscle at least twice a week. With weekly volume matched, the head-to-head studies found that split and full-body routines built similar strength and muscle,1 so pick the one your week will hold. Full body costs the least when you miss a day; upper/lower leaves room for more exercises per area. (The workout plan quiz turns your days and equipment into one of the written-out plans.)

Push/pull/legs or upper/lower?

Mostly a question of days: upper/lower fits four, push/pull/legs fits six, and run on those days both train each muscle twice a week. None of the studies on this page tests the two against each other directly. What they do show is that the layout matters much less than the sets you put in it.

Push day and pull day

A push day is the presses: chest, shoulders and triceps. A pull day is the pull-ups and rows: back and biceps. A typical version of each, with the page that covers each part:

  • Push day: a bench press or push-ups, an overhead press, an Arnold press or an incline press, then lateral raises and a triceps extension. The chest workout and the shoulder workout count the sets.
  • Pull day: a pull-up or a pulldown, a barbell row or a dumbbell row, then face pulls and a curl. The back workout lays out the menu.

The arm work at the end of each day can stay short, because the presses already train the triceps and the pulls the biceps. The 2026 meta-regression below found that counting each of those indirect sets as half a set had the strongest support.2 The arm workout does that count for you.

Which split for your number of days

Two days: full body

Full body both days, with at least a day between them. An upper/lower split fits two days too, but then each muscle is trained once a week.

Three days: full body, or a rotation

Full body is the default: three sessions, each muscle three times a week, a day off between sessions. The other option is a rotation: upper, lower, upper one week and lower, upper, lower the next, so each half of the body gets three sessions every two weeks. If you came to me with three days a week, full body is where I'd usually start (the three-day gym plan, written out). Push/pull/legs fits three days on paper, but it trains each muscle once a week.

Four days: upper/lower

Upper/lower, twice each. Each muscle is trained twice a week and no session has to cover everything. Put the days off where your week has gaps; two training days in a row is normal on this split, because they train different halves. (The upper/lower split workout plan is the four days written out; strength training for women has a worked four-day week.)

Five or six days: push/pull/legs

Six days fits push/pull/legs exactly, each muscle twice a week. Five doesn't divide neatly: run push/pull/legs as a rolling rotation that carries on whatever the day of the week, or do upper/lower twice plus one full-body day. Either way, five or six days is a choice, not a requirement. The push/pull/legs routine is the six sessions written out, with the rolling version; how often to train covers picking the number itself.

Once or twice a week: what the research says

The common rule is to train each muscle at least twice a week. A 2016 meta-analysis of ten studies backs it: training a muscle more often was linked to more growth. Looking at studies that compared one to three days a week with the amount of training matched, the authors concluded that twice a week beat once, inferred that the major muscle groups should be trained at least twice a week to maximize growth, and left open whether three times beats two.3

A bigger update in 2019 changed the picture. With 25 studies, the same lead author and colleagues found no significant difference between training a muscle more or less often once the weekly volume was equated, and concluded that people can choose how often to train each muscle "based on personal preference". Where volume wasn't matched, more frequent training came out ahead, but the difference between once a week and three or more times was modest.4

Splits have been compared head to head as well. The 2024 meta-analysis mentioned above pooled 14 studies with 392 participants and found similar gains in bench press strength, lower-body strength and muscle size from split and full-body routines when volume was equated. Its conclusion: people can choose a routine "based on their personal preferences".1

What does move the needle is weekly volume: how many sets each muscle gets in a week. A 2017 meta-analysis of 15 studies found a graded dose-response, with more weekly sets bringing more muscle growth.5 A meta-regression of 67 studies published in 2026 found that muscle size and strength both rise with weekly sets, with diminishing returns. In the same 2026 analysis of 67 studies and 2,058 participants, the effect of frequency on muscle size was compatible with being negligible, while strength did rise with frequency, again with diminishing returns.2

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand, a review of 137 systematic reviews, lands in the same place. Training frequency, from one day a week to more than five, did not change muscle growth when total volume was equated, while ten or more sets per muscle group a week enhanced it. For strength, two or more sessions a week helped. Its primary recommendation is lifting with high effort at least twice a week, with all major muscle groups trained.6

So for muscle, the layout matters far less than the weekly sets. For strength, training a muscle on more days helps, with diminishing returns. Twice a week per muscle is still a sensible default: it matches the ACSM's at-least-twice-a-week recommendation, and spreading a muscle's sets over two sessions leaves fewer of them in any one session. The deciding question is still the one this page started with: which layout lets you do your sets every week?

How to switch splits without losing progress

Because the matched-volume studies find so little difference between layouts, what you protect when you switch is your weekly sets and your working weights, not the old schedule.

  1. Count your weekly sets per muscle before you switch, and land the new split in the same range. Three full-body days with three sets of a squat is nine sets a week; two lower days with a squat and a lunge at two sets each is eight. Close enough.
  2. Keep the lifts you're progressing on, at the weights in your logbook. A new split is not a reason to change every exercise.
  3. Start any new exercise a little light and add from there. New exercises can mean new soreness: soreness is common when a kind of activity is new to you, and one review of the research advises easing into new exercise over a week or two.7
  4. Give it eight weeks before you judge it, and keep adding weight or reps the way you did before. (Progressive overload, explained.)

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a split for the week you wish you had. A six-day plan run on three days trains each muscle once a week. Pick for the days you can repeat, and add a day later if it keeps turning up free.
  • Skipping the missed day. On a split, a missed session is a missed muscle. Do the session you missed next, not the one the calendar says. Traveling? Run full body on the road.
  • Counting sessions instead of sets. Two splits with the same hard sets per muscle are close to equal; a split that quietly halves them isn't. A hard set ends with only a few reps left in you. (Here's how to judge that.)
  • Upper days that are all pushing. Every upper session needs a pull as well as a press, and a lower day needs the hamstrings and glutes as much as the quads. (How to do a pull-up and bench press form.)
  • Switching splits every few weeks. The logbook is how you know a plan is working, and it only works if the plan holds still.
  • Training through pain. Sharp pain is not soreness. If you have an injury, a medical condition or a medical question, talk to a doctor before you change how you train. I'm a fitness coach, not a doctor.
